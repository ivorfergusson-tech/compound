## Recap

In module 1 you saw that cash in a savings account earns [[interest]], but [[inflation]] eats into it, so what matters is the [[real return]]. Savings are safe, but over long periods they often only just keep up with prices. This module meets the three main things people invest in instead, hoping to grow their money faster: **shares**, **bonds** and **funds**.

## Saving vs investing

**Saving** means keeping money somewhere safe, like a bank account, where the amount you put in can't fall. **Investing** means buying an [[asset]] that you hope will grow in value or pay you an income, accepting that it **can fall**.

Why take that risk? Because over long periods, investments have usually grown faster than prices. In the US, where the records go back furthest, shares beat inflation by {{fig:usLongRun+}}. Other countries' records differ, and the past doesn't promise the future. But the pattern, more risk for more expected return, shows up almost everywhere.

The catch is the word **long**. Over a few months or years, investments can fall a lot. That's why the usual rule is: don't invest money you'll need within about five years.

## Shares: owning a slice

A **[[share]]** (also called a stock or equity) is a small piece of ownership in a company. If a company is split into 1 billion shares and you own 1,000, you own one-millionth of it.

As an owner, you can make money in two ways:

1. **The share price rises.** If the company grows and makes more profit, other people may pay more for its shares. You make a **[[capital gain]]** if you sell for more than you paid.
2. **[[dividend|Dividends]].** Many companies pay part of their profits to shareholders, usually twice a year or quarterly. Dividends aren't guaranteed: companies can cut or stop them, especially when times are hard.

You also usually get a vote at the company's annual meeting.

The risk: share prices go up **and down**, sometimes sharply, and a company can go bust. If it does, shareholders are paid last, after lenders and suppliers, and usually get nothing. The most you can lose on a share is what you paid for it (unlike some trading products in module 7, where you can lose more).

## Bonds: lending your money

A **[[bond]]** is a loan. You lend money to a government or company for a set time. In return they promise to:

- pay you a fixed amount of interest each year, called the **[[coupon]]**, and
- pay back the original amount (the **face value**, often £100 per bond) on a set date, called **maturity**.

UK government bonds are called **gilts**. Bonds from companies are **corporate bonds**.

Bonds are usually less risky than shares, because lenders get paid before owners, and the payments are agreed in advance. But they're not risk-free:

- **Default risk:** the borrower might not pay. A UK gilt is seen as very safe; a bond from a struggling company isn't.
- **Interest rate risk:** if you sell a bond before it matures, its price depends on interest rates at the time. When rates rise, existing bonds with lower coupons become less attractive, so their prices fall. Module 24 explains why.
- **Inflation risk:** fixed payments buy less if prices rise fast.

Because bonds are less risky, they've usually paid less than shares over the long run. In the US, government bonds beat inflation by about 1.6% a year from 1900 to 2025, against about 6.6% for shares.

## The yield: what you earn on what you pay

A share's **dividend yield** is its yearly dividend divided by its price. A share at £4 paying 20p a year has a yield of 5%.

A bond's **current yield** is its coupon divided by its price. A bond paying £4 a year bought for £100 yields 4%. But if you buy it for £80, the same £4 is a 5% yield on what you paid. Prices and yields move in opposite directions.

## Funds: buying many at once

Picking individual shares or bonds is hard: you need money, time and knowledge, and you can be badly hit if one company fails. A **[[fund]]** solves this by pooling money from many investors and buying lots of different investments at once.

With one purchase you might own small slices of hundreds or thousands of companies, or a mix of shares and bonds. That spreading out is called **diversification**, and it's the biggest idea in module 10.

The main kinds of fund you'll see in the UK:

- **Unit trusts and OEICs:** you buy units from the fund manager, priced once a day.
- **Investment trusts:** companies whose business is investing; you buy their shares on the stock market.
- **ETFs** (exchange-traded funds): funds that trade on a stock exchange like shares, with prices all day.

Funds can be **active** (a manager picks what to buy, trying to beat the market) or **passive** (an **index fund** that simply copies a market, such as the 100 biggest UK companies). Module 14 compares them. Spoiler: most active funds fail to beat cheap index funds over the long run, mainly because of fees.

Funds charge a yearly fee, called the **ongoing charge**, taken out of the fund automatically.

## How these compare

| | Shares | Bonds | Funds |
|---|---|---|---|
| What you are | Part-owner | Lender | Pooled owner of many |
| How you earn | Price rises and dividends | Coupons, and face value at the end | Whatever the fund holds |
| Usual risk | Higher | Lower (depends on the borrower) | Depends what's inside |
| If things go wrong | Can fall to zero | Borrower may not pay | Spread across many, so one failure hurts less |

Back to the question at the start. Over 20 years, a slice of hundreds of big companies has **usually** ended up well ahead of savings, often by a lot. But "usually" isn't "always": there have been 20-year stretches in some countries where shares barely beat cash, and in any single year shares can fall by a third or more. Module 10 shows how to handle that risk.

## Tax on investments, briefly

Outside an ISA or pension, investment returns can be taxed:

- Dividends above the {{fig:dividendAllowance+}}.
- Gains when you sell above the {{fig:cgtAllowance+}}.

Inside a Stocks and Shares ISA or a pension, there's no tax on either. That's why most UK investors start with those wrappers (modules 17 and 21).

## The common mistake

**"Investing is just gambling with extra steps."**

It's an easy assumption when you see prices jumping around. But owning a slice of real companies that make products and profits is different from a bet, where the house wins on average and the money just changes hands. Long-term investors in a broad spread of companies have historically shared in the growth of the economy. Short-term guessing at price moves is closer to gambling, and module 3 draws that line clearly.

## In real life

- **Investing is for money you won't need for at least five years.** Emergency funds and short-term goals belong in cash.
- **Spread it out.** A fund holding hundreds of companies is far less risky than a handful of shares you like.
- **Use the wrappers.** A Stocks and Shares ISA (module 17) means no tax on gains or dividends.
- **Red flag:** anyone offering a bond or investment paying a "guaranteed" high fixed return. If it really were guaranteed and high, banks would be offering it. Check firms on the FCA register.

## Think about it

:::reveal Why do shareholders expect a higher return than bondholders in the same company?
Because they take more risk. Bondholders get fixed payments first; shareholders only get what's left, and nothing if the company fails. Nobody would take the extra risk without the chance of extra reward, so on average shares must offer more.
:::

:::reveal If interest rates rise, why might the price of an old bond paying 3% fall?
New bonds now pay more, say 5%. Nobody will pay full price for an old bond paying 3% when they could get 5% elsewhere, so the old bond's price drops until its yield is competitive.
:::
