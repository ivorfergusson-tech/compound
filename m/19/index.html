Recap

Module 15 taught you to read candlestick charts, trends, support and resistance, and warned how easily charts fool us. Many traders add indicators to their charts: lines and numbers calculated from past prices and volume. This module explains the most common ones, what they really measure, and why every one of them looks backwards.

Before you read

An indicator flashes "overbought" on a share that has risen strongly for weeks.

The idea in one sentence

Indicators are just maths applied to past prices and volume, so they summarise what has happened rather than predict what will; they can help you stay consistent and spot conditions, but they lag, and they don't create an edge on their own.

Moving averages

A moving average (MA) is the average closing price over the last N periods, recalculated each day. A 10-day MA averages the last 10 closes; tomorrow it drops the oldest and adds the newest.

What it does:

  • Smooths the noise, so the trend is easier to see.
  • Shows direction: price above a rising MA suggests an uptrend; below a falling one, a downtrend.
  • Longer = smoother but slower. A 50- or 200-day MA shows the big picture but turns late; a 10-day MA reacts faster but gives more false signals.

Traders use moving averages to filter trades ("only buy when price is above the 50-day MA") or as signals ("buy when a short MA crosses above a long one"). Move the slider below: notice that the line always trails the price, because it's built from the past.

Volume

Volume is how many shares or contracts traded in a period. It shows how much conviction is behind a move:

  • A breakout on high volume suggests lots of participants agree, so it's more believable.
  • A breakout on low volume is easier to reverse: few people took part.
  • Volume spikes often come with news, results or panic.

RSI

The RSI (Relative Strength Index) measures how strong recent gains have been compared with recent losses, usually over 14 periods, on a scale of 0 to 100:

  • Above 70 is often labelled overbought: prices have risen a lot, quickly.
  • Below 30 is often labelled oversold: they've fallen a lot, quickly.

Back to the question at the start. "Overbought" doesn't mean "about to fall". In a strong uptrend, RSI can stay above 70 for weeks while the price keeps rising. Look at the made-up chart: RSI sat above 70 through most of the rise. Selling every time RSI hit 70 would have missed most of it. RSI is more useful for spotting when a move is stretched, or for confirming other signals, than as a buy or sell button.

MACD

MACD (Moving Average Convergence Divergence) compares a fast and a slow moving average, usually 12 and 26 periods, giving one line. A further 9-period average of that line is the "signal line".

  • When the MACD line crosses above its signal line, momentum is turning up; below, turning down.
  • It's really just a smoothed version of "is the short-term trend faster than the longer-term one?"

Like the others, it's built from moving averages of past prices, so its signals come after the move has started.

Why every indicator lags

All of these are lagging indicators: calculated only from past prices and volume. They can't know about tomorrow's news. That has consequences:

  • Signals come late. By the time a moving average turns up, part of the move has happened.
  • Whipsaws in ranges. When prices go sideways, trend indicators flip back and forth, giving a string of small losing signals.
  • They don't add new information. Everything an indicator shows is already in the price chart, just rearranged.

So why use them? Because they help with consistency: a clear rule ("buy when price closes above the 20-day MA and RSI is under 70") is easier to follow and to test (module 27) than a gut feeling. The rule itself still has to be tested to see whether it has an edge.

Indicator overload

A common beginner move is piling five or six indicators on one chart, waiting for them all to agree. Problems:

  • They're mostly built from the same prices, so they tend to agree or disagree together: it's the same information several times.
  • More conditions means fewer trades and more room to fool yourself after the fact.
  • The chart becomes so busy you stop seeing the price.

Most experienced traders use one or two indicators, if any, alongside price, levels and strict risk management.

The common mistake

"I just need to find the right indicator settings."

Endlessly tweaking settings until an indicator would have caught every past move is called overfitting (module 27). It produces settings that look perfect on old data and fail on new data. No setting turns a lagging indicator into a crystal ball.

In real life

  • Start with price, trend and levels. Add at most one or two indicators.
  • Use indicators as filters or confirmation, not as automatic buy and sell buttons.
  • Check volume on breakouts.
  • Write the exact rule in your journal so you can test it later.
  • Red flag: paid indicators or "signals" claiming high win rates. Ask for a full record of every signal, including losses, over years.

Think about it

If indicators only rearrange past prices, could they ever be useful?

Yes, as tools for consistency and for describing conditions (trending vs ranging, stretched vs calm). A rule based on them can be tested and followed without emotion. Whether that rule makes money after costs is a separate question that only testing can answer.

Why might a moving-average crossover strategy do well in some years and badly in others?

It makes money in long, strong trends, catching big moves late but riding them. In sideways markets it whipsaws: many small losses as the lines cross back and forth. Results depend heavily on what kind of market you're in.

Try it

  1. In the calculator, set the moving average to 3 and then 30. Which follows the price more closely? Which would give fewer false signals in the sideways stretch?
  2. On a real chart of your market, add a 50-day moving average and RSI. Write down one rule using them, then mark the last five times it would have triggered. How many worked?
