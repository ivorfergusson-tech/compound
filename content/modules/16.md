## The two big jobs

Corporate finance at an investment bank splits into two halves:

1. **[[advisory|Advisory]]:** advising companies on **M&A** (mergers and acquisitions): buying, selling, merging or defending against an unwanted bid. Banks sell their expertise and judgement.
2. **Capital markets:** helping companies **raise money** from investors:
   - **[[ecm|ECM]]** (equity capital markets): issuing shares, such as IPOs (module 6), or selling new shares to existing investors.
   - **[[dcm|DCM]]** (debt capital markets): issuing bonds (module 24) or arranging big loans.

Alongside these, investment banks have **sales and trading** and **research** (module 4), which deal with investors rather than companies.

## M&A: why companies buy companies

**[[m&a|M&A]]** covers one company buying another (an acquisition) or two joining together (a merger). Common reasons:

- **Growth:** buying customers, products or a new country faster than building them.
- **Synergies:** cost savings (one head office instead of two) or extra sales from combining.
- **Capabilities:** technology, brands or skilled teams.
- **Defence:** buying a rival before a competitor does.

Back to the question at the start. To buy a listed company, the buyer usually has to pay a **premium** over the current share price, often tens of percent, to persuade enough shareholders to sell, and because control lets the buyer change how the business runs. The buyer believes the synergies and control are worth more than the premium. Whether that turns out true is another matter: many studies have found that a large share of acquisitions fail to create value for the buyer's shareholders, often because they overpaid or the synergies didn't arrive.

## How a deal happens

A typical sale of a company, run by its advisers ("sell-side"):

1. **Preparation:** the advisers analyse the business, build valuation models and write an **information memorandum** describing it for buyers.
2. **Marketing:** contacting likely buyers, under confidentiality agreements.
3. **Bids:** first indicative offers, then a shortlist gets deeper access (due diligence: lawyers, accountants and experts checking everything).
4. **Negotiation:** price, terms and legal documents.
5. **Signing and completion:** with shareholder and regulator approval where needed. UK takeovers of listed companies follow the **Takeover Code**, overseen by the Takeover Panel.

Advisers on the buying side ("buy-side") help find targets, value them, structure the offer and negotiate.

## Valuation in deals

Advisers value companies three main ways, using the tools from module 22:

- **Trading comparables ("comps"):** what similar listed companies are worth, as multiples like EV/EBITDA or P/E.
- **Precedent transactions:** what buyers paid in similar past deals, including premiums.
- **DCF:** the present value of future cash flows.

They show the results as ranges on a chart (often called a "football field") to frame negotiations.

## Pitch books and fees

To win work, bankers present a **[[pitch book]]**: slides showing the bank's ideas for the client, market analysis, possible buyers or targets, valuation ranges and the bank's track record.

Advisory fees are usually mostly a **success fee**: a percentage of the deal value, paid only if the deal completes, often with a smaller retainer. The percentage tends to be lower for bigger deals. Because most of the fee depends on completion, critics point out that advisers have an incentive to push deals through; boards and independent advice help balance that.

## Boutiques

A **[[boutique]]** is an independent advisory firm that focuses mainly on M&A and related advice, without a big balance sheet, lending business or trading floor. Examples with big London offices include firms such as Evercore, Lazard, Rothschild & Co, PJT Partners and Moelis, and smaller UK specialists. (Examples only, not recommendations.)

Why clients use them:

- **Independence:** no lending or trading business, so fewer conflicts of interest.
- **Senior attention:** smaller teams led by very experienced bankers.
- **Specialism:** deep expertise in a sector or deal type.

Big banks' advantage is scale: they can advise, lend money for the deal and sell the shares or bonds, all under one roof.

## What the work actually involves

For junior staff (analysts), most days involve:

- **Financial modelling** in spreadsheets: forecasts, valuations, deal maths.
- **Researching** companies and sectors.
- **Building slides:** pitch books, valuation summaries, board presentations.
- **Managing the process:** data rooms, document lists and calls with lawyers, accountants and clients.

Long hours, attention to detail and clear writing matter as much as maths. Many firms hire graduates, and some offer apprenticeships and school-leaver programmes; check each firm's careers pages.

## The common mistake

**"Investment banking is all about trading shares."**

That's one part (sales and trading). Corporate finance, especially advisory, is closer to consulting: analysis, valuation, negotiation and managing complex projects for clients. Boutiques often do no trading at all.

## In real life

- **Follow deals in the news:** note the premium, the price multiple and who advised each side (named in announcements).
- **Learn the language:** M&A, ECM, DCM, comps, precedents, DCF, synergies, premium, due diligence.
- **Interviews:** be ready to walk through a DCF and explain why a buyer might pay a premium.
- **Red flag:** anyone offering "inside information" about a takeover before it's announced. Trading on it is insider dealing, a criminal offence.

## Think about it

:::reveal Why do so many acquisitions fail to create value for the buyer's shareholders?
Buyers often pay a high premium in a competitive auction (the "winner's curse"), overestimate synergies, underestimate the cost and difficulty of combining companies and cultures, or buy for empire-building rather than value. The target's shareholders, who get the premium, usually do better.
:::

:::reveal Why might a company choose a boutique over a big bank to advise on a sale?
To avoid conflicts (the big bank might also lend to a bidder or have its own interests), and to get senior bankers' full attention and specialist expertise. A big bank might be chosen when the deal also needs large-scale financing.
:::
