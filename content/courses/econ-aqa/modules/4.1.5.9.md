## What contestability means

A **[[contestable market]]** is one where the **threat** of new firms entering affects how existing firms behave. What matters is how easily firms **could** enter, not how many are there right now.

A market is more contestable when:

- Barriers to entry are low.
- Barriers to exit are low.
- [[sunk costs|Sunk costs]] are low.
- New firms can get the same technology and can reach customers.

A market is **non-contestable** when entry or exit is costly, as with a new rail line or a power network.

## Sunk costs

**Sunk costs** are costs that can't be recovered if a firm leaves the market, such as advertising or a very specialised machine.

They're an important **barrier to entry and exit**. If a firm could lose a large sum by entering and then leaving, it may not take the risk.

Compare these two:

- Buying a delivery van: most of the money can be recovered by selling it.
- A large advertising campaign: that money is gone.

## Hit-and-run competition

**[[hit-and-run competition|Hit-and-run competition]]** happens when a firm enters a market to take the abnormal profits on offer, then leaves when they've gone. It's possible only when entry and exit are cheap, so sunk costs must be low.

The threat of this is what matters. If existing firms make high profits, they invite hit-and-run entry. So they may hold their prices down and keep their costs low to stop it.

## Why contestability matters for performance

In a highly contestable market, even a monopolist or an oligopolist may act much like a competitive firm:

- **Prices:** kept close to average cost, so that abnormal profits are limited.
- **Efficiency:** firms keep costs down, since a rival could undercut them.
- **Profit:** closer to normal.

So the number of firms alone doesn't tell us how a market will perform. A market with one firm and a real threat of entry could serve consumers better than one with four firms and high barriers.

Governments can use this idea. Lowering barriers can improve a market's performance, without needing a lot of firms. Removing a legal monopoly or making sure that new firms can use existing networks are examples.

## The limits of the idea

- Few markets have **zero** sunk costs.
- Existing firms can respond quickly, for example with a price cut, so a new firm may not get the time to earn a return.
- Brand loyalty may protect existing firms, even when entry is cheap.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain why a firm in a contestable market may not make large abnormal profits, even if it has few rivals. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** A contestable market is one with low barriers to entry and exit, and low sunk costs, so the threat of entry is real.

**Application:** A coach firm that's the only operator on a route could face rivals quickly, since coaches can be hired and later returned.

**Analysis:** If the firm charged a high price, the abnormal profits would be a signal to outsiders. With low sunk costs, they could enter, undercut it and leave when the profit has gone, with little risk. To stop this hit-and-run competition, the firm keeps its price closer to average cost. So the threat of entry limits its profit, whatever the number of firms in the market.
:::

Why it scores: it links low sunk costs to the threat, and the threat to the firm's behaviour.
