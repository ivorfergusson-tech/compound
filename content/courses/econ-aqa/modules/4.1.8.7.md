## What competition policy is for

**[[competition policy|Competition policy]]** is government action to promote competition and to protect consumers from market power. You don't need the detail of the law, only the general principles.

In the UK, the main body is the **[[competition and markets authority|Competition and Markets Authority]] (CMA)**. Its main areas of work are:

- **Mergers:** it can investigate mergers and can block them, or require changes, if they would substantially lessen competition.
- **Cartels:** price-fixing and market-sharing agreements are illegal, and firms can be fined.
- **Abuse of a dominant position:** dominant firms mustn't, for example, drive out rivals unfairly. Being large isn't itself illegal, but abusing that power is.
- **Market studies:** it can look into a whole market that isn't working well.

## Digital markets

Digital platforms raise new issues, since network effects can lead a few firms to dominate. Under a law that took effect in January 2025, the CMA can give very powerful firms **strategic market status** and set rules for how they behave. In October 2025 it gave this status to Google in search, and to both Apple and Google in mobile platforms.

This status isn't a finding of wrongdoing. It lets the regulator act before harm is done.

## The EU

The **European Commission** does a similar job across the EU. It can block mergers, and it can fine firms that form cartels or abuse a dominant position. Since Brexit, the CMA has acted separately, so a large merger may need approval from both.

## Benefits of competition policy

- **Lower prices** and more choice for consumers.
- **Better quality and innovation,** as firms have to keep improving.
- **Lower costs,** with less X-inefficiency.
- **Better allocation of resources,** as prices move closer to marginal cost.
- **Protection for small firms** against unfair behaviour by dominant ones.

## Costs of competition policy

- **Administrative costs:** investigations are slow and expensive for regulators and firms.
- **Lost economies of scale:** blocking a merger could keep costs high, and it may prevent firms from competing globally.
- **Less incentive to innovate,** if firms fear being punished for success.
- **Inadequate information:** regulators must judge what would happen in future, and they may get it wrong.
- **Hard to detect:** cartels are secret, and tacit collusion is hard to prove.

The models from 4.1.5 are useful here. A merger may raise monopoly power, but it may also lower costs. Whether it's good depends on which effect is larger, and on how contestable the market is.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain why a competition authority might block a merger between two large firms. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Competition policy aims to promote competition. In the UK, the CMA can block a merger that would substantially lessen competition.

**Application:** If two of the largest firms in a concentrated market merged, the concentration ratio would rise.

**Analysis:** With fewer rivals, the merged firm has more monopoly power. Its demand becomes less elastic, so it can raise prices and restrict output, which lowers consumer surplus and adds to deadweight loss. With less pressure from rivals, it may also let costs rise and innovate less. So the authority may block the merger to protect consumers.
:::

Why it scores: it uses concentration, elasticity and welfare together, in one chain.
