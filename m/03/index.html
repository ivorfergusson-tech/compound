Recap

Module 1 showed how money grows with compound interest. Module 2 introduced shares, bonds and funds, and the idea that owning real businesses has historically paid off over long periods. But you've probably also seen adverts and social media posts about trading: buying and selling quickly for profit. This module draws a clear line between investing, trading and gambling, using one tool that cuts through the hype: expected value.

Before you read

A friend says they made £400 last month trading on an app, "so it obviously works".

The idea in one sentence

Investing, trading and gambling differ in their expected value, not in how exciting they feel: long-term investing in businesses has a positive expected return, most retail trading has a negative one after costs, and gambling is designed to lose on average.

Three activities that can look alike

On a phone screen, buying a share, opening a trade and placing a bet can look almost the same: a price, a button, a number going up or down. Underneath they're very different:

  • Investing: buying assets, like a slice of hundreds of companies, and holding them for years to share in their profits and growth. Your time horizon, how long you plan to hold, is long: often five, ten or forty years.
  • Trading: buying and selling over days, hours or minutes, trying to profit from price moves. The aim isn't to own a business but to be right about where the price goes next. That's speculation.
  • Gambling: staking money on an uncertain outcome, like a football score or a roulette wheel, where the odds are set so the bookmaker or casino wins on average.

The difference isn't risk alone; all three can lose money. It's what you should expect to happen over many tries.

Expected value

Expected value (EV) is the average result per go if you repeated something many times:

EV = (chance of winning × amount won) − (chance of losing × amount lost)

A fair coin, win £100 or lose £100: EV = 0.5 × 100 − 0.5 × 100 = £0. Over time you'd break even.

Roulette, £10 on red (European wheel): 18 of the 37 numbers are red, so you win £10 with probability 18/37 and lose £10 with probability 19/37. EV = (18/37 × 10) − (19/37 × 10) = −27p per spin. The single green zero gives the casino a 2.7% edge on every bet. Any one night you might win; over thousands of spins the casino can't lose.

That's the point about EV: it doesn't tell you what happens next time. It tells you what happens over many times. Luck dominates the short run; EV wins the long run.

The expected value of each

Long-term investing in a broad spread of companies: positive EV. Companies make products and profits; shareholders share in them. In the US, shares beat inflation by about 6.6% a year above inflation (shares) against about 1.6% (government bonds) from 1900 to 2025. Not every year, but on average over long periods.

Gambling: negative EV by design. The odds are set so the house keeps a slice of every pound bet.

Short-term trading: usually negative EV for individuals, after costs. Here's why:

  • Over minutes or days, price moves are mostly noise. Before costs, for every trader who profits from a move there's another on the other side who loses. Collectively, short-term traders roughly break even before costs.
  • Costs push it below zero: spreads, commissions and overnight charges (module 7) come out of every trade.
  • You're up against professionals with faster systems, better information and more experience.

The evidence matches. Under FCA rules, UK providers must publish how many of their retail customers lose money on CFDs: for big providers it's about 68–82% (big UK providers' required risk warnings, 2026 (average about 70% across 14 FCA-authorised brokers, April 2026)). A study of everyone who started day trading Brazilian equity futures between 2013 and 2015 found that 97% (all individuals who started day trading Brazilian equity futures in 2013–2015; only 0.4% earned more than a bank teller's wage).

Edge: the only reason to trade

An edge is a genuine, repeatable reason why your expected value is positive after all costs. Casinos have an edge (the zero). Index investors effectively have one too: they own productive businesses at very low cost.

A trader needs an edge over the people on the other side of their trades. "I've got a feeling", "it's been going up" or "a YouTuber said so" are not edges. Possible genuine edges include faster or better information, a tested strategy that holds up across many trades (module 27), or strict risk management that keeps losses smaller than wins (module 23). Most beginners have none of these yet.

Back to the question at the start. One good month is no evidence of an edge. With a coin, you'd win a month of flips sometimes. Results only start to mean something over many trades, and even then you have to check they're not luck.

So why learn trading at all?

Because understanding how trading works protects you. You'll know what's really going on when an app gamifies trading, when an advert promises easy profits, or when a friend shows you their winning month. And some people do go on to trade professionally, or simply enjoy it as a skill.

That's why this strand is paper only: you'll practise with pretend money, keep a journal and test your ideas honestly, so you can see whether you have an edge before any real money is involved. Many people discover they don't, which is a cheap and valuable lesson.

The common mistake

"I won, so I made a good decision."

Results and decisions aren't the same. A good decision can lose (a sound investment in a bad year); a bad decision can win (a reckless bet that happened to come off). Judge decisions by their expected value and the process behind them, not by one result. This idea, sometimes called "resulting", is central to trading psychology (module 31).

In real life

  • Keep your core money invested in low-cost diversified funds for the long term. If you want to try trading, it's a separate, small, paper-first activity.
  • Read the risk warning: every UK CFD and spread betting provider must show the percentage of its clients who lose money.
  • Be sceptical of screenshots. People share wins, not losses.
  • Red flag: "trading signals", courses or mentors promising regular profits. If they could reliably make money trading, they wouldn't need to sell courses. The FCA warns that many such schemes are scams.

Think about it

Why do casinos and bookmakers welcome big winners but sometimes ban consistent ones?

A big winner on a negative-EV game is just luck, and the publicity brings in more customers. A consistent winner probably has an edge over the bookmaker's odds, which costs them money over time, so some limit or ban them. It shows how much the long-run edge matters.

Can a trade have a positive expected value even if it loses most of the time?

Yes. If you win 30% of the time but your wins are four times your losses, EV is positive: 0.3 × 4 − 0.7 × 1 = +0.5 per unit risked. Many trend-following strategies work like this. Win rate alone tells you nothing without the size of wins and losses (module 27).
