Two countries have the same population. One has clever engineers, good banks and new motorways.

:::guess {"prompt":"Why would it be able to produce more in the long run? Could the other one catch up by just spending more?","kind":"text","id":"econ-aqa:4.2.2.6-guess"}
:::

:::callout idea
Long-run aggregate supply is what an economy can sustainably produce, and it depends on the quantity and quality of resources and on how well its institutions put them to use.
:::

## What LRAS shows

**Long-run aggregate supply** shows the economy's productive potential. The position of the vertical LRAS curve represents the **normal capacity level of output**.

It's vertical because, in the long run, output depends on real resources, not the price level. A higher price level alone doesn't create any more workers or factories.

Output can rise above this level for a while, as firms use overtime and run machines harder, but that can't be kept up.

## The fundamental determinants

LRAS shifts right when the economy's capacity grows. The main causes are:

- **Technology:** better ways of producing.
- **Productivity:** output per worker, through skills and capital.
- **Attitudes:** to work, risk and training.
- **Enterprise:** people willing to start firms and take risks.
- **Factor mobility:** workers and capital moving to where they're most useful.
- **Economic incentives:** rewards for working, saving and investing, such as those from the tax system.

More labour or capital also adds to capacity, through migration, a higher retirement age or more investment.

## The institutional structure

Institutions are the organisations and rules of an economy. They matter a lot for AS. The spec picks out **the role of the banking system in providing business investment funds**: if banks and other lenders turn savings into loans for firms, more productive investment takes place. If lending is weak or short-term, firms can't fund new capital.

Others include a reliable legal system, secure property rights, good schools and stable government.

## Two views of AS

The vertical LRAS curve is often called the **classical** or free-market view. In this view, markets adjust so that the economy returns to normal capacity in the long run.

**Keynesian** economists see things differently. The **[[keynesian as curve|Keynesian AS curve]]** has three parts:

- **Flat:** when there's a lot of spare capacity, firms can produce more without raising prices, as they can hire idle workers at the same wage.
- **Rising:** as the economy nears capacity, bottlenecks appear, so costs and prices rise.
- **Vertical:** at full capacity, output can't rise any further, so more demand only raises prices.

The two views lead to different policy advice. In the Keynesian view, a government can boost AD in a recession with little inflation. In the classical view, an economy tends to correct itself, so policy should aim to raise capacity.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain how investment in education could affect long-run aggregate supply. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** LRAS shows the productive capacity of the economy. Education is investment in human capital.

**Application:** Better technical training in the UK would raise the skills of the labour force.

**Analysis:** Workers with more skills can produce more per hour, and they can use more advanced technology. They may also be more able to move between jobs, which improves factor mobility. So the quality of labour rises and the capacity of the economy grows, which shifts LRAS to the right. This is underlying growth, though it would take years to come through.
:::

Why it scores: it links skills to productivity and capacity, and it notes the time lag.
