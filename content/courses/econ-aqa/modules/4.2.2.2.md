:::callout idea
AD and AS curves show the whole economy on one diagram: where they cross gives real output and the price level, and shifts in them explain inflation, unemployment and growth.
:::

## The AD/AS diagram

The diagram has the **price level** on the vertical axis and **real output** on the horizontal axis.

- **[[aggregate demand|Aggregate demand (AD)]]** is total planned spending on domestic output at each price level. AD = C + I + G + (X − M).
- **[[short-run aggregate supply|Short-run aggregate supply (SRAS)]]** is the total output firms will supply at each price level, while some costs stay fixed.
- **[[long-run aggregate supply|Long-run aggregate supply (LRAS)]]** shows the economy's productive capacity. It's vertical at the normal capacity level of output.

**[[macroeconomic equilibrium|Macroeconomic equilibrium]]** is where AD = AS.

## Movements and shifts

A change in the **price level** causes a **movement along** the AD and SRAS curves. Anything else causes a **shift**.

AD slopes down because a lower price level:

- Makes UK goods more competitive, so exports rise and imports fall.
- Raises the real value of people's money, so they spend more.
- Tends to reduce interest rates, so borrowing and spending rise.

## Shifts in AD

AD shifts when any of its parts change for a reason other than the price level:

- **C:** confidence, income tax, interest rates or asset prices.
- **I:** interest rates, confidence or expected sales.
- **G:** government decisions.
- **X − M:** the exchange rate, or incomes abroad.

## Shifts in SRAS

SRAS shifts when **costs** change: wages, raw material prices, business taxes or productivity. Higher costs shift it to the left, which means up on the diagram.

## Shifts in LRAS

LRAS shifts when the economy's **productive capacity** changes: the quantity or quality of its factors of production, such as technology, skills and investment. Short-run costs don't move it.

**Underlying economic growth is shown by a rightward shift in LRAS.**

## Demand-side shocks

A **demand-side shock** is a sudden change in AD.

- A **positive** shock shifts AD right, which raises output, the price level and employment.
- A **negative** shock shifts AD left, which lowers output and prices. Firms need fewer workers, so there's **demand-deficient (cyclical) unemployment**.

The 2008 financial crisis is an example of a negative shock: confidence fell and lending dried up, so spending dropped.

## Supply-side shocks

A **supply-side shock** is a sudden change in costs or capacity.

- A **negative** shock shifts SRAS left. Output falls **and** the price level rises. This is **cost-push inflation**, and it's hard for policy, since prices and unemployment worsen together.
- A **positive** shock, such as a fall in oil prices, shifts SRAS right.

The jump in energy prices after Russia invaded Ukraine in 2022 is an example of a negative supply shock.

## The global economy

Global events reach the UK through several routes. A recession abroad cuts exports, so AD falls. Rising world commodity prices raise costs, so SRAS falls. Foreign investment and the exchange rate can shift both.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Using an AD/AS diagram, explain how a fall in consumer confidence could lead to unemployment. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** AD is total planned spending at each price level, and consumption is its largest part. Demand-deficient unemployment arises when AD is too low.

**Application:** Worries about jobs and the cost of living may lead UK households to save more and spend less.

**Analysis:** Consumption falls, so AD shifts left, and the new equilibrium has lower real output and a lower price level. Firms sell less, so they need fewer workers, because labour is a derived demand. Output is now below capacity, which is a negative output gap, and unemployment rises.
:::

Why it scores: it shifts the right curve, gives both effects and links output to jobs.
