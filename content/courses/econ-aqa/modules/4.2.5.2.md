:::callout idea
Supply-side policies aim to raise an economy's capacity: some do this by freeing up markets, and others by the government stepping in where markets fall short.
:::

## Policies and improvements

These aren't the same thing.

**[[supply-side improvements|Supply-side improvements]]** are any changes that raise productive capacity, and they often come from the private sector, through productivity gains, innovation and investment, with no government action.

**[[supply-side policies|Supply-side policies]]** are government measures designed to bring about those improvements.

## How they work

They shift **LRAS** to the right, which can raise the **trend rate of growth**. Tax changes aimed at personal incentives are one example: if people keep more of what they earn, they may work more, train more or start firms, which raises potential output.

They can also have **microeconomic** effects, by changing how particular markets work, as well as **macroeconomic** ones.

## Free market policies

These aim to improve incentives and let markets work better:

- **Tax cuts:** on income, to encourage work, and on profit, to encourage investment.
- **Privatisation:** to bring in the profit motive.
- **Deregulation:** to cut costs and increase competition.
- **Labour market reforms:** weakening trade union power, making hiring easier, or reducing benefits to raise the incentive to work.

## Interventionist policies

These use government action to fix market failures, such as **short-termism**, where firms and banks won't fund long-term projects:

- **Spending on education and training:** to raise skills.
- **Industrial policy:** supporting key industries or regions.
- **Subsidising research and development:** which has positive externalities.
- **Infrastructure spending:** transport and broadband.

## Effects on the main objectives

Here capacity has grown by 20, costs have fallen and AD has grown to match. Real output rises from 80 to 100, and the price level stays at 50. That's growth with no extra inflation.

:::chart adas {"d":20,"s":-20,"l":20}

- **Growth:** LRAS shifts right, so potential output rises.
- **Unemployment:** better skills and incentives reduce structural unemployment, and lower the **natural rate of unemployment**.
- **Inflation:** with more capacity, demand can grow without pushing up prices.
- **The current account:** higher productivity and lower costs make UK goods more competitive, so exports rise and imports fall.

So supply-side policies may ease the conflicts between objectives, as you saw with the long-run Phillips curve.

## Limits

- **Time lags:** training or research takes years to pay off.
- **Cost:** spending has an opportunity cost, and tax cuts reduce revenue.
- **Uncertain results:** tax cuts may not lead people to work more. Some may choose more leisure.
- **Inequality:** free market policies can widen the income gap, and weaken workers' rights.
- **Weak demand:** extra capacity is of little use if nobody buys the output.
- **Government failure:** industrial policy can back the wrong firms.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain how government spending on training could improve the UK's current account. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Training is an interventionist supply-side policy. The current account records trade in goods and services, and income flows.

**Application:** UK firms in engineering have reported shortages of skilled workers.

**Analysis:** Better training raises labour productivity, so firms' unit costs fall. UK goods get more competitive in price and quality, so exports rise, and UK buyers switch from imports. Net exports improve, so the deficit narrows. This would take some years, and it depends on other countries not improving faster.
:::

Why it scores: it follows the chain from skills to costs to trade, and it adds a judgement.
