## Guess first

A small café has one coffee machine. The owner hires a second barista, then a fifth, then a tenth.

:::guess {"prompt":"What happens to the extra coffees made by each new barista? Why?","kind":"text","id":"econ-aqa:4.1.4.3-guess"}
:::

:::callout idea
In the short run, adding more of one input to a fixed input eventually adds less and less output; in the long run, all inputs can change, and output may rise faster or slower than the inputs.
:::

## Short run and long run

- The **[[short run]]** is the period when **at least one factor of production is fixed**, usually capital such as a building.
- The **[[long run]]** is the period when **all factors can be changed**.

These aren't set lengths of time. A market stall's long run may be a week. A power station's may be ten years.

## Total, average and marginal returns

Say the input is labour:

- **Total returns** (total product): all the output made.
- **Average returns**: output per worker (total ÷ workers).
- **Marginal returns**: the **extra** output from one more worker.

| Workers | Total output | Marginal returns | Average returns |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 10 | 10 | 10 |
| 2 | 24 | 14 | 12 |
| 3 | 42 | 18 | 14 |
| 4 | 54 | 12 | 13.5 |
| 5 | 60 | 6 | 12 |
| 6 | 60 | 0 | 10 |

:::

## The law of diminishing returns

The **[[law of diminishing returns]]** says: in the **short run**, as more of a variable factor is added to a **fixed factor**, the marginal returns will **eventually fall**.

In the table, marginal returns rise at first because the extra workers can specialise. From the fourth worker, they fall, because there's only one café. Workers get in each other's way and queue for the machine.

Note: marginal returns fall, while total output can still rise. It's also a **short-run** idea, because it depends on a fixed factor.

## Returns to scale

**[[returns to scale|Returns to scale]]** is a **long-run** idea: what happens to output when **all** inputs increase by the same proportion.

- **Increasing returns to scale:** output rises by a bigger proportion than inputs. Double the inputs, more than double the output.
- **Constant returns to scale:** output rises by the same proportion.
- **Decreasing returns to scale:** output rises by a smaller proportion.

## What this means for costs

Both ideas explain how inputs link to output, so both affect costs:

- **Diminishing returns** push short-run costs up: each extra unit takes more labour to make, so marginal cost rises.
- **Increasing returns to scale** push long-run average costs down. Decreasing returns push them up.

You'll see these effects on the cost curves in the next lesson.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain the difference between the law of diminishing returns and decreasing returns to scale. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** The law of diminishing returns applies in the short run, when at least one factor is fixed. Decreasing returns to scale apply in the long run, when all factors can be changed.

**Application:** A farm with a fixed area of land that hires more workers will eventually gain less extra output from each one. A firm that doubles all its factories and staff but gets less than double the output has decreasing returns to scale.

**Analysis:** Diminishing returns occur because more of a variable factor is sharing a fixed factor. Decreasing returns to scale are not caused by a fixed factor, as all inputs have increased. Instead, they usually come from the problems of running a very large organisation.
:::

Why it scores: it separates the time periods, gives an example of each, and explains why the causes differ.
