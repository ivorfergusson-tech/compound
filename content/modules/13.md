## What borrowing really is

When you borrow, you get money now and pay back more later. The extra is **[[interest]]**: the price of using someone else's money. Borrowing isn't good or bad in itself. A mortgage lets people live in a home while paying for it; a student loan funds a degree. The question is always whether the thing is worth the price, and whether you can afford the repayments.

The main kinds of consumer borrowing in the UK:

- **Credit cards:** you can borrow up to a limit, repay any amount above the minimum each month, and borrow again.
- **[[overdraft|Overdrafts]]:** your current account goes below £0.
- **Personal loans:** a fixed amount, repaid in fixed monthly payments over a set time.
- **[[bnpl|Buy now, pay later]]** (BNPL): splitting a purchase into a few payments, often interest-free.
- **Car finance:** loans or PCP deals to buy or use a car.
- **Payday and other high-cost short-term loans:** small, short loans at very high rates.

## APR: the price tag

**[[apr|APR]]** (Annual Percentage Rate) is the yearly cost of borrowing, including interest and any compulsory fees, written as a percentage. It lets you compare different loans and cards on the same scale. Lower is cheaper.

Two things to know:

- Adverts show a **representative APR**. By law, at least 51% of people accepted must get that rate or better. Up to 49% may be offered more, depending on their credit record. You only find out your rate after applying (or from an eligibility checker).
- Most cards charge interest **monthly**. A 30% APR is about 2.2% a month. That doesn't sound like much, but on £1,500 it's about £33 in the first month.

## Credit cards: useful tool, expensive habit

A credit card can be the cheapest borrowing there is, or among the dearest:

- **Pay the full balance every month** and you usually pay **no interest at all** on purchases. You also get protection: for single purchases over £100 and up to £30,000 paid by credit card, the card company is jointly responsible if something goes wrong (Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act).
- **Pay less than the full balance** and interest is charged, often on the whole amount from the date of each purchase.
- **Cash withdrawals** on a credit card usually cost a fee plus a higher rate from day one. Avoid them.

The safest setup for a first card: a low limit, a **direct debit for the full balance** each month, and only spending what you'd spend from your current account anyway.

## The minimum payment trap

Each month a card asks for a **minimum payment**, often interest plus about 1% of the balance, or a small fixed amount if that's more. Pay only that and the balance falls painfully slowly, because most of each payment is eaten by interest.

Back to the question at the start. £1,500 at 30% APR, paying only the minimum (interest + 1%):

- It takes about **279 months: over 23 years**.
- You pay about **£3,080 in interest**, more than twice what you borrowed.

Pay a fixed **£100 a month** instead and it's gone in **19 months** with about **£343** of interest.

This is the **[[minimum payment trap]]**. The FCA's rules say that if you've paid more in interest and charges than off the balance for 18 months, the card company must contact you, and after three years in that state it must help you clear it faster. But you don't need to wait for that: set a fixed payment you can afford, well above the minimum.

## Overdrafts and buy now, pay later

**Overdrafts.** An **arranged** overdraft is agreed with your bank; an **unarranged** one is going below £0 without agreement (or past your limit), and the bank can refuse payments. Overdrafts are meant for short gaps. At about 40% EAR, borrowing £500 for a whole year costs around **£200**, which is far more than a credit card paid in full or a personal loan.

**Buy now, pay later.** BNPL splits a purchase, often into three or four interest-free payments. It's easy to use, which is the risk: several BNPL plans at once can quietly add up to more than you can pay. Missed payments can bring late fees, be passed to debt collectors, and show on your credit file.

Since {{fig:bnplRules+}}, BNPL lenders working through shops have had to follow FCA rules. Treat each plan as a debt and add it to your budget.

## Your credit file and credit score

When you apply to borrow, the lender checks your **[[credit file]]**: your record of borrowing and paying. The UK has three main **credit reference agencies**: Experian, Equifax and TransUnion. Each keeps its own file on you, showing:

- your credit accounts (cards, loans, overdrafts, phone contracts, BNPL) and whether you've paid on time,
- how much of your credit limits you're using,
- searches made when you applied for credit,
- whether you're on the electoral roll at your address,
- serious problems such as defaults or County Court Judgments (CCJs). Most records stay for six years.

Your **[[credit score]]** is a number each agency (or app) works out from your file to estimate how risky you are. Lenders don't see that number; they use your file plus their own rules. So there's no single "credit score" and no universal pass mark. But the things that improve one improve them all.

You have a legal right to see your file from each agency free of charge, and there are free apps that show it.

## Building a good record

At 18 most people have a **thin file**: no history, which lenders can treat as risky. Ways to build one:

- **Register to vote** at your address. It confirms who and where you are.
- **Pay every bill on time**, including phone contracts. Set up direct debits.
- **One credit card, used lightly and paid in full** each month by direct debit. Using a small part of the limit and clearing it shows you can handle credit.
- **Avoid lots of applications** in a short time. Each full application leaves a "hard search" on your file. Use eligibility checkers first: they use a "soft search", which lenders can't see.
- **Keep credit separate from people who don't pay**: joint accounts link your file to theirs.
- **Never miss a payment.** One missed payment can stay on your file for six years.

## High-cost credit and getting help

**Payday loans** and other high-cost short-term credit are very expensive. The FCA caps them (no more than 0.8% a day in interest and fees, and you can never pay back more than double what you borrowed), but they're still among the dearest ways to borrow. Many people who use them end up borrowing again to repay.

**Illegal lenders (loan sharks)** lend without FCA authorisation and may use threats. You can check any lender on the FCA register and report illegal lending to the Stop Loan Sharks service.

**If debt is getting on top of you**, free, confidential help is available from services such as MoneyHelper, StepChange and National Debtline. Avoid companies that charge to "sort out" your debts: the free charities do the same job. Talking to the lender early is also better than missing payments.

## The common mistake

**"I need to borrow and pay interest to build my credit score."**

You don't. The way to show you can handle credit is to use it and **pay on time**. A credit card paid in full every month builds your record without costing a penny in interest. Paying interest proves nothing extra.

## In real life

- **Compare on APR**, and use eligibility checkers before applying.
- **Set a direct debit** for the full card balance, or at least a fixed amount well above the minimum.
- **Count BNPL as debt** in your budget.
- **Check your credit file** from all three agencies once a year and fix mistakes.
- **Register to vote** at your current address.
- **Red flag:** "guaranteed approval", lenders not on the FCA register, or anyone asking for a fee upfront before lending.

## Think about it

:::reveal Why do lenders charge some people higher rates than others for the same card?
They price for risk. People with a strong record of paying on time are less likely to default, so lenders compete for them with lower rates. People with no history or missed payments look riskier, so they're offered higher rates or turned down.
:::

:::reveal Is it ever sensible to keep savings while having credit card debt?
A small emergency fund, yes, so a surprise doesn't add more debt. But beyond that, savings earning around 4–5% while a card charges around 30% is a losing trade: paying off the card is like earning 30% risk-free.
:::
