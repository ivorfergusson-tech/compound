Index funds, ETFs, fees and platforms

Recap

Module 6 introduced the index, a number that tracks a whole market, and module 10 showed that diversification cuts risk without cutting expected return. This module brings them together in the simplest, cheapest way to invest that most people will ever need: the index fund. It also shows the cost that quietly decides how much of the return you keep: fees.

Before you read

You invest £1,000 now and £100 a month for 30 years. Your investments grow 6% a year before fees. Option A charges 0.3% a year in total fees; option B charges 1.3%.

The idea in one sentence

Most professional fund managers fail to beat the market after their fees, so for most people the best plan is to own the whole market cheaply and let low costs and time do the work.

Active vs passive

There are two ways to run a fund:

  • Active: a manager and their team pick which shares or bonds to buy, trying to beat the market (or a benchmark index). They research, visit companies and trade.
  • Passive: the fund simply copies an index, holding every company in it in proportion to its size. No picking, no forecasting. This is an index fund, also called a tracker.

Active management sounds like it should win: surely experts can spot the good companies? But the evidence says most don't, over the long run:

  • AJ Bell's research found only about 30% (across seven equity sectors, to mid-2025; S&P's SPIVA found 97% of European active equity funds lagged their benchmark over 10 years to end-2025).

Why do the experts lose? Mostly arithmetic:

  1. Before costs, all investors together are the market. For every active manager who beats it, another must lag it. On average, active investors earn the market return before fees.
  2. After costs, the average active investor must lag. Active funds charge more (to pay for research and trading), so after fees most fall behind a cheap tracker that earns the market return minus a tiny fee.
  3. Picking the winners in advance is hard. Last decade's top funds are often not next decade's.

None of this says no active manager ever wins. Some do. But picking them in advance is hard, and the fee drag is certain.

Fees: the return you can control

You can't control what markets do. You can control what you pay. Investing costs come in layers:

  • The fund's ongoing charge (the OCF): a yearly percentage of what you hold in the fund, taken automatically from its value. Index trackers are often about 0.05–0.25% a year (examples include FTSE All-Share trackers at 0.06%; active funds average about 0.9–1%).
  • The platform fee: what the investment app or website charges to hold your investments. Some charge a percentage of your pot each year; some charge a flat monthly fee, which can be better value for bigger pots and worse for small ones.
  • Trading costs: some platforms charge per trade. Buying UK company shares also has 0.5% (Stamp Duty Reserve Tax on most UK company shares; not charged on buying units in UK funds from the fund manager).

Percentages that look tiny compound into big sums, because they're charged on your whole pot every year, including all past growth.

Back to the question at the start. On those numbers you'd pay in £37,000. With 0.3% fees you'd end up with about £97,600; with 1.3%, about £81,300. The extra 1% a year costs about £16,300, nearly half of everything you paid in.

ETFs and other kinds of tracker

Index funds come in two main forms:

  • Index funds (unit trusts or OEICs): bought from the fund at a price set once a day.
  • ETFs (exchange-traded funds): trade on a stock exchange like shares, with prices all day.

For a long-term investor both work well; the differences (when you can trade, how fees are charged on your platform) matter less than the index and the total cost. Pick by cost and what the fund holds.

Which index? This decides what you own:

  • A UK index (like the FTSE 100 or All-Share) holds only UK-listed companies, a small slice of the world's markets, with a lot in a few industries.
  • A global index (like the MSCI World or FTSE All-World) holds thousands of companies across many countries. Many beginners start here, for maximum diversification.
  • Multi-asset or "lifestyle" funds hold shares and bonds in a set mix, such as 60/40, and rebalance automatically (module 30).

Note: global indices are market-cap weighted, so US companies currently make up a large share of them. That's a feature of how markets are sized, not a choice by the fund.

Accumulation or income?

Funds usually come in two versions, called share classes or units:

  • Accumulation (Acc): dividends are automatically reinvested inside the fund, so your holding grows. Best for building wealth.
  • Income (Inc): dividends are paid out to you as cash. Useful if you want an income.

The fund's investments are the same; only what happens to the dividends changes. For long-term growth, most people choose accumulation.

Choosing a platform

To buy funds you need an account with an investment platform (or a provider's own app). Things to compare:

  • Fees for your pot size: percentage or flat?
  • The account types offered: Stocks and Shares ISA, Lifetime ISA, SIPP, general account.
  • The funds available and any trading charges.
  • FCA authorisation: check the firm on the FCA register. Platforms must keep your investments separate from their own money.
  • Ease of use: you'll stick with something you understand.

Then the routine is simple: open a Stocks and Shares ISA (module 17), set up a monthly direct debit, choose a low-cost global index fund (accumulation), and actually buy it. Then leave it alone.

Investing regularly

Putting in a fixed amount every month has a useful side effect called pound-cost averaging: when prices are low, your £100 buys more units; when high, fewer. You never have to guess the "right time" to invest. It also builds the habit, which matters more than timing.

If you have a lump sum, Vanguard's research found that investing it straight away beat spreading it over a year roughly two-thirds of the time, in the US and UK alike, because markets rise more often than they fall. But spreading it over a few months can make it easier to live with if prices drop soon after. Either is reasonable; not investing at all usually isn't.

The common mistake

"Cheap funds must be worse; you get what you pay for."

In most things, paying more gets you more. In investing, the opposite tends to hold: the fee is one of the few things that reliably predicts future returns, and lower is better. A tracker charging 0.1% isn't a cheap, bad version of a good fund. It's simply giving you the market return without paying someone to try (and usually fail) to beat it.

In real life

  • Add up all the fees: fund OCF + platform fee + trading costs.
  • Start with a broad, low-cost index fund in a Stocks and Shares ISA or pension.
  • Choose accumulation units if you're building wealth.
  • Automate a monthly amount and don't check prices every day.
  • Check your pension's default fund too: what does it hold, and what does it cost?
  • Red flag: a "manager" or "adviser" on social media promising to beat the market reliably, especially with high or hidden fees.

Think about it

If index funds are so good, what happens if everyone uses them?

Active investors are what set prices by researching companies. If almost everyone went passive, mispricings could appear and active investing would become more rewarding again, pulling some people back. In practice active investors still set most prices today, so this isn't close to being a problem.

Why might a global index fund feel risky for a UK investor, and is it?

Most of your money would be in foreign companies, priced in other currencies, so currency moves affect your returns. But spreading across many countries usually lowers overall risk, because you're not relying on one economy. Many big UK companies earn much of their money abroad anyway.

  2. Look up the platform's fee for a pot of £1,000 and £20,000. Percentage or flat: which is cheaper for you?
