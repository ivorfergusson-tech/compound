Before you read

You own a UK government bond paying a 4% coupon, with 30 years left to run. Market interest rates jump by one percentage point.

The idea in one sentence

A bond's price is the present value of its fixed payments, so when market yields rise its price falls, and the longer the bond the bigger the fall; the yield curve compares yields across lengths of loan and shows what markets expect for interest rates and the economy.

Gilts and other bonds

UK government bonds are gilts, named after the gilded edges of old paper certificates. The government sells them through the Debt Management Office to fund the gap between what it spends and what it raises in tax. They range from a year or two to more than 50 years. There are also index-linked gilts, whose payments rise with inflation.

Companies issue corporate bonds, which usually pay more than gilts because there's more chance they won't pay (default risk).

Price and yield

A bond's yield is the yearly return you'd get if you bought at today's price and held it to maturity, counting both the coupons and the difference between the price paid and the £100 you get back at the end.

The key rule: price and yield move in opposite directions. A bond's coupon is fixed when it's issued. If market rates rise, new bonds pay more, so nobody will pay full price for an old bond paying less: its price falls until its yield matches the market. When rates fall, old bonds paying more become valuable, and their price rises.

Mathematically, the price is the present value of all the future coupons plus the £100 at the end, discounted at the market yield (module 22). A 4% coupon bond with 10 years left:

  • If the market wants a 4% yield, it's worth £100 ("at par").
  • If the market wants 5%, it's worth about £92.28.
  • If the market wants 6%, about £85.28.

Duration: why longer bonds swing more

Duration measures how sensitive a bond's price is to changes in yields. The longer you wait for your money, the more each change in yield is compounded over those years, so long bonds are much more sensitive than short ones.

Back to the question at the start. For a 4% bond, a rise in yields from 5% to 6% does this:

  • 2 years left: price falls from about £98.14 to £96.33, less than 2%.
  • 10 years left: from about £92.28 to £85.28, nearly 8%.
  • 30 years left: from about £84.63 to £72.47, over 14%.

So the answer is closer to 15%. "Safe" government bonds can lose a lot of value if you have to sell before maturity, especially long ones. If you hold to maturity, you still get the £100 back (unless the issuer defaults).

This bit hard in September 2022. After the government's "mini-budget" of unfunded tax cuts, gilt yields jumped sharply. Many pension schemes used "liability-driven investment" (LDI) strategies with borrowed money backed by long gilts. As prices plunged, they had to sell gilts to raise cash, which pushed prices down further. The Bank of England stepped in to buy long gilts to stop the spiral. It showed how bond markets can become a financial stability problem.

Credit ratings

A credit rating is an agency's opinion of how likely a borrower is to pay its debts in full and on time. The best-known agencies are S&P, Moody's and Fitch. Scales run from AAA (strongest) down through AA, A and BBB to C and D (in default).

  • Bonds rated BBB− or above (on the S&P and Fitch scale) are investment grade.
  • Below that they're high yield, also called "junk": higher risk, higher yields.

The lower the rating, the higher the yield investors demand: that extra yield over a government bond is the credit spread. The UK government is rated in the AA range. Ratings aren't infallible: in 2007–08, many mortgage-linked bonds rated AAA collapsed (module 32).

The yield curve

The yield curve plots yields on bonds from the same issuer against how long they have to run, from short to long.

Normally it slopes upwards: lenders want more for tying money up longer and taking more interest-rate and inflation risk. In October 2026, UK gilt auctions paid about 4.3% (2028 gilt), 4.8% (2031), 5.4% (2036) and 5.8% (2063) (Debt Management Office auctions, 7–20 October 2026: longer loans paying higher yields): a normal upward slope.

What moves its shape:

  • Short yields follow Bank Rate and expectations of the next few moves (module 12).
  • Long yields reflect expected inflation and growth over decades, plus how much extra investors demand for the risk of lending for so long, including worries about government borrowing.

Inversion

Sometimes short-term yields rise above long-term ones: an inverted curve, called inversion. It usually happens when the central bank has pushed short rates up to fight inflation, while markets expect a slowdown that will force rate cuts later.

In the US, inversions came before most recessions of recent decades, which is why people watch them. But it's not a reliable timer: the gap between inversion and recession has varied a lot, and some inversions haven't been followed by one.

Why bonds matter to everyone

  • Mortgage rates: fixed-rate mortgages are priced off markets for 2- and 5-year rates, closely linked to gilt yields.
  • Pensions: annuity rates and many pension schemes depend on long gilt yields.
  • Government budgets: higher yields mean the government pays more interest on its debt, leaving less for public services.
  • Share prices: higher bond yields raise the discount rate for valuing companies (module 22).

The common mistake

"Government bonds are risk-free."

A UK gilt held to maturity is extremely unlikely to default. But its price can fall a lot if yields rise, as 2022 showed, and inflation can eat into fixed payments. "Safe from default" isn't the same as "safe from losses".

In real life

  • Watch 2- and 5-year gilt yields if you're fixing a mortgage soon.
  • Match bond length to when you need the money if you buy bonds or bond funds: long bond funds swing a lot.
  • Check a bond fund's duration on its factsheet.
  • Red flag: "high-yield bonds" or "mini-bonds" sold to ordinary investors promising fixed high returns. High yield means high risk, and many mini-bonds have collapsed.

Think about it

Why would anyone buy a 30-year gilt yielding less than a 2-year one?

If they expect rates to fall a lot, locking in a yield for 30 years could be valuable, and the bond's price would rise as rates fall. Pension schemes and insurers also need long bonds to match long-term promises, whatever the curve looks like.

How could rising government borrowing push up long gilt yields?

More gilts must be sold, and investors may worry about inflation or the government's finances over the long term, so they demand higher yields to lend for decades. That then raises the government's interest bill, which is why markets watch Budgets closely.
