## The economic cycle

The **[[economic cycle|economic cycle]]** (or trade cycle) is the pattern of ups and downs in real GDP around its long-run trend.

The phases are:

- **Boom (peak):** output is high and above trend.
- **Downturn:** growth slows. In a **recession**, real GDP falls, usually defined as two quarters in a row of negative growth.
- **Trough:** the low point.
- **Recovery:** growth picks up again.

## Using indicators to spot the phase

| Indicator | Boom | Recession |
|---|---|---|
| Real GDP | Growing quickly | Falling |
| Inflation | Rising | Falling |
| Unemployment | Low | Rising |
| Investment | High | Falling sharply |

Unemployment often lags behind output, as firms are slow to fire or hire. Investment swings the most, as the accelerator suggests.

## Output gaps

An **[[output gap]]** is the difference between actual real GDP and the economy's productive potential.

- A **positive output gap** means that real GDP is **above** potential. Resources are overused, so there's upward pressure on wages and prices.
- A **negative output gap** means that real GDP is **below** potential. Resources are idle, and there's cyclical unemployment.

Potential output can't be seen directly, so output gaps have to be estimated, and early estimates can be wrong.

## What causes the phases to change?

**Demand-side shocks** shift AD. They can be:

- **Domestic:** a change in interest rates, taxes or confidence.
- **Global:** a recession in trading partners, which cuts exports.

**Supply-side shocks** shift AS. They can be:

- **Domestic:** a big strike, a poor harvest or a change in taxes on firms.
- **Global:** a jump in oil prices, or a break in supply chains.

The 2008 financial crisis and the 2020 pandemic each brought a sharp fall in UK output.

## Why do cycles happen?

Some causes of instability come from within the economy itself.

- **Excessive growth in credit and debt.** In good times, lending grows quickly. When borrowers can't repay, banks cut back, and spending falls.
- **Asset price bubbles.** House or share prices can rise far above their true value, then crash, which destroys wealth and confidence.
- **Destabilising speculation.** People buy because prices are rising, which pushes prices up further, until the trend reverses.
- **Animal spirits or herding.** Keynes's term "animal spirits" describes the mood of optimism or fear that drives decisions. People copy each other, so swings get bigger.

The multiplier and accelerator then make any change in spending larger.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain how an asset price bubble could lead to a recession. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** An asset price bubble occurs when the price of assets, such as houses, rises far above its underlying value. A recession is a fall in real GDP.

**Application:** Before the 2008 crisis, UK house prices and mortgage lending rose rapidly.

**Analysis:** Rising prices make people feel wealthier, so they borrow and spend more. When the bubble bursts, prices fall, so people's wealth drops and many are left with large debts. Consumption falls, banks lend less and firms cut investment, so AD shifts left. The multiplier makes the fall larger, so real GDP falls.
:::

Why it scores: it follows the bubble up and down, and links it to AD and the multiplier.
