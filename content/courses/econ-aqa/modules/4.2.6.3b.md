## Guess first

A country has a big current account deficit. Its government could try to make people buy fewer imports.

:::guess {"prompt":"How would you do that? What might it cost in jobs, prices or growth?","kind":"text","id":"econ-aqa:4.2.6.3b-guess"}

:::callout idea
Governments can correct a deficit by switching spending towards home goods or by reducing spending overall, or by improving competitiveness, and each option affects other objectives.
:::

## Expenditure-switching policies

**[[expenditure-switching|Expenditure-switching]]** policies aim to move spending from imports to home-produced goods.

- **Devaluation or depreciation:** a lower exchange rate makes exports cheaper and imports dearer.
- **Protectionism:** tariffs and quotas make imports dearer or scarcer.

## Expenditure-reducing policies

**[[expenditure-reducing|Expenditure-reducing]]** policies aim to reduce total spending, so that less goes on imports.

- **Contractionary fiscal policy:** higher taxes or lower government spending.
- **Contractionary monetary policy:** higher interest rates.

## Supply-side policies

Over the long run, the deepest fix is to improve **competitiveness**. Better skills, productivity and innovation lower costs and raise quality, so exports grow and fewer imports are needed.

## Effects on other objectives

| Policy | Effect on the current account | Conflict |
|---|---|---|
| Depreciation | Improves, if demand is elastic enough | Dearer imports raise inflation |
| Tariffs | Reduces imports | Higher prices, retaliation, less efficiency |
| Higher interest rates | Spending and imports fall | Lower growth, more unemployment, and a stronger pound can hurt exporters |
| Higher taxes | Imports fall | Lower growth and more unemployment |
| Supply-side policies | Improves over time | Slow, and costly |

So a government may have to trade off the current account against growth, jobs and inflation.

A fall in the exchange rate may also take time to work. In the short run, the deficit can get worse, as contracts are fixed and people take time to switch.

## Does a deficit matter?

It depends on the cause and the size.

**A deficit may be a concern if:**

- It's large and lasting, and it reflects poor competitiveness.
- It's funded by short-term flows that could leave quickly.
- It's financed by rising debt or selling assets to foreigners, which means future outflows of income.
- It leads to a sharp fall in the currency.

**It may matter less if:**

- It's small, or temporary, as the economy recovers.
- It's funded by long-term FDI that adds to capacity.
- It reflects strong growth and imports of capital goods.

## What about a surplus?

A surplus isn't always good. It may mean:

- Low consumption at home, or an undervalued currency.
- Fewer goods and services for people to enjoy now.
- Upward pressure on the exchange rate.
- Pressure from other countries, as one country's surplus is another's deficit.

## Global imbalances

Some major economies run large surpluses for years, while others run large deficits. The surplus countries lend their savings to those in deficit.

If a major economy takes corrective action, the effects spread worldwide. If a deficit country cuts its spending, or adds tariffs, its partners sell less, so their growth slows. If a surplus country raises spending, or lets its currency rise, others can export more. Corrections are smoother when they're shared. If a country acts alone, or suddenly, the world economy can suffer, and trade disputes may follow.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain how a depreciation of the pound could affect the UK's current account and its rate of inflation. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** A depreciation is a fall in the value of a currency in a floating exchange rate system. It's an expenditure-switching policy.

**Application:** If the pound falls, UK whisky is cheaper in dollars, and imported oil costs more in pounds.

**Analysis:** Foreign buyers purchase more UK exports, and UK buyers switch from imports, so the current account improves, provided demand is price elastic enough. But imported goods and raw materials now cost more, which raises firms' costs and shop prices, so cost-push inflation rises. AD also rises, which adds to inflation. So there's a conflict between the two objectives.
:::

Why it scores: it covers both effects, adds a condition and names the conflict.
