## From markets to the whole economy

Paper 1 looked at single markets. **Macroeconomics** looks at the whole economy: total output, the general price level, total employment and trade with the rest of the world.

## The four main objectives

- **[[economic growth|Economic growth]]:** a sustained rise in real output, which can raise living standards.
- **[[price stability|Price stability]]:** low and stable inflation. In the UK, the government sets a target of **2%** for CPI inflation.
- **Minimising [[unemployment]]:** as many people who want work as possible have jobs.
- **A stable [[balance of payments on current account|balance of payments on current account]]:** no large or lasting deficit or surplus in trade and income with other countries.

## Other objectives

Governments may also aim for:

- **Balancing the budget:** bringing government spending and tax revenue closer together.
- **An equitable distribution of income:** a fair sharing out of income, which you met in 4.1.7.
- **Protecting the environment,** so that growth can be sustained.

## Conflicts between objectives

The objectives can clash, at least in the short run.

**Growth and inflation.** If demand grows faster than the economy can produce, firms raise prices.

**Unemployment and inflation.** As unemployment falls, workers can bargain for more pay, which adds to costs and prices. You'll meet this as the Phillips curve in 4.2.3.

**Growth and the current account.** As incomes rise, people buy more imports, which can widen a deficit.

**Growth and the environment.** More output can mean more pollution and resource use.

**Growth and equality.** Lower top tax rates might encourage enterprise, but could widen the income gap.

Some conflicts fade in the long run. Supply-side improvements that raise productive capacity can allow more growth with no extra inflation.

## Priorities change over time

The importance given to each objective changes with events and ideas.

In the decades after the Second World War, full employment was a leading aim in the UK. In the 1970s and 1980s, high inflation moved price stability to the top. The Bank of England was given independence over interest rates in 1997, to pursue the inflation target. After the 2008 financial crisis, recovery and government debt came to the fore.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain why a government may find it hard to achieve low unemployment and low inflation at the same time. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Macroeconomic objectives include minimising unemployment and price stability, which in the UK means CPI inflation of 2%.

**Application:** A government may raise spending to create jobs when unemployment is high.

**Analysis:** More spending raises aggregate demand, so firms hire more workers. As spare labour runs out, firms compete for staff and wages rise, which adds to their costs. Firms pass this on, and rising demand also pulls prices up. So the policy that reduces unemployment can raise inflation, at least in the short run.
:::

Why it scores: it builds a chain from the policy to jobs, wages and prices.
