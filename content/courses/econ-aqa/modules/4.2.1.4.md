## National income and living standards

**National income** data, such as GDP, measures the value of output and income in an economy. **Living standards** are about people's quality of life, including health, education, leisure and the environment.

Rising real GDP per capita usually means that people can buy more, so it's the most used measure. But the two aren't the same.

## Limits over time

Comparing the same country across years has these problems:

- **Inflation and population:** use real figures per capita, not nominal totals.
- **Income distribution:** an average can rise while most people gain little.
- **The hidden economy:** cash jobs not declared to the tax authorities aren't counted.
- **Unpaid work:** care in the home and volunteering aren't counted.
- **Quality:** a phone costs about the same as it did years ago, but does far more.
- **Externalities:** pollution isn't subtracted, and clearing it up can add to GDP.
- **Hours worked:** output may rise because people work longer, with less leisure.
- **What's produced:** extra spending on defence or crime prevention adds to GDP, but may not make people better off.

## Limits between countries

Comparing countries adds further problems:

- **Exchange rates:** figures must be converted to one currency, and market rates can mislead, as you'll see below.
- **Different prices:** the same income buys more in some countries.
- **Size of the hidden economy,** which is larger in some countries.
- **Non-market output:** in poorer countries, more food may be grown for the family and never sold.
- **Different needs:** cold countries must spend more on heating.
- **Population structure:** countries differ in the share of children and retired people.
- **Inequality and culture:** the same average can hide very different lives.

## Purchasing power parity

Market exchange rates reflect trade and flows of money, not what income buys at home. If you convert a poor country's income at market rates, you usually understate its living standards, as many local goods and services are cheap.

**[[purchasing power parity|Purchasing power parity (PPP)]]** exchange rates adjust for this. A PPP rate is the rate at which a given amount of money buys the same basket of goods in both countries.

Here's an example with simple numbers. A basket costs £100 in the UK and 1,000 rupees in another country, so the PPP rate is 10 rupees to £1. Say the market rate is 100 rupees to £1. Someone earning 100,000 rupees would have £1,000 at the market rate, but their income buys what £10,000 would buy in the UK.

So PPP gives a fairer comparison, and it's what economists should use when comparing living standards between countries.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain why PPP exchange rates should be used when comparing living standards between countries. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Purchasing power parity exchange rates are rates at which a given sum buys the same basket of goods and services in each country.

**Application:** Many services, such as haircuts and meals, are much cheaper in India than in the UK.

**Analysis:** If incomes in India are converted at market exchange rates, this cheaper cost of living is ignored, so people there look poorer than they are. Market rates are also affected by flows of money and can change sharply. A PPP rate shows what income can actually buy, so it gives a more accurate comparison of living standards.
:::

Why it scores: it defines PPP and shows what goes wrong without it.
