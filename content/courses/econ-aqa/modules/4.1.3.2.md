A petrol station raises its prices by 10%. A coffee shop on a busy street with three rivals does the same.

:::callout idea
Elasticity measures how strongly demand responds to a change in price, income or the price of another good, and it tells firms and governments what will happen to sales and revenue.
:::

## Price elasticity of demand (PED)

**[[price elasticity of demand|Price elasticity of demand]]** measures how responsive quantity demanded is to a change in the good's own price.

**PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price**

If price rises 10% and quantity demanded falls 20%, PED = −20 ÷ 10 = **−2**.

PED is normally negative, because price and quantity move in opposite directions. Look at the size, ignoring the sign, when comparing.

| Size (ignoring the sign) | Name | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| More than 1 | **Elastic** | Quantity changes by a bigger % than price |
| Less than 1 | **Inelastic** | Quantity changes by a smaller % than price |
| Exactly 1 | Unit elastic | Same % change |
| 0 | Perfectly inelastic | Quantity doesn't change at all |
| Infinity | Perfectly elastic | Any price rise loses all sales |

To work out a % change: (new − old) ÷ old × 100.

## What makes demand elastic or inelastic?

- **Substitutes:** the more close substitutes, the more elastic. This is usually the key factor. One brand of crisps is elastic; food as a whole is inelastic.
- **Necessity or luxury:** necessities and addictive goods tend to be inelastic.
- **Share of income:** a big share (a car) tends to be elastic, a tiny share (matches) inelastic.
- **Time:** demand becomes more elastic over time, as people find alternatives.
- **Definition of the market:** one brand of petrol is more elastic than petrol as a whole.

## PED and total revenue

**[[total revenue|Total revenue]] = price × quantity sold.** It's also what consumers spend in total (total expenditure).

- If demand is **inelastic**: raise price, revenue **rises**. The lost sales are small compared with the extra paid per unit.
- If demand is **elastic**: raise price, revenue **falls**. Cutting price raises revenue.
- If demand is **unit elastic**: revenue stays about the same.

Set a price rise, then move PED either side of 1.

This is one reason governments tax goods like tobacco and fuel: demand is inelastic, so tax raises a lot of money and sales fall by only a little.

## Income elasticity of demand (YED)

**[[income elasticity of demand|Income elasticity of demand]]** measures how responsive demand is to a change in income.

**YED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in income**

Here, the **sign matters**:

- **Positive YED:** a **[[normal good]]**. Demand rises with income. Between 0 and 1 it's income inelastic, often a necessity. Above 1 it's income elastic, often a luxury such as foreign holidays.
- **Negative YED:** an **[[inferior good]]**. Demand falls as income rises, because people switch to something better, such as from value-range to premium food.

## Cross elasticity of demand (XED)

**[[cross elasticity of demand|Cross elasticity of demand]]** measures how responsive demand for good A is to a change in the price of good B.

**XED = % change in quantity demanded of A ÷ % change in price of B**

The sign tells you how the goods are related:

- **Positive XED:** **[[substitutes]]**. B gets dearer, demand for A rises. The bigger the number, the closer the substitutes.
- **Negative XED:** **[[complements]]**. B gets dearer, demand for A falls.
- **Zero:** unrelated goods.

## Why elasticity matters

Firms use PED to set prices and YED to plan for booms and recessions. Governments use it to choose what to tax. One limit: estimates come from past data, and "ceteris paribus" rarely holds, so real results can differ.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** A cinema estimates the PED for its tickets at −1.6. Explain the likely effect on its total revenue of cutting prices by 10%. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** PED measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price. A value of −1.6 means demand is price elastic, because its size is greater than 1.

**Application:** A 10% price cut should raise quantity demanded by about 16% (1.6 × 10).

**Analysis:** Because quantity rises by a bigger percentage than price falls, the gain from extra tickets sold outweighs the loss from the lower price on each ticket, so total revenue should rise. For example, 100 tickets at £10 give £1,000, and 116 tickets at £9 give £1,044.
:::

Why it scores: it interprets the number, does the calculation, and explains why revenue rises, with a quick example to prove it.
