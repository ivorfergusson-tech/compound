## The structure

The **[[balance of payments|balance of payments]]** records all transactions between a country and the rest of the world. It has three main parts:

- **Current account:** trade and income flows.
- **Capital account:** small transfers of assets, such as debt forgiveness.
- **Financial account:** flows of investment, such as FDI, portfolio investment and bank loans.

Overall, the accounts must balance. A current account deficit is matched by a surplus on the financial and capital accounts: money flows in from abroad to pay for it.

## The current account

You need to know this in detail. It has four parts.

**1. Trade in goods:** exports minus imports of physical goods. The UK has a large deficit here.

**2. Trade in services:** such as finance, insurance, tourism and education. The UK has a surplus here.

**3. Primary income:** income earned on assets abroad and paid to foreign owners of UK assets, such as interest, dividends and profits, and wages.

**4. Secondary income:** transfers with nothing in return, such as foreign aid and money sent home by migrants.

## Deficit and surplus

- A **current account deficit** means more money flows out through trade and income than flows in.
- A **current account surplus** means the reverse.

In April to June 2026, the UK's deficit, excluding precious metals, was £11.3 billion, or 1.4% of GDP, according to the ONS. The ONS total including precious metals was £19.9 billion, or 2.5% of GDP.

## What influences the balance

- **Productivity:** higher productivity lowers costs, so exports are more competitive.
- **Inflation:** if UK prices rise faster than those abroad, exports fall and imports rise.
- **The exchange rate:** a stronger pound makes exports dearer and imports cheaper.
- **Incomes:** strong UK growth raises imports, and strong growth abroad raises exports.
- **Quality, innovation and non-price factors,** such as design and reliability.
- **Structural factors:** the UK's move away from manufacturing, for example.

## Investment flows

Money flows between countries in two main forms.

**[[foreign direct investment|Foreign direct investment (FDI)]]** is long-term investment in productive assets abroad, such as building a factory or buying a controlling stake in a firm.

**[[portfolio investment|Portfolio investment]]** is buying financial assets, such as shares and bonds, without control. It can move quickly.

Inward FDI can bring jobs, technology and exports, and it can raise capacity. But it also creates future **outflows** of profit on the primary income account. Portfolio flows can leave suddenly, which could cause the currency to fall sharply.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain two factors that could cause the UK's current account deficit to widen. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** A current account deficit means that outflows from trade and income are greater than inflows.

**Application:** The UK has a large trade in goods deficit, and imports a lot of its energy and manufactured goods.

**Analysis:** First, faster growth in UK incomes raises spending on imports, as the UK buys a lot of goods from abroad, and this widens the deficit. Second, if UK inflation is higher than in other countries, UK exports become less competitive in price, so exports fall and buyers switch to imports. Both reduce net exports.
:::

Why it scores: it gives two separate causes, each with a clear chain.
