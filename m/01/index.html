Recap

In the introduction you met two words that matter here: interest (the price of using money) and inflation (prices rising, so £1 buys less). This module shows how they push against each other, and why time is the biggest lever you have.

Before you read

You've saved £1,500 for a car. You put it in a savings account paying 4.5% a year, leave the interest in, and don't touch it for 10 years.

The idea in one sentence

Interest makes money grow, compounding makes that growth speed up over time, and inflation quietly shrinks what the money can buy. What counts is the gap between the two.

Interest: the price of money

Interest is money paid for the use of money. When you put savings in a bank, you're lending to it, so it pays you interest. When you borrow, you pay interest instead.

Banks pay savers because deposits help fund the loans they make, and they charge borrowers more than they pay savers. That gap is a big part of how banks make money (module 8).

The principal is the amount you start with, before any interest: your £1,500. The interest rate is the interest as a percentage of the principal, and it's nearly always per year. So 4.5% on £1,500 is 4.5 ÷ 100 × £1,500 = £67.50 a year.

Where do rates come from? The Bank of England, the UK's central bank, sets Bank Rate. When Bank Rate moves, savings and borrowing rates tend to follow, though not on the same day or by the same amount. Bank Rate is 3.75% (held on 17 September 2026; next decision 5 November 2026). Module 12 covers how and why it's set.

Simple vs compound interest

With simple interest, you earn interest only on the original principal. If the £67.50 is paid out to you each year, you get £67.50 every year, no more.

With compound interest, the interest is added to your balance, and next year you earn interest on the interest too. Most savings accounts work this way.

  • Year 1: 4.5% of £1,500 = £67.50. Balance £1,567.50.
  • Year 2: 4.5% of £1,567.50 = £70.54. Balance £1,638.04.
  • Year 3: 4.5% of £1,638.04 = £73.71. Balance £1,711.75.

Each year's interest is a bit bigger than the last. At first the difference is pennies. Over decades it becomes most of the money. That's why time is the big lever: compounding rewards whoever starts earliest and leaves the money alone.

Interest on interest is not the same as total interest. After three years you've earned £211.75 in total. Simple interest would have paid £67.50 × 3 = £202.50. So the part that came from interest earning interest is just £9.25. That small slice is what grows huge over decades.

Play with the numbers. Try doubling the years and see what happens to the gap between the two lines.

The rule of 72

A handy shortcut: divide 72 by the interest rate to estimate how many years it takes money to double. This is the rule of 72.

  • At 6%: 72 ÷ 6 = 12 years.
  • At 3%: 72 ÷ 3 = 24 years.
  • At 4.5%: 72 ÷ 4.5 = 16 years (the exact answer is 15.75).

Notice that halving the rate doubles the time. A small difference in rate makes a big difference over a working life. It's a good estimate, not a law, and it works best for rates between about 2% and 15%.

Compounding works against borrowers too

Unpaid interest on a debt gets added to what you owe, and then that gets charged interest. The average advertised credit card rate was 35.8% (February 2026). At that rate, a debt left alone doubles in about two years (72 ÷ 36 = 2).

So compounding is a friend when you save and an enemy when you borrow. Module 13 covers debt properly.

Inflation: why £100 today isn't £100 next year

Inflation is the rate at which prices in general rise over time. The UK measures it mainly with CPI, the Consumer Prices Index. Every month the Office for National Statistics checks the prices of about 700 everyday items, and CPI is how much that whole "basket" costs compared with a year earlier.

CPI inflation was 3.5% (12 months to September 2026 (published 21 October 2026)). The Bank of England's target is 2%. It isn't aiming for zero, because a little inflation is healthier than prices falling (deflation): if people expect things to be cheaper next month, they put off spending, businesses sell less and jobs suffer.

Nominal vs real

This is the most important idea in the module.

  • The nominal return is the number on the statement: "4.5%", "£1,567.50".
  • The real return is what you gain after inflation, in spending power: how much more you can actually buy.

As a quick estimate, real return ≈ nominal rate − inflation. Subtract, never add: inflation works against you.

The exact way is:

real return = (1 + nominal rate) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1

Say inflation is 3.5%. £1,500 at 4.5% grows to £1,567.50, but with prices up 3.5% that buys what about £1,514 buys today. Your real gain is about 1%, not 4.5%. In an account paying 1%, you'd have more pounds but less spending power.

Two honest caveats. First, inflation is an average: your own costs (rent, fuel, food) might rise faster or slower. Second, savings interest can be taxed above certain allowances. Most people with modest savings pay none, and module 5 explains the rules.

An analogy: the down escalator

Think of your savings as walking up a down escalator. Interest is your walking speed. Inflation is the escalator moving down underneath you.

  • Walk faster than the escalator (4.5% vs 3.5%) and you slowly climb.
  • Walk at the same speed and you stay put.
  • Stand still (cash at home) and you sink.

Compounding is like getting a little fitter with every step, so you walk faster the longer you keep going.

Where it breaks: a real escalator runs at a fixed speed. Inflation and interest rates both change, sometimes a lot, so the gap can widen or flip. And most easy-access savings rates are a variable rate, so the bank can change them whenever it likes.

Worked example

These numbers assume a 4.5% rate that never changes, which it will. Treat them as a picture of how compounding works, not a forecast.

Back to the question at the start. £1,500 at 4.5% for 10 years:

  • Simple: £1,500 + (£67.50 × 10) = £2,175
  • Compound: £1,500 × 1.045¹⁰ = £2,329.45

If you guessed around £2,175, you used simple interest; compounding added another £154. If you guessed lower, you probably applied the 4.5% only once.

The gap grows with time.

Years Simple Compound From compounding
1 £1,568 £1,568 £0
5 £1,838 £1,869 £32
10 £2,175 £2,329 £154
20 £2,850 £3,618 £768
40 £4,200 £8,725 £4,525

After 40 years, more than half the compound pot is interest earned on interest.

Starting at 18 vs 28. The same £1,500 at 4.5%, left until age 58:

  • Start at 18 (40 years): £8,725
  • Start at 28 (30 years): £5,618

Waiting 10 years costs about £3,100, with the same money and the same rate. Starting late doesn't just lose 10 years at the start; it chops off the 10 most valuable years at the end, when the pot is biggest and growing fastest.

The common mistake

"My balance went up, so I'm better off."

It feels true because the number on the screen is bigger. But what matters is what the money can buy. If your account pays 1% and prices rise 3.5%, your balance goes up and your spending power goes down. Lots of people leave savings in low-rate accounts for years without noticing, because the nominal number never falls. Always compare your rate with inflation.

In real life

  • Check your rate against inflation. If your savings rate is below CPI (3.5%), your money is losing spending power.
  • Time beats amount early on. Small sums started young can grow larger than bigger sums started late.
  • Rates are usually variable. Top easy-access rates are about 4.5–5.0% AER (late October 2026; several only with a temporary bonus). When a bonus ends, the rate drops.
  • Compounding cuts both ways. A credit card at around 36% compounds against you about eight times faster than 4.5% compounds for you.
  • Red flag: anyone promising high, guaranteed returns well above savings rates. Real returns come with risk (modules 2, 3 and 10).

Think about it

Why does the Bank of England aim for 2% inflation rather than 0%?

Falling prices sound good but can be harmful. If people expect things to be cheaper next month, they delay spending, businesses sell less, and jobs suffer. Debts also get heavier in real terms. A small, steady rise keeps things moving and gives the Bank room to cut rates in a slowdown. Module 12 goes further.

If inflation jumped to 6% while your savings rate stayed at 4.5%, who wins and who loses?

Your real return turns negative, at roughly −1.4%. Savers lose spending power, while people with fixed-rate debts quietly gain, because the pounds they repay are worth less. That's partly why central banks raise rates when inflation rises.

Try it

Find your own real return.

  1. Open your banking app and find what your savings (or current account) pay. Look for AER and whether the rate is variable.
  2. Work out your real return: your rate − 3.5%. Negative means you're losing spending power.
  3. Note any bonus that runs out or age limit. Module 9 shows how to pick a better account.
