## Guess first

Imagine you're Chancellor and want to cut poverty.

:::guess {"prompt":"Would you give people more money, change the tax system, or try something else? Who might object, and why?","kind":"text","id":"econ-aqa:4.1.7.3-guess"}
:::

:::callout idea
Governments can redistribute through taxes, benefits, public services and labour market rules, but each policy has costs and side effects, and the choice depends on moral and political views.
:::

## The main policies

**Taxes**

- **Progressive income tax:** a rising share of income is taken as income rises, which narrows the gap.
- **Taxes on wealth:** such as inheritance tax and capital gains tax.
- **Raising the tax-free allowance:** this helps those on low pay by taking more income out of tax.

**Benefits and transfers**

- **Means-tested benefits:** paid to those on low incomes, such as Universal Credit.
- **Universal benefits:** paid to everyone in a group, such as the state pension.

**Public services**

- Free or subsidised health care and education, which matter most to those on low incomes, and which can break the cycle of poverty.

**Labour market and supply-side policies**

- National minimum wage.
- Education and training, which raise skills and wages.
- Help getting into work, and laws against discrimination.

**Wealth and housing**

- Pension schemes and help to save.
- Building more affordable homes.

## Economic consequences

| Policy | Benefit | Possible cost |
|---|---|---|
| Higher income tax on high earners | Narrows the gap and raises revenue | Weakens incentives, and encourages tax avoidance or moving abroad |
| Means-tested benefits | Targeted at those in need | Unemployment and poverty traps, low take-up, admin costs |
| Universal benefits | Simple, high take-up, no stigma | Costly, and also go to people who don't need them |
| Minimum wage | Raises low pay | Possible job losses, misses those out of work |
| Education and training | Tackles causes, and adds to growth | Slow, and costly at the start |

## Traps and incentives

Redistribution can change behaviour.

**[[poverty trap|The poverty trap]]:** when a low-paid worker earns more, they pay more tax and lose benefits, so they keep little of the rise. There's little reason to work more.

**[[unemployment trap|The unemployment trap]]:** when benefits are close to what a person could earn in a low-paid job, there's little reason to take the job.

## Moral and political perspectives

There's no purely economic answer to how much to redistribute.

- Some people stress **equality of outcome** and social justice. They argue that wealth owes much to luck and inheritance.
- Others stress **equality of opportunity**. They'd focus on education and removing barriers, and accept unequal results.
- Others stress **freedom and incentives**. They argue that people should keep what they earn and that growth does more for the poor than redistribution.

Each view is a value judgement. Economics can show the likely trade-offs, but it can't tell you which outcome is right.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain one economic disadvantage of raising income tax on high earners to reduce inequality. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** A progressive income tax takes a rising share of income as income rises, so it can narrow the gap between high and low earners.

**Application:** If the top rate of income tax rose, high earners would keep less of each extra pound.

**Analysis:** This weakens the reward for working more, taking on responsibility or starting a business. Some may work less, avoid the tax, or move abroad, particularly if they're mobile. This could reduce the supply of skilled labour and enterprise, which lowers productive potential. It could also raise less revenue than expected, so there's less to spend on helping the poor.
:::

Why it scores: it explains how the incentive changes and follows it through to the economy and to tax revenue.
