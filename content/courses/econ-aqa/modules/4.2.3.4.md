## Output gaps and policy conflicts

Output gaps link unemployment and inflation.

- **Positive output gap:** low unemployment and upward pressure on inflation.
- **Negative output gap:** high unemployment and weak inflation.

So a government that pushes AD higher to cut unemployment risks more inflation. This is the conflict you met in 4.2.1.

## The short-run Phillips curve

In 1958, A.W. Phillips showed that, in UK data, there was an inverse relationship between unemployment and the rate of change of wages. Economists adapted this to a link between unemployment and **inflation**.

The **[[short-run phillips curve|short-run Phillips curve (SRPC)]]** slopes down. It suggests a **trade-off**: lower unemployment comes with higher inflation. The reason is that as unemployment falls, labour gets scarcer, workers can win higher pay, and firms raise prices.

**The policy implication:** governments can choose where to be on the curve, using demand-side policy.

The stagflation of the 1970s, when inflation and unemployment both rose, showed that this trade-off isn't stable.

## The long-run Phillips curve

Milton Friedman argued that the trade-off only lasts as long as people are fooled.

1. The economy is at the natural rate of unemployment, and people expect 2% inflation.
2. The government raises AD. Unemployment falls, and inflation rises.
3. Workers see that real wages have fallen, so they demand more pay. Firms' costs rise.
4. Unemployment returns to the natural rate, but with higher inflation.
5. People now expect higher inflation, so the **short-run curve shifts up**.

So in the long run, there's no trade-off. The **[[long-run phillips curve|long-run Phillips curve (LRPC)]]** is **vertical** at the natural rate of unemployment. The AQA spec also calls this the **L-shaped** Phillips curve.

Try it: lower unemployment, then raise expected inflation.

**The policy implication:** demand-side policy can't keep unemployment below the natural rate, without inflation rising. To lower unemployment for good, you have to lower the natural rate, using **supply-side policies**.

## Reconciling the conflicts

Policy can try to ease conflicts, in the short run and the long run.

**In the short run:**

- **Choosing the policy mix:** using different tools for different goals. Monetary policy can target inflation, while targeted spending helps those out of work.
- **Credible targets:** an independent central bank can keep inflation expectations low, so that wage demands stay modest. This makes the short-run trade-off better.
- **Accepting a temporary cost:** tolerating some inflation after a supply shock, to avoid a deep recession.

**In the long run:**

- **Supply-side policies** shift LRAS right. Output grows without pushing up prices, and the natural rate of unemployment falls.

So the **monetarist and supply-side view** is that the main objectives are **compatible in the long run**: stable prices give firms the confidence to invest, which supports growth and jobs.

Keynesians are less sure. They point out that supply-side policies take time, that demand can stay low for years and that long-term unemployment can itself raise the natural rate.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain why a government may not be able to reduce unemployment below the natural rate in the long run. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** The natural rate of unemployment is the rate at which the labour market is in equilibrium. The long-run Phillips curve is vertical at this rate.

**Application:** Suppose the government raises AD when unemployment is at its natural rate.

**Analysis:** In the short run, unemployment falls along the short-run Phillips curve, and inflation rises. Workers soon see that their real wages have fallen, so they demand higher pay. Firms' costs rise, so they cut back on hiring, and unemployment returns to the natural rate. Inflation is now higher and so are expectations, so the short-run curve has shifted up. There's no lasting gain.
:::

Why it scores: it follows the expectations chain step by step to the long-run result.
