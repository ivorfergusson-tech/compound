## The main functions of a central bank

The **[[central bank|central bank]]** of the UK is the **Bank of England**. Its main functions are to:

- Carry out **monetary policy**.
- Issue banknotes.
- Act as banker to the government and to commercial banks.
- Act as **lender of last resort**: lending to banks in a crisis.
- Help to keep the financial system stable, which you'll see in 4.2.4.4.

## What monetary policy is

**[[monetary policy|Monetary policy]]** is action by the central bank to influence interest rates, the supply of money and credit and the exchange rate, in order to meet the government's objectives.

## The objectives

The government sets the objectives. The main one is **price stability**: a target of **2% CPI inflation**. Subject to that, the Bank must support the government's economic policy, including growth and employment.

The Bank has been independent in setting interest rates since 1997. The aim is to take short-term politics out of the decision, and to make the target more credible.

## The Monetary Policy Committee

The **[[monetary policy committee|Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)]]** has nine members and sets **[[bank rate|Bank Rate]]**, the interest rate that the Bank pays on money that commercial banks hold with it. It influences other interest rates across the economy.

Each member has one vote, and the majority decides. In September 2026, the MPC voted 6 to 3 to hold Bank Rate at **3.75%**, with inflation at 3.5%. The next decision was due on 5 November 2026, so check the latest rate.

## What the MPC considers

Changes in Bank Rate take time to work, up to about two years. So the MPC has to look **ahead**, using forecasts. It considers factors such as:

- **Wages and earnings,** which feed into costs.
- **Unemployment and vacancies,** which show how tight the labour market is.
- **Output gap and GDP growth.**
- **Consumer spending and confidence.**
- **House prices and credit.**
- **Commodity prices,** such as oil and gas.
- **The exchange rate,** which affects import prices.
- **Inflation expectations.**
- **Global growth and interest rates abroad.**

If it expects inflation to rise above the target, it's likely to raise Bank Rate, or hold it. If it expects inflation to fall below target, or the economy looks weak, it's likely to cut.

Cost-push shocks are especially hard. When a rise in energy prices pushes inflation up while growth is weak, a rise in rates can hurt output, without changing the world price of gas.

## How the Bank can influence the money supply

The Bank can influence the growth of the money supply in several ways:

- **Bank Rate:** higher rates make borrowing dearer, so banks create less new credit.
- **Asset purchases (quantitative easing):** buying bonds adds new money to the economy, as the next lesson shows.
- **Rules on banks:** capital and liquidity requirements limit lending.

It doesn't control the money supply directly. Most money is created by commercial banks when they lend, and it depends on the demand for loans.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain two factors the MPC may consider when deciding whether to raise Bank Rate. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** The MPC sets Bank Rate to achieve the government's target of 2% CPI inflation.

**Application:** In September 2026, CPI inflation was 3.5%, above the target.

**Analysis:** First, wage growth: if pay is rising faster than productivity, firms' costs rise, and spending power grows, so inflation is likely to stay high, which supports a rate rise. Second, the output gap: if the economy is weak, with a negative output gap, inflation pressure may fade by itself, so raising rates could cause needless harm. Together these show whether inflation is likely to be above the target in two years' time.
:::
