## Recap

Module 5 showed what comes off a payslip, module 9 how to build a [[budget]], and module 13 how borrowing works. This module puts them together for one of the biggest crossroads: leaving school or college. Work, an apprenticeship or university? And what does it cost to live on your own?

There's no single right answer, and money is only one part of the decision. But it's a part people often guess at. This module gives you the real numbers.

## Before you read

## Three routes, three money stories

**Straight into work.** You earn from day one, but starting pay is often lower and progression can depend on the job. No student debt.

**An [[apprentice wage|apprenticeship]].** You work and train at the same time, often towards a qualification up to degree level (a degree apprenticeship), with **no tuition fees**: the employer and government pay for the training. You're paid a wage from day one.

**University.** Tuition fees and living costs are usually funded by **student loans**. You earn little while studying, but graduates in many fields go on to earn more on average. The gap varies hugely by subject and university.

## Pay: the legal minimums

From 1 April 2026 the minimum hourly rates are:

- 21 and over (National Living Wage): {{fig:nlw}}
- 18 to 20: {{fig:nmw1820}}
- Under 18: {{fig:nmwUnder18}}
- **Apprentice rate:** {{fig:nmwApprentice+}}. After that, apprentices must get at least the rate for their age.

At the apprentice rate, 37.5 hours a week is **£300 a week, about £15,600 a year**. Many employers pay apprentices well above the minimum, especially for degree apprenticeships, so always check the advertised wage. At the National Living Wage, the same hours come to about **£24,785 a year**.

Use the take-home calculator from module 5 for any salary. At £15,600 you'd pay about £606 in income tax and £242 in NI a year, so you'd take home about £1,229 a month.

## Student loans in England: how they really work

If you study in England, you can usually borrow:

- a **tuition fee loan**, paid straight to the university: the fee cap is {{fig:tuitionFee+}};
- a **maintenance loan** for living costs, which depends on household income and where you live: up to {{fig:maintenanceLoan+}}.

Courses starting from August 2023 use **[[student loan plans|Plan 5]]**. The rules:

- You start repaying the April after you leave your course.
- You repay **9% of your income above** {{fig:plan5+}}. Below the threshold, you repay nothing.
- Repayments come out of your pay through PAYE, like tax.
- **Interest** is set at RPI inflation: {{fig:plan5Interest+}}.
- Anything left after **40 years** is **written off**.

(Older loans are on **Plan 2**, with a threshold of {{fig:plan2}} and interest of up to RPI + 3%, written off after 30 years. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own systems and plans.)

Back to the question at the start. At £30,000 you repay 9% × (£30,000 − £25,000) = **£450 a year, £37.50 a month**. It doesn't matter whether you borrowed £30,000 or £60,000: the repayment depends only on your income.

## So is it really a debt?

Because repayments depend on income and the rest is written off, a Plan 5 loan behaves differently from a credit card or a mortgage:

- **It doesn't affect your credit file** the way other debts do, and you can't be chased for it if you earn below the threshold.
- **Earn less, repay less.** Career breaks, part-time work or lower pay mean lower repayments.
- **Higher earners repay it all**, and so pay back more in total.
- **Mortgage lenders do count the repayment** as an outgoing when working out what you can afford, because it reduces your take-home pay.

Many people think of it as a **graduate contribution**: an extra 9% on earnings above £25,000, for as long as it takes to clear, up to 40 years. Because Plan 5 interest only matches inflation, the balance doesn't grow in real terms the way older loans could.

## Living on your own: the real costs

Whatever route you take, leaving home brings new [[bills]]. The main ones:

**[[rent|Rent]].** The biggest cost for most people. The average UK private rent is {{fig:rentUK+}}, though that's for whole properties; renting a room in a shared house is usually much cheaper. In England, when you rent:

- a **holding deposit** is capped at one week's rent;
- the **tenancy deposit** is capped at five weeks' rent (when yearly rent is under £50,000), and the landlord must protect it in a government-approved scheme;
- most other fees, like "admin fees", are banned.

**[[council tax|Council tax]].** Paid to your local council. The average Band D bill in England is {{fig:councilTax+}}, though most homes are in lower bands. If you live alone you get a **25% discount**. Full-time students are usually exempt; apprentices on low pay may be too.

**Energy.** Gas and electricity. The price cap for a typical home is {{fig:energyCap+}}. Shared houses split it.

**Other bills.** Water (often included in rent for rooms), broadband, phone, a TV licence if you watch live TV or BBC iPlayer, contents insurance, and travel.

Put each bill in your budget as a monthly cost. Divide yearly bills by 12, and build up a small pot for one-off costs like deposits and furniture.

## Comparing routes fairly

When you compare, look at more than year-one pay:

- **Total cost of each route**: fees, living costs, lost earnings while studying.
- **Likely pay after 3, 5 and 10 years** in the career you want, not averages for all graduates.
- **Qualifications at the end**: some careers need a degree; many don't; degree apprenticeships give both.
- **Where you'd live**: rent in your city can matter more than the salary difference.
- **What you'd enjoy and stick at**: the route you'll finish beats the one that looks best on paper.

Money is a big factor, but not the only one. The aim is to decide with real numbers, not guesses.

## The common mistake

**"I'll owe £60,000, so I'll be paying it off like a mortgage."**

Plan 5 repayments are set by your **income**, not your **balance**. Two graduates on the same salary repay the same each month, whether they owe £40,000 or £70,000. And whatever is left after 40 years is written off. That doesn't make the loan free (higher earners repay a lot), but it does mean the balance figure on your statement isn't the scary monthly bill people imagine.

## In real life

- **Look up real pay** for the jobs and apprenticeships you're considering, not just minimums.
- **Apply for student finance** early; maintenance loans depend on household income, and there may be grants or bursaries from universities.
- **Budget for your first months**: deposits, the first month's rent, furniture and travel often come before the first payday or loan payment.
- **Check council tax discounts and exemptions** as soon as you move.
- **Red flag:** letting agents demanding banned fees, or landlords not protecting your deposit. Your local council and Shelter can help.

## Think about it

:::reveal Why might two graduates with the same degree end up repaying very different amounts?
Repayments depend on income over many years. One might earn far more, repay in full and pay back more in total; the other might take career breaks, earn less, repay less and have the rest written off. The amount borrowed matters less than lifetime earnings.
:::

:::reveal Is an apprenticeship always better financially because there's no debt?
Not always. It avoids fees and pays a wage, which is a big head start. But long-term earnings depend on the career. Some professions require a degree; in others, a degree apprenticeship leads to the same place debt-free. Compare the specific jobs, not the routes in general.
:::

2. Use the take-home calculator (module 5) on each, with Plan 5 switched on for the university route.
