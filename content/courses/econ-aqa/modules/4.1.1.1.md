## Economics as a social science

Economics studies how people, firms and governments use scarce resources to meet their needs and wants. Because it studies **people**, it's a [[social science]], like psychology or sociology.

Economists try to work like natural scientists:

1. **Observe** something, for example that prices rose faster this year.
2. **Build a theory or [[economic model]]** that explains it: a simplified picture of the real world.
3. **Make a prediction** from the model ("if interest rates rise, spending will fall").
4. **Test it** against data, then keep, change or drop the theory.

## Why it isn't like physics

The method is similar, but there are big differences:

- **No laboratory.** A chemist can repeat an experiment a hundred times. An economist can't raise income tax in the UK twice to compare. Most evidence comes from what already happened, where many things changed at once.
- **[[ceteris paribus|Ceteris paribus]].** Because the real world won't hold still, economists assume "all other things stay the same" while they look at one change. It makes models usable, but real results can differ.
- **People react and learn.** A ball doesn't change how it falls because you predicted it. People do change what they spend when they hear a forecast, so predictions can change the outcome.
- **Models simplify.** Many models assume people are rational and well informed. Real people often aren't (you'll meet this in behavioural economics, 4.1.2).

So economic predictions are usually tendencies ("more likely", "on average"), not laws like gravity.

## Positive and normative statements

This is the part of the topic that's examined most often.

- A **[[positive statement]]** is about **what is, was or will be**. It can be tested against evidence, even if it turns out to be **wrong**. "UK unemployment rose last year." "A tax on sugary drinks will cut how much people buy."
- A **[[normative statement]]** is about **what ought to be**. It includes a **[[value judgement]]**: an opinion about what is good or fair. It can't be proved true or false. "The government should tax sugary drinks." "Inequality in the UK is too high."

Watch out for these traps:

- A positive statement **can be false**. "Raising the minimum wage will cause millions of job losses" is positive: it's a claim you could check.
- Look for words like **should**, **ought**, **fair**, **too high**, **better**, **unacceptable**. They usually signal a normative statement.
- Numbers don't make a statement positive. "A 2% inflation target is the right level" is normative.

## Value judgements and policy

Economics can tell a government what is **likely to happen** if it does something: the positive part. It can't tell it what it **should** do: that needs value judgements.

Take a sugar tax. Economists can estimate how much less sugar people buy, how much tax is raised and who pays most of it (positive). Whether that's worth it depends on views about health, personal freedom and fairness to poorer households, who spend more of their income on food (normative).

So people who agree on the evidence can still disagree on the best policy, because they weigh the results with different **moral and political** views. That's why economists often disagree in public: sometimes about the evidence, often about the values.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain the difference between a positive and a normative statement, using an example of each. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** A positive statement is objective: it describes what is or will be and can be tested against evidence. A normative statement is subjective: it contains a value judgement about what ought to be.

**Application:** "The UK's sugar tax has reduced the amount of sugar in soft drinks" is positive, because it could be checked with sales and recipe data. "The government should tax sugary food as well" is normative, because "should" shows an opinion.

**Analysis:** The difference matters because evidence can settle a positive disagreement, but a normative one depends on values, so it can't be settled by data alone.
:::
