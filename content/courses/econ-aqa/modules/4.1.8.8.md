## Guess first

Your train and your water bill both come from services that have moved between public and private hands.

:::guess {"prompt":"Should the state or private firms run services like these? Why?","kind":"text","id":"econ-aqa:4.1.8.8-guess"}
:::

:::callout idea
Who owns an industry and how it's regulated both matter: state ownership can serve social goals, private ownership can bring efficiency, and neither guarantees a good outcome.
:::

## Public ownership

**[[public ownership|Public ownership]]** means the state owns a firm or industry. **Nationalisation** is the transfer of a firm from private to state ownership.

**Arguments for:**

- **Natural monopolies:** a single network may be best run for the public, not for profit.
- **Social objectives:** it can keep unprofitable but useful services running.
- **Externalities:** it can take wider costs and benefits into account.
- **Strategic industries:** it can protect key services in a crisis.

**Arguments against:**

- **Weak incentives:** with no profit motive and no threat of failure, costs can rise.
- **Political interference:** short-term politics can affect long-term decisions.
- **Cost to taxpayers:** buying firms and funding losses has an opportunity cost.
- **Less choice** for consumers.

## Privatisation

**[[privatisation|Privatisation]]** is the transfer of state-owned assets to the private sector. The UK privatised many industries from the 1980s, including telecoms, gas, electricity, water and rail.

**Arguments for:**

- **Efficiency:** the profit motive and shareholders push firms to cut costs.
- **Competition,** if the market is opened up, which can lower prices and improve choice.
- **Investment:** firms can raise funds from the market.
- **Government revenue** from sales of assets.

**Arguments against:**

- **Private monopolies:** a public monopoly may simply become a private one.
- **Short-term focus** on shareholders over long-term investment.
- **Loss of social objectives,** such as unprofitable services.
- **Selling assets** gives a one-off gain, and the future profits are lost.

## Regulation

**[[regulation|Regulation]]** means rules that control how firms behave. UK examples include Ofgem for energy, Ofcom for communications and Ofwat for water.

Regulators can use:

- **Price caps,** which limit price rises and encourage firms to cut costs.
- **Quality standards,** to make sure that firms don't cut corners.
- **Performance targets,** with fines for failing to meet them.

**Arguments for:** it protects consumers from monopoly power, sets safety and environmental standards and corrects market failure without public ownership.

**Arguments against:** it's costly to run and to comply with, regulators may lack information, and rules can reduce competition and innovation.

## Regulatory capture

**[[regulatory capture|Regulatory capture]]** happens when a regulator ends up serving the interests of the firms it regulates, not the public. This can happen because:

- Firms know much more about their costs than the regulator does.
- Firms can lobby hard, while consumers are poorly organised.
- Staff may move between the regulator and the firms.

## Deregulation

**[[deregulation|Deregulation]]** means removing rules, to open up markets to competition.

**Arguments for:** lower barriers to entry, more competition, lower costs for firms and more innovation.

**Arguments against:** firms may cut safety or quality, and lightly regulated markets can take on too much risk. Many people link this to the 2007 to 2008 financial crisis.

## The UK today

These debates are live ones.

- **Rail:** the Passenger Railway Services (Public Ownership) Act 2024 allows train operators to move into public ownership as their contracts end. South Western Railway was the first, in May 2025.
- **Water:** after an independent review led by Sir Jon Cunliffe, the government said in 2025 that it would abolish Ofwat and create a new regulator. This followed concerns about sewage, investment and debt in a privatised industry.

Assess each case on its effects on prices, quality, investment and efficiency. Ownership alone doesn't decide the result. Competition and good regulation often matter as much.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain one argument for and one argument against the privatisation of a state-owned industry. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Privatisation is the transfer of assets from the public sector to the private sector.

**Application:** The UK privatised its water industry in England and Wales, as well as telecoms, gas and electricity.

**Analysis:** For: private owners want profit and answer to shareholders, so firms have a stronger incentive to cut costs, which raises productive efficiency. Against: a natural monopoly such as water still has no direct competition, so a private owner could raise prices or spend too little on investment. So privatisation needs strong regulation if consumers are to gain.
:::

Why it scores: it gives one clear chain each way, and it uses a real industry.
