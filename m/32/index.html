Before you read

In 2008, governments around the world, including the UK's, spent huge sums rescuing banks.

The idea in one sentence

Finance is regulated because one firm's failure can spread through the whole system and hurt everyone; the 2008 crisis showed how, and the rules since (more capital, ring-fencing, closer supervision) aim to make banks safer and failures less contagious, so taxpayers don't have to step in again.

Systemic risk

Most businesses can fail without hurting the rest of the economy. Banks are different, because of systemic risk: the danger that trouble at one firm spreads to others and to the whole economy.

  • Banks lend to each other, so one failure can leave others with losses.
  • Fear spreads: if one bank fails, depositors and lenders may pull money from others (module 8).
  • Banks run the payments system: if they stop, wages, shops and bills stop too.
  • When banks cut lending to protect themselves, businesses and homebuyers can't borrow, and the economy shrinks.

Back to the question at the start: governments rescue banks because letting a big one collapse could crash the payments system, savings and lending for everyone. That's also the problem: if banks expect rescues, they may take more risk (moral hazard). Regulation tries to make rescues unnecessary.

2008: how it happened

A simplified chain of events:

  1. Cheap credit and a housing boom. In the US especially, lenders gave mortgages to borrowers who couldn't really afford them ("subprime"), assuming house prices would keep rising.
  2. Packaging the loans. Banks bundled thousands of mortgages into complex bonds and sold them worldwide. Many were rated AAA (module 24), and investors trusted the ratings.
  3. Leverage everywhere. Banks held these assets with very little capital and lots of borrowed money, often short-term. Derivatives like credit default swaps spread the risk in ways nobody could see (module 28).
  4. The turn. US house prices fell, borrowers defaulted, and the "safe" bonds collapsed in value. Nobody knew which banks held the losses, so banks stopped lending to each other.
  5. Runs and failures. In the UK, Northern Rock suffered the first bank run in a generation in September 2007. In September 2008 the US investment bank Lehman Brothers failed, and markets froze worldwide.
  6. Rescues. The UK government took large stakes in RBS and Lloyds and guaranteed or lent huge sums. The final cost to taxpayers was about £33 billion (Office for Budget Responsibility estimate, October 2021 (a £35.5 billion loss on RBS, now NatWest, offset by gains elsewhere); total support at the peak was about £850 billion including guarantees (National Audit Office, 2009)).

The recession that followed was the deepest in the UK for decades, and many people's jobs, wages and savings suffered for years.

What changed after 2008

New UK regulators. On 1 April 2013 the Financial Services Authority was replaced by two bodies:

  • the PRA (Prudential Regulation Authority), part of the Bank of England, which supervises the safety and soundness of banks, building societies and insurers;
  • the FCA (Financial Conduct Authority), which regulates conduct: how firms treat customers and whether markets are fair. In 2023 it added the Consumer Duty, requiring firms to deliver good outcomes for retail customers.

Basel rules. International standards (Basel III, agreed after the crisis) made banks hold more and better capital (their own money to absorb losses) and enough liquid assets to survive a run. Big, systemically important banks must hold extra.

Ring-fencing. UK banks with more than £35 billion (ring-fencing has applied since 1 January 2019 (the threshold started at £25 billion and has since been raised; banks in scope as of April 2026: Barclays, HSBC, Lloyds, NatWest and Santander UK; further changes are before Parliament)) of core deposits must keep everyday banking (deposits, payments, loans to households and small firms) separate from riskier investment banking, so a trading disaster can't sink the bank where people keep their wages.

Making failure survivable. Banks must plan how they could fail without a taxpayer bailout ("resolution"), with losses falling on shareholders and certain bondholders first ("bail-in").

Personal accountability. Senior managers at banks and other firms are now personally responsible for areas they run.

Critics argue rules are now too heavy and hold back growth; others say risks have just moved to less-regulated corners like private credit. Debates about the right balance continue, and every regulation you've met in this course is part of it.

Your stock pitch

A stock pitch is a short, structured argument for (or against) buying a company's shares. It's a classic finance interview task and a great test of everything in this course. Keep it to about two minutes spoken, or one page.

The standard structure:

  1. Recommendation: buy, sell or hold, and the company in one line.
  2. The business: what it does, how it makes money, who its customers and competitors are.
  3. Your thesis: two or three reasons the market is missing or under-rating. Specific, not "it's a great brand".
  4. Valuation: P/E, EV/EBITDA against peers, or a simple DCF (module 22). What's it worth, compared with the price?
  5. Risks: what would make you wrong, and how likely is it?
  6. Catalysts: what event could make the market see what you see (results, a new product, a deal)?

Pick a company you can understand: one whose products you use or whose annual report you read in module 18. Use real, sourced numbers. And remember module 14: even good pitches are often wrong, which is why most people's own money belongs in diversified funds.

The common mistake

"Regulation is just red tape that stops banks doing business."

Some rules do add cost, and getting the balance right matters. But the 2008 crisis showed what happens without enough: millions of people who'd never taken a financial risk lost jobs and homes, and taxpayers paid for the rescues. Good regulation is what lets ordinary people trust banks with their wages and savings.

In real life

  • Check firms on the FCA register and use the Financial Ombudsman if things go wrong.
  • Stay within the FSCS limit per banking licence.
  • Practise your stock pitch out loud and time it.
  • Follow the debate: regulation changes after every crisis, and the next one will bring new rules.
  • Red flag: any product or firm that's unregulated but sounds like a bank or an investment. Unregulated means no FSCS, no Ombudsman, and often no way to get your money back.

Think about it

What is moral hazard, and how do the post-2008 rules try to reduce it?

Moral hazard is taking more risk because someone else will bear the cost if it goes wrong, like banks expecting bailouts. Bail-in rules, resolution plans and personal accountability for senior managers aim to make shareholders, bondholders and managers bear losses, not taxpayers.

Why might risk move to less-regulated parts of finance after tighter bank rules?

When banks face higher capital requirements, some lending moves to funds and other non-banks with lighter rules (often called shadow banking or private credit). The risk doesn't disappear; it moves somewhere regulators may see less clearly, which is why regulators now watch non-banks too.
