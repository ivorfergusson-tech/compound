:::callout idea
Price elasticity of supply measures how quickly and how much firms can raise output when the price rises, which depends mostly on time and on how easily production can be expanded.
:::

## Calculating PES

**[[price elasticity of supply|Price elasticity of supply]]** (PES) measures how responsive quantity supplied is to a change in price.

**PES = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price**

If price rises 10% and quantity supplied rises 5%, PES = 5 ÷ 10 = **0.5**.

PES is normally **positive**, because price and quantity supplied move in the same direction.

| PES | Name | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| More than 1 | **Elastic** | Supply responds by a bigger % than price |
| Less than 1 | **Inelastic** | Supply responds by a smaller % than price |
| Exactly 1 | Unit elastic | Same % change |
| 0 | Perfectly inelastic | Quantity can't change, as with seats at a sold-out match |
| Infinity | Perfectly elastic | Any quantity is supplied at one price |

## What influences PES?

- **Time:** the most important factor. In the **short run**, firms can only use the resources they have, so supply is inelastic. In the **long run**, they can build factories and train staff, so it's more elastic.
- **Spare capacity:** if machines are idle and staff are available, output can rise quickly.
- **Stocks:** firms with goods in storage can respond at once. Fresh food can't be stored for long.
- **Length of production:** strawberries take months to grow, while a factory can add another shift.
- **Mobility of factors:** if resources can easily switch from another use, supply is more elastic.
- **Barriers to entry:** if new firms can join easily, supply is more elastic.

## Why PES matters

PES affects what happens to prices when demand changes.

- If supply is **inelastic**, a rise in demand mostly raises the **price**. This helps explain high rents and house prices: housing can't be built quickly.
- If supply is **elastic**, a rise in demand mostly raises the **quantity**, with little change in price.

Governments and firms care too. If a market's supply is inelastic, a subsidy may push prices up more than it raises output.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain why the supply of new housing in the UK is likely to be price inelastic in the short run. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Price elasticity of supply measures the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price. Supply is inelastic if quantity changes by a smaller percentage than price.

**Application:** Building houses takes a long time, needs land with planning permission, and depends on skilled workers.

**Analysis:** Even if house prices rise, builders cannot quickly increase output, as they have to find land, gain permission and hire workers. So the percentage rise in the number of new homes in the short run is smaller than the percentage rise in price. Given more time, more land and workers can be found, so supply becomes more elastic.
:::

Why it scores: it defines the term, applies specific features of housing, links them to why output can't rise quickly, and points out the importance of time.
