## Guess first

A café owner is deciding whether to hire one more barista at £90 a day.

:::guess {"prompt":"What would the owner need to know to make that decision? When would hiring them be a mistake?","kind":"text","id":"econ-aqa:4.1.6.1-guess"}
:::

:::callout idea
Firms want workers for what they produce, so the demand for labour is derived from the demand for the product, and a firm hires up to the point where the last worker adds as much revenue as they cost.
:::

## Derived demand

Nobody hires a worker for the sake of it. A firm wants labour because of the goods and services labour helps to make. So the demand for labour is **[[derived demand]]**: it comes from the demand for the product.

If demand for restaurant meals rises, restaurants want more chefs. If demand for a product falls, so does the demand for the people who make it.

## Marginal productivity theory

Marginal productivity theory says that firms hire workers up to the point where the extra revenue from the last worker equals the wage.

Two ideas build this up.

- **Marginal physical product (MPP):** the extra output from one more worker.
- **[[marginal revenue product]] (MRP):** the extra revenue from one more worker.

**MRP = MPP × MR**

For a firm in a competitive product market, MR is the price of the product.

| Worker | MPP (units) | Price | MRP |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1st | 20 | £5 | £100 |
| 2nd | 18 | £5 | £90 |
| 3rd | 15 | £5 | £75 |
| 4th | 10 | £5 | £50 |

If the wage is £75, the firm hires 3 workers. The 4th would cost £75 and add only £50.

MPP falls because of the **law of diminishing returns**: with capital fixed, extra workers eventually add less.

## The demand curve for labour

The demand curve for labour shows the relationship between the wage rate and the number of workers employed. It's the **MRP curve**, and it slopes down since MRP falls as more workers are hired.

- A **higher wage** means fewer workers: a movement along the curve.
- A change in anything else **shifts** the curve.

## Shifts in the demand for labour

The curve shifts right when:

- **Demand for the product rises,** so its price rises and MRP rises.
- **Labour productivity rises,** through better training or better equipment.
- **The price of capital rises,** if capital and labour are substitutes.

It shifts left when the reverse happens, or when new technology replaces workers.

Capital can work in both directions. A new machine can replace workers, or it can make them more productive and raise demand for them.

## The elasticity of demand for labour

This shows how much employment changes when the wage changes. Demand for labour is more elastic when:

- **Labour is easy to replace** with capital or with other workers.
- **Demand for the product is elastic,** so a wage-driven price rise cuts sales by a lot.
- **Wages are a large share of total costs,** so a rise matters more.
- **It's the long run,** which gives firms time to change their methods.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain why the demand curve for labour slopes downwards. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** The demand curve for labour is the marginal revenue product curve. MRP is the extra revenue from employing one more worker.

**Application:** In a factory with a fixed number of machines, each extra worker has less equipment to use.

**Analysis:** Because of the law of diminishing returns, the marginal physical product of each extra worker falls in the short run, so MRP falls too. A firm will only hire another worker if their MRP is at least equal to the wage. So at a lower wage, more workers are worth hiring, and at a higher wage fewer are, which gives a downward-sloping curve.
:::

Why it scores: it links diminishing returns to MRP, and MRP to the hiring decision.
