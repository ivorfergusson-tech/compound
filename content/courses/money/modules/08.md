## The business model: the gap

A bank's core business is simple:

- It **takes [[deposit|deposits]]** from savers and pays them interest (often less than Bank Rate).
- It makes **[[lending|loans]]** (mortgages, business loans, overdrafts, cards) and charges interest (more than Bank Rate).

The difference between the interest it earns and the interest it pays is **net interest income**. As a percentage of what it lends, that's the **[[net interest margin]]** (NIM).

Say a bank holds £1,000 million of deposits, paying savers 2.5%, and lends the same amount at 6%. It earns £60m and pays £25m, so net interest income is **£35m**: a margin of 3.5 percentage points (in this simplified example). Real UK bank margins are smaller, because much lending is low-margin mortgages.

Out of that margin the bank must pay for staff, branches, technology, **bad debts** (loans that aren't repaid) and tax. What's left is profit. Banks also earn **fees**: account charges, card fees paid by shops, overdraft interest and so on.

This is why banks often make more when Bank Rate rises: the rates they charge borrowers tend to rise faster than what they pay savers (module 1 mentioned savers who never switch). Regulators and politicians watch this closely.

## Where the money for loans comes from

Many people picture a bank as a warehouse: savers deposit money, and the bank lends that same money out. The Bank of England has explained that this isn't how it works in modern economies.

When a bank makes a loan, it **creates a new deposit** in the borrower's account. That deposit is new money. In the Bank of England's words, most money in the modern economy is created by commercial banks making loans. Back to the question at the start: the bank doesn't take your £1,000 and hand it over. It types £1,000 into the borrower's account, creating a new deposit, while your £1,000 stays yours.

When loans are repaid, the money is destroyed again. So the amount of money in the economy rises and falls with bank lending.

## Fractional reserve: the textbook story and its limits

You may meet the **[[fractional reserve]]** story in economics books: banks must keep a fraction of deposits as "reserves" and lend out the rest, so a deposit gets "multiplied" through the system.

It's useful history, but the Bank of England's 2014 explanation says it's misleading as a description of how UK banks work today. Banks don't wait for deposits before lending, and reserves don't mechanically limit lending. What really limits it:

1. **Profit and risk.** A bank only lends if it expects to be repaid with interest. Lending to people who can't repay loses money.
2. **The interest rate.** The Bank of England's Bank Rate sets the cost of money in the system, which affects how much people want to borrow (module 12).
3. **Regulation.** Banks must hold enough **[[capital]]** (their own money, from shareholders and kept profits) to absorb losses, and enough **liquid assets** to pay depositors who want their money back.
4. **Competition and customers:** borrowers have to want the loans, and deposits can move to other banks.

## Capital and liquidity: what keeps a bank safe

Two different things can sink a bank:

- **Running out of capital (insolvency):** losses on bad loans eat through the shareholders' money until the bank owes more than it owns. Rules force banks to hold capital as a cushion, sized to how risky their lending is.
- **Running out of cash (illiquidity):** a bank lends long (a 25-year mortgage) but borrows short (deposits you can withdraw today). If too many depositors want their money at once, even a solvent bank can't pay. That's a **bank run**.

In September 2007, savers queued outside branches of **Northern Rock**, which relied heavily on borrowing from other banks and couldn't refinance when markets froze. The government guaranteed its deposits on 17 September 2007, and it was nationalised in 2008. It was the first big sign in the UK of the financial crisis (module 32).

Today the PRA (part of the Bank of England) sets rules on both capital and liquidity, and the Bank can lend to banks in an emergency.

## The FSCS: your safety net

If a UK-authorised bank or building society does fail, the **[[fscs|FSCS]]** pays back depositors up to {{fig:fscs+}}. It means a bank failure shouldn't cost ordinary savers anything, which also makes runs less likely: if you know your money is protected, there's less reason to rush to withdraw it.

Check the limit is per **banking licence**, not per brand.

## The common mistake

**"My savings are sitting in the bank's vault."**

Your deposit is really a promise from the bank to pay you, recorded in its books, not a pile of notes with your name on it. The bank's own assets (mainly loans) back that promise, along with its capital, its liquid assets, and in the last resort the FSCS. That's why bank regulation and deposit protection matter so much.

## In real life

- **Compare savings rates against Bank Rate.** If your bank pays far below it, it's keeping a big margin; switching is easy (module 9).
- **Stay under the FSCS limit per banking licence** if you ever have large sums.
- **Building societies and banks are both protected** by the FSCS.
- **Red flag:** "bank-like" accounts from firms that aren't authorised banks (some investment or crypto products). Their money may not be FSCS-protected.

## Think about it

:::reveal If banks can create money by lending, why don't they lend unlimited amounts?
Every loan carries risk: if it isn't repaid, the bank loses. They also need capital to absorb losses and liquid assets to meet withdrawals, both set by regulators, and borrowers only want so much credit at a given interest rate. Lending too freely is how banks fail.
:::

:::reveal Why do banks often profit more when interest rates rise?
They can usually raise loan rates faster than savings rates, especially on variable-rate lending, while many savers stay in low-paying accounts. That widens the margin. Competition and regulators' pressure push the other way.
:::
