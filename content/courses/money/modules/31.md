## The psychology of trading

Module 26's biases hit traders hard, because trading compresses decisions into minutes and puts the results on screen:

- **[[tilt|Tilt]]:** a state of frustration after losses (a poker term) where you stop following your rules. You trade bigger, faster, and outside your plan to "win it back".
- **Revenge trading:** jumping straight into another trade after a loss, to undo it. It usually turns one loss into several.
- **FOMO** (fear of missing out): chasing a move that's already happened because you can't bear watching it go without you.
- **Overconfidence after wins:** a winning streak makes people increase size and loosen rules, just before the inevitable losing run.
- **Moving stops and taking profits early:** loss aversion (module 26) makes losses feel worse than gains, so traders hold losers hoping and cut winners quickly, the exact opposite of what positive expectancy needs.

**[[discipline|Discipline]]** is following your plan when your feelings say not to. It isn't about being emotionless; it's about having rules that make the right action automatic: written setups, stops placed when you enter, a fixed 1% risk, and a daily loss limit after which you stop.

## Judging the process, not the outcome

Back in module 3 you met **resulting**: judging a decision only by its outcome. Over 20 trades, luck dominates. A good trade can lose and a bad one can win. So grade each trade on **process**, separately from its result:

| Grade | Means |
|---|---|
| **A** | Valid setup, correct size, stop and target as planned, exited by the rules |
| **B** | Small slip (entered a bit late, exited a bit early) |
| **C** | Broke a rule: wrong size, moved stop, no setup |
| **D** | Impulse or revenge trade, no plan at all |

Then compare: how did your A trades do in R, against your C and D trades? Traders are often surprised to find their rule-breaking trades cost them most.

## Making one change at a time

After a review, change **one** thing, then trade another 20 (or more) before judging it. Change five things at once and you won't know which one helped. Common useful changes:

- Only take your best (A-grade) setup.
- A daily loss limit: stop after 2R down.
- No trades within 30 minutes of a loss.
- Stops placed automatically at entry, never moved further away.

## Should you ever trade real money?

The honest answer from everything in this strand: **most people shouldn't**, and certainly not until they've shown, over at least a hundred paper trades with honest records, a positive expectancy after costs **and** the discipline to follow their rules. Even then, start tiny, and remember that real money feels different.

For most people, the best financial results come from the 📈 Investing strand: low-cost diversified funds, held for decades. Trading knowledge is still valuable: it helps you understand markets, spot hype and scams, and talk confidently about how prices move. That's a good outcome too.

## The common mistake

**"I'll stop breaking my rules once I'm trading real money and it matters."**

It works the other way. Real money makes emotions stronger, not weaker, so habits from paper trading get worse, not better. If you can't follow your rules with pretend money, you won't with real money. Paper trading is exactly where to fix it.

## In real life

- **Grade every trade's process** in your journal, separately from the result.
- **Review every 20 trades**, change one thing, repeat.
- **Use hard limits** to block tilt: a daily loss limit, a pause after losses.
- **Stay on paper** until you have a long, honest record.
- **Red flag:** feeling an urge to "win it back". That's tilt. Close the app.

## Think about it

:::reveal Why might a trader's results improve just by taking fewer trades?
Many losing trades come from boredom, FOMO or revenge rather than real setups. Cutting to A-grade setups only removes the trades with the worst expectancy, and lowers costs too.
:::

:::reveal Why do traders say "plan the trade, trade the plan"?
Decisions made calmly beforehand (setup, stop, size, target) are better than decisions made under pressure with money moving. Writing the plan first and following it mechanically keeps emotions out of the moments they do most damage.
:::
