## Two prices: bid and ask

At any moment a market shows two prices:

- **The [[bid]]:** the highest price a buyer is willing to pay right now. It's what **you** get if you sell.
- **The [[ask]]** (or offer): the lowest price a seller will accept right now. It's what **you** pay if you buy.

The gap between them is the **[[spread]]**. If a share is quoted 99.9p / 100.1p, the spread is 0.2p.

The spread is a cost you pay on every round trip. Buy at the ask, then immediately sell at the bid, and you've lost the spread. Every trade starts slightly in the red and needs the price to move in your favour by at least the spread just to break even.

## Liquidity

**[[liquidity|Liquidity]]** is how easily you can buy or sell without moving the price. A liquid market has lots of buyers and sellers, like shares in big FTSE 100 companies or major currency pairs. Spreads are tight and orders fill instantly.

An illiquid market, like shares in a tiny company, has few traders. Spreads are wide, and a big order can push the price against you. In a panic, liquidity can vanish just when you most want to get out.

## Order types

When you place a trade you choose an **order type**. The three you need to know:

- **[[market order|Market order]]:** buy or sell **now**, at the best price available. Fast and almost certain to fill, but you don't control the exact price. In a fast or thin market you might get a worse price than you saw, called **slippage**.
- **[[limit order|Limit order]]:** buy or sell only at **your price or better**. "Buy at 95p or lower." You control the price but it might never fill if the market doesn't get there.
- **[[stop order|Stop order]]:** becomes a market order **once the price reaches a level you set**. Its main use is the **stop-loss**: "if the price falls to 90p, sell", to cap your loss (module 23).

A stop-loss isn't a guarantee. If the price **gaps** (jumps past your level, say overnight on bad news), your order fills at the next available price, which can be much worse. Some providers offer **guaranteed stops** for an extra charge.

## Going long and going short

- **Going long:** you buy, hoping the price rises.
- **Going short:** you profit if the price **falls**. You effectively sell first and buy back later, hopefully cheaper.

Shorting is riskier than it sounds. When you buy, the most you can lose is what you paid (the price can't go below zero). When you short, the price can in theory rise without limit, so your potential loss has no ceiling unless you use a stop.

## Leverage and margin

**[[leverage|Leverage]]** lets you control a big position with a small deposit. The deposit is called **[[margin]]**. With 20:1 leverage, £500 of margin controls a £10,000 position.

Leverage multiplies **everything**:

- A 2% rise on £10,000 is **+£200**: 40% of your £500.
- A 2% fall is **−£200**: 40% of your £500 gone.

Back to the question at the start: a 2% move against a 20:1 position loses **£200**, which is **40%** of your deposit. A 5% move would wipe out the whole £500.

## CFDs and spread bets

In the UK, most leveraged trading by individuals happens through two products. You never own the underlying share or currency; you take a position on its price.

- **[[cfd|CFD]] (contract for difference):** you and the provider agree to settle the difference in price between opening and closing. Profits are usually taxed as capital gains.
- **[[spread bet]]:** you bet a set amount per point the price moves (say £2 per point). Because HMRC treats it as gambling, profits are {{fig:spreadBetTax+}}.

Both are leveraged, both let you go long or short, and both charge for holding positions overnight (financing charges), on top of the spread. They're designed for short-term trading; holding for months gets expensive.

Every UK provider must show a warning with the share of its clients who lose money: for big providers, {{fig:cfdLossRate}}. And crypto derivatives are {{fig:cryptoRules+}}.

## What a trade really costs

Adding it up, a short-term trade can cost:

- **The spread**, every time.
- **Commission** on some platforms.
- **Overnight financing** on leveraged positions.
- **Stamp Duty** on buying UK shares outright: {{fig:stampDutyShares}} (not on CFDs or spread bets).
- **Currency conversion** fees on foreign markets.

These are small per trade but huge for frequent traders. Ten trades a week, each costing 0.2% in spread, adds up to about **100% of your trading money a year** if your pot doesn't grow. That's the cost an edge has to beat before you make a penny.

## The common mistake

**"Leverage means I can make big profits from a small amount of money."**

It does, in exactly the same way it lets you make big **losses** from a small amount. Leverage doesn't improve your chances; it just magnifies the result, including the costs. Most people who lose money trading CFDs lose it because leverage turns ordinary price wobbles into account-ending moves.

## In real life

- **Read the bid and ask** before any trade, and treat the spread as a cost.
- **Use limit orders** in thin or fast markets to avoid nasty fills.
- **Never trade with leverage** until you've proven an edge on paper over many trades, and even then, use a fraction of the maximum.
- **Check the provider is FCA-authorised** and read its risk warning.
- **Red flag:** offers of "100:1 leverage" or bonuses for depositing. UK-regulated firms can't offer them to retail customers, so it's an offshore, unregulated firm.

## Think about it

:::reveal Why do market makers and brokers offer a spread instead of one price?
They're providing a service: always being ready to buy or sell. The spread is their payment for taking the risk of holding positions and for providing liquidity. In busy markets competition squeezes it; in quiet ones they need more to cover the risk.
:::

:::reveal Why did the FCA cap leverage instead of banning CFDs altogether?
CFDs have legitimate uses, such as hedging and for experienced traders who understand the risks. The FCA judged that capping leverage, closing positions at 50% margin and preventing negative balances would cut the worst harm while keeping access. Many retail clients still lose money, which is why the warnings remain.
:::
