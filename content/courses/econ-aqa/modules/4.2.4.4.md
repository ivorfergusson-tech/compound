## Why a bank might fail

Banks **borrow short and lend long**. Deposits can be taken out at any time, while mortgages take decades to repay.

This creates risks:

- **Liquidity risk:** the bank may not have enough cash to meet withdrawals, even if its loans are sound. If savers lose confidence, a **bank run** can follow, since each person wants to get out first.
- **Credit risk:** borrowers may not repay, which causes losses. If losses are larger than the bank's capital, it's **insolvent**.
- **Funding risk:** a bank that relies on borrowing from other banks may find this dries up.

Northern Rock relied on borrowing in markets. When those markets froze in 2007, it ran short of funds, savers queued to withdraw, and it was nationalised in 2008.

## Liquidity and capital ratios

**[[liquidity ratio|Liquidity ratios]]** compare a bank's liquid assets with its short-term liabilities. A higher ratio means that it's better able to meet withdrawals.

**[[capital ratio|Capital ratios]]** compare a bank's capital (mainly shareholders' funds) with its assets, weighted by risk. Capital absorbs losses. A higher ratio means that it can lose more before it fails.

Higher ratios make banks safer, but they also limit lending and profit. You won't be asked to calculate them.

## Moral hazard

**[[moral hazard|Moral hazard]]** arises when people take more risk because they don't bear the full cost if things go wrong.

If bankers believe the government will rescue them, they have a reason to take bigger risks: they keep the profits if it works, and taxpayers carry the losses if it fails. Banks seen as **too big to fail** are the clearest case. A deposit guarantee can also reduce savers' reasons to check whether a bank is safe.

So a rescue can stop a crisis now, yet make the next one more likely.

## Systemic risk

**[[systemic risk|Systemic risk]]** is the risk that trouble at one institution spreads and threatens the whole financial system. Banks lend to one another, so one failure can cause losses at others, and fear can spread quickly.

Problems in financial markets reach the **real economy**:

- Banks cut lending, so firms can't fund investment or keep running.
- Households lose wealth and confidence, so they spend less.
- AD falls, and unemployment rises.
- Governments spend heavily on rescues, so public debt rises.

The 2008 financial crisis showed this, and it led to the UK's deep recession that followed.

## Who regulates the UK's financial system?

You need to know the role of each, and not the detail.

- **The Bank of England** has responsibility for protecting financial stability.
- **The [[financial policy committee|Financial Policy Committee (FPC)]]** is a committee of the Bank. It looks at risks to the **whole system**, and can tell regulators what to do. It sets the countercyclical capital buffer, for example, which requires banks to hold more capital in good times and allows them to use it in bad times.
- **The [[prudential regulation authority|Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA)]]** is part of the Bank. It supervises **individual** banks, building societies and insurers, to keep them safe and sound.
- **The [[financial conduct authority|Financial Conduct Authority (FCA)]]** is separate from the Bank. It regulates how firms **behave towards customers**, and it aims to protect consumers and keep markets fair.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain how the failure of a large bank could affect the real economy. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Systemic risk is the risk that problems at one institution spread through the financial system. The real economy is the part that produces goods and services.

**Application:** In 2008, UK banks suffered heavy losses, and some needed government support.

**Analysis:** If a large bank fails, other banks that lent to it make losses, so all of them cut lending. Firms can't get credit, so they cut investment and jobs. Households lose confidence and spend less. AD shifts left, so real output falls and unemployment rises, and the government may need to borrow more for a rescue.
:::

Why it scores: it links the failure inside the financial system to spending, output and jobs.
