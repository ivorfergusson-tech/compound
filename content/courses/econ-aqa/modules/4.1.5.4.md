A new coffee shop opens in your town and it's packed, so it makes large profits.

:::callout idea
Monopolistic competition has many firms with slightly different products: each has a little power over price in the short run, but easy entry removes abnormal profit in the long run.
:::

## The main characteristics

[[monopolistic competition|Monopolistic competition]] is the structure of many everyday markets, such as hairdressers, cafés, restaurants and small online sellers:

- **Many firms**, each small next to the market.
- **Differentiated products:** a little different in location, brand, style or service.
- **Low barriers to entry and exit.**

Because each product is a little different, a firm that raises its price keeps some customers. So it faces a **downward-sloping** demand curve, and it's fairly elastic since there are many close substitutes. The firm is a **[[price maker]]**, though only a weak one.

## The short run

The firm produces where **MC = MR** and charges the price shown by the demand curve (AR) at that output. If AR is above ATC, it makes abnormal profit.

## The long run

With low barriers, abnormal profit attracts new firms. They take customers, so demand for each existing firm shifts **left**, and it also becomes more elastic since there are more substitutes.

This continues until **AR just touches ATC**. At that point the firm makes **normal profit** at the output where MC = MR. Set the slider to +0 to see this.

If firms are making losses, the reverse happens: some leave, and demand for the remaining firms shifts right.

## Efficiency

In long-run equilibrium the firm is **not** at the lowest point of ATC, because AR slopes down and touches ATC to the left of its lowest point. So:

- It isn't **productively efficient**. It has **[[excess capacity]]**: it could lower average cost by making more.
- It isn't **allocatively efficient**, since P is above MC.

In exchange, consumers get variety. A town with ten different cafés has more choice than one with two big identical ones, and many people value that.

## Non-price competition

Firms in this market try to stand out, because a stronger brand makes demand higher and less elastic. **[[non-price competition|Non-price competition]]** means competing in ways other than price, such as:

- Branding and advertising.
- Quality, service and opening hours.
- Location.
- Loyalty schemes and special offers.

It raises costs, and it may only give a short-lived gain, since rivals can copy the idea and new firms can enter.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain why a firm in monopolistic competition is unlikely to make abnormal profit in the long run. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Monopolistic competition has many firms selling differentiated products, with low barriers to entry.

**Application:** If a café in a town makes abnormal profit, others can open nearby at quite low cost.

**Analysis:** New firms attract some of its customers, so the demand curve for the first café shifts left and becomes more elastic. This goes on until average revenue just equals average total cost at the output where MC = MR. At that point only normal profit is made, and there's no incentive for more firms to enter.
:::

Why it scores: it uses the low barriers to build a chain, and it gets the end point right.
