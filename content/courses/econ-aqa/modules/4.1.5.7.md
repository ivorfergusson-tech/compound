:::callout idea
Price discrimination is charging different prices for the same product where the cost doesn't differ: firms charge more where demand is less elastic, and earn more profit than with one price.
:::

## What it is

**[[price discrimination|Price discrimination]]** means charging different consumers different prices for the same good or service, for reasons not linked to cost.

Real-world examples include:

- Peak and off-peak train fares.
- Student and pensioner discounts.
- Cheaper cinema tickets in the daytime.
- Higher prices for flights booked at the last minute.

A higher price for a bigger item isn't price discrimination if it just reflects higher costs. Check that the price difference doesn't come from a cost difference.

## The conditions needed

Three conditions must hold.

1. **Price-setting power.** The firm must be a price maker. A price taker has no choice over its price.
2. **Different price elasticities of demand.** There must be groups of consumers with different PEDs.
3. **Separated markets.** The firm must be able to keep the groups apart, so that people who buy cheaply can't resell to those who pay more. Selling on is called **arbitrage**.

Rail fares meet these. Commuters have few alternatives at 8am, so demand is inelastic. A ticket is for a given time, so it can't be passed on from one group to another.

## The diagram

A firm treats each group as a separate market and sets **MR = MC in each one**. Where demand is less elastic, MR falls away faster, so MR = MC gives a smaller output and a higher price.

## Advantages and disadvantages

**For producers:**

- **Higher revenue and profit**, which can pay for investment.
- Spare capacity gets used, such as seats on off-peak trains.

**For consumers:**

- Some pay **less**, and some can buy who would otherwise be priced out, such as students or people on low incomes.
- Some pay **more**. The surplus that those consumers lose goes to the firm.
- The outcome can be unfair, and it depends on which group you're in.

**For efficiency:**

- Output may be higher than with one monopoly price, so more people are served.
- Firms may spend money keeping the markets separate, and it can protect an inefficient firm.

Price discrimination can sometimes make a product possible at all. A train service may only cover its costs if it can charge different groups different fares.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain the conditions needed for a firm to price discriminate. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Price discrimination is charging different prices to different groups for the same product, for reasons not linked to cost.

**Application:** A train company charges commuters more at peak times than people travelling at off-peak times.

**Analysis:** First, it needs price-setting power. Second, the groups must have different price elasticities of demand. Commuters have few substitutes, so their demand is inelastic and they can be charged more. Third, the markets must be kept separate, or those who buy cheaply could sell on to those paying more, and the higher price would collapse. Tickets valid only at certain times prevent this.
:::

Why it scores: three conditions, each one linked to a real example.
