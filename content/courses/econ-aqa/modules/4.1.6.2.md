## Guess first

Two jobs pay the same. One is outdoors, varied and well thought of. The other is dull, dirty and at unsocial hours.

:::guess {"prompt":"Which job would have more applicants? How much more would the second job need to pay for you to take it?","kind":"text","id":"econ-aqa:4.1.6.2-guess"}
:::

:::callout idea
People choose jobs on more than pay, so the supply of labour to an occupation depends on money and on what the job is like, and it shifts when skills, conditions or the pay of other jobs change.
:::

## Monetary and non-monetary considerations

The **supply of labour** to an occupation is the number of workers willing and able to do that job.

**Monetary considerations** are the wage, overtime, bonuses and the pay of other jobs.

**Non-monetary considerations** include:

- **Job satisfaction:** interest, status and a sense of doing something worthwhile.
- **Job dissatisfaction:** danger, boredom or stress.
- **Working conditions:** hours, flexibility, holidays, location and the place of work.
- Training, promotion prospects and job security.

So an unpleasant job may need to pay more to attract workers. A job people love may attract them at lower pay.

## The supply curve for labour

The supply curve for labour shows the relationship between the wage rate and the number of workers willing to work in an occupation.

It slopes **upwards**: a higher wage attracts more workers from other jobs, and draws in people who weren't working.

A change in the wage is a **movement along** the curve. A change in anything else **shifts** it.

## Shifts in the market supply curve

The supply of labour to an occupation shifts right when:

- **Non-monetary benefits improve,** such as better hours or conditions.
- **Pay in other jobs falls,** so this one looks better.
- **More people have the skills or qualifications,** perhaps because training is cheaper or easier.
- **The population grows,** or migration brings in more workers.

It shifts left when the reverse happens, for example if a job becomes more stressful, or if the qualifications become harder or more costly to get.

## The elasticity of labour supply

Supply is more elastic when workers can move into the job easily. It's less elastic when the job needs long training or rare talent, since it takes years for a rise in pay to bring in more qualified doctors or pilots.

This helps to explain why some jobs are paid so much. Demand can be high while supply is small and slow to respond.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain two reasons why the supply of labour to an occupation might fall. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** The supply of labour is the number of workers willing and able to work in an occupation at each wage.

**Application:** Care work involves long shifts and physical strain, and retail or warehouse jobs may offer similar pay.

**Analysis:** First, if pay in other jobs rises, care work looks worse by comparison, so some workers leave, and supply shifts left. Second, if conditions get worse, for example through more stress or more unsocial hours, the job gives less non-monetary reward. Fewer people are then willing to do it at any given wage, which also shifts supply left.
:::

Why it scores: there are two separate causes, one monetary and one non-monetary, and each is taken to the shift.
