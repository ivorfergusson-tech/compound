## Rational decisions and incentives

Traditional economic theory assumes people are **[[rational decision making|rational]]**. That doesn't mean they're clever or never make mistakes. It means they weigh up the costs and benefits of each option and choose the one that does most to meet their own aims.

- **Consumers** aim to get the most satisfaction from their income.
- **Firms** are usually assumed to aim for the most profit.
- **Workers** weigh up pay against effort and free time.

If people respond to costs and benefits, then changing the costs and benefits changes what they do. That's an **[[economic incentive]]**: something that encourages or discourages a choice. A sale price, a bonus and a fine are all incentives. A tax on cigarettes is an incentive to smoke less.

## Total and marginal utility

**[[utility|Utility]]** is the satisfaction, or benefit, a person gets from consuming something. Nobody can really measure it, but imagine it in made-up units to see how it works.

- **[[total utility|Total utility]]** is the satisfaction from **all** the units consumed.
- **[[marginal utility|Marginal utility]]** is the **extra** satisfaction from **one more** unit.

| Slices of pizza | Total utility | Marginal utility |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 10 | 10 |
| 2 | 18 | 8 |
| 3 | 24 | 6 |
| 4 | 28 | 4 |
| 5 | 30 | 2 |
| 6 | 30 | 0 |
| 7 | 28 | −2 |

To get marginal utility, take the total from the row before away from this row's total: the third slice adds 24 − 18 = 6.

Total utility keeps rising as long as marginal utility is positive. It peaks when marginal utility hits zero, and falls if marginal utility turns negative (the seventh slice makes you feel worse).

## Diminishing marginal utility

The table shows the **[[diminishing marginal utility|hypothesis of diminishing marginal utility]]**: as a person consumes more of a good in a given period, the extra satisfaction from each additional unit eventually falls.

Two things to get right:

- It's **marginal** utility that falls, not total utility. Total utility is still rising at slice 5.
- It's about a **given period**. A pizza next week doesn't count, because you'll be hungry again.

This supports the **downward-sloping demand curve** you'll meet in 4.1.3. If each extra unit gives less satisfaction than the last, you'll only buy more of something if the price falls.

## Utility maximisation and the margin

**[[utility maximisation|Utility maximisation]]** means getting the greatest possible total satisfaction from your limited income.

Rational people don't decide "all or nothing". They decide **at the margin**: is **one more** worth it? Compare the extra benefit with the extra cost.

Say one unit of utility is worth £1 to you and each slice costs £3. The third slice gives you £6 of benefit for £3, so you buy it. The fourth gives £4 for £3, so you buy it. The fifth gives £2 for £3, so you stop. You stop **before** you're full, because the fifth slice isn't worth what it costs: that £3 would give you more satisfaction spent on something else.

Marginal thinking works everywhere: should a firm make one more unit? Should you revise one more hour? Should the government clean up the last bit of pollution? The last bit is often not worth its cost, even when the first bit clearly was.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain how the hypothesis of diminishing marginal utility helps to explain why demand curves slope downwards. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Marginal utility is the extra satisfaction from consuming one more unit of a good. The hypothesis of diminishing marginal utility says this falls as more of the good is consumed in a given period.

**Application:** A person buying bottles of water on a hot day gets a lot of satisfaction from the first bottle but much less from the third.

**Analysis:** A rational consumer buys another unit only if its marginal utility is worth the price. As each extra bottle gives less satisfaction, the consumer will only be willing to buy more if the price is lower. So quantity demanded rises as price falls, which gives a downward-sloping demand curve.
:::

Why it scores: it defines the key term, gives a real example, and builds the chain step by step from falling satisfaction to a lower price to more bought. AQA doesn't require the equi-marginal principle here, so leave it out.
