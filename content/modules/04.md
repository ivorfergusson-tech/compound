## Recap

Module 2 met the things people invest in: [[share|shares]], [[bond|bonds]] and [[fund|funds]]. But who actually creates, sells, looks after and trades them? Behind every savings account, pension, share and mortgage there's a firm earning a living from it. This module draws the map: who does what in finance, how money moves between them, and who keeps watch.

## What finance is for

Strip away the jargon and finance does a few simple jobs:

1. **Moving money from savers to users.** Your savings fund someone's mortgage, a company's new factory or a government's hospital.
2. **Payments.** Getting money from one person to another, instantly and safely.
3. **Managing risk.** Insurance, pensions and hedging let people share or pass on risks they can't carry alone.
4. **Pricing.** Markets constantly work out what companies, bonds and currencies are worth.

Financial services are a big part of the UK economy: {{fig:ukFinanceSize+}}. London is one of the world's biggest financial centres, but finance jobs are spread across the country, from Edinburgh and Leeds to Bristol and Birmingham.

## Retail banks

**[[retail bank|Retail banks]]** (and building societies) are the ones you deal with: current accounts, savings, mortgages, loans, cards and payments. They make most of their money from the gap between what they charge borrowers and what they pay savers, plus fees (module 8).

Examples include the big high-street groups, building societies (owned by their members rather than shareholders), and newer app-based banks. Commercial banks do the same for businesses.

## Investment banks

**[[investment bank|Investment banks]]** serve companies, governments and big investors rather than the public. Their main jobs:

- **Advisory:** advising companies on buying or merging with other companies (M&A).
- **Raising money:** helping companies issue new shares (like an IPO, module 6) or bonds.
- **Markets (sales and trading):** buying and selling shares, bonds, currencies and derivatives for big clients, and making markets so there's always someone to trade with.
- **Research:** analysing companies and economies.

Some large banking groups do both retail and investment banking. Since 2019, the biggest UK banks have had to **ring-fence** their everyday banking from their investment banking, to protect ordinary customers (module 32). Smaller advisory-only firms are called **boutiques** (module 16).

## Asset managers

**[[asset manager|Asset managers]]** (also called fund managers or investment managers) invest other people's money: pension schemes, insurers, charities and individuals through funds. They earn a fee, usually a percentage of the money they manage.

UK investment managers look after {{fig:ukAum+}}. Some are passive (running index funds cheaply, module 14), some active (picking investments). Hedge funds and private equity are specialised kinds of asset manager (module 20).

Because asset managers **buy** investments, they're called the **buy side**. Investment banks, which **sell** services and products to them, are the **sell side**.

## Brokers and platforms

A **[[broker]]** buys and sells on behalf of clients. Stockbrokers place your share orders on an exchange; investment platforms (module 14) hold your ISA and funds and route your trades; insurance and mortgage brokers find you products from many providers.

They earn through fees, commission or the spread (module 7). A broker is a middleman: it shouldn't be making your investment decisions unless you've paid for advice.

## Insurers and pension schemes

**Insurers** collect premiums from many people and pay out to the few who suffer a loss. Because they hold the premiums for years before paying claims, they're huge investors too.

**Pension schemes** collect contributions from workers and employers (module 21) and invest them for decades, usually through asset managers. Together, insurers and pension schemes are among the biggest owners of shares and bonds in the UK.

## Regulators: who keeps watch

Finance holds other people's money, and failures can hurt millions, so it's heavily regulated. The main UK bodies:

- **The Bank of England:** the central bank. It sets Bank Rate (module 12) and is responsible for the stability of the whole financial system.
- **The PRA** (Prudential Regulation Authority), part of the Bank of England: makes sure banks, building societies and insurers are safe and strong enough to survive shocks.
- **The FCA** (Financial Conduct Authority): makes sure firms treat customers fairly and markets work honestly. It authorises firms, sets conduct rules and keeps the register you can check.
- **HM Treasury:** the government department responsible for financial policy and the law.

Plus services that protect you directly: the **Financial Ombudsman Service** settles complaints for free, and the **FSCS** pays compensation if an authorised firm fails (module 9).

These are the **[[regulator|regulators]]** whose rules you've already met: risk warnings on CFDs (module 7), the FSCS limit (module 9), the BNPL rules (module 13).

## Following your £100

Back to the question at the start. Your monthly £100 pension contribution might touch:

1. **Your employer's payroll**, which deducts it.
2. **The pension provider**, which runs your account.
3. **An asset manager**, which runs the global index fund.
4. **Brokers and market makers**, which carry out the fund's trades on **stock exchanges**.
5. **A custodian bank**, which holds the fund's shares safely.
6. **The companies** whose shares the fund buys.
7. **Regulators** (The Pensions Regulator, the FCA and PRA) overseeing much of the chain.

Each one takes a small fee or earns a spread somewhere. That's why fees matter so much (module 14): the chain is long.

## The common mistake

**"Finance is just people moving money around and skimming off the top."**

Some of it can feel that way, and there are real problems with excessive fees and risk-taking (module 32). But without finance, there'd be no mortgages, no pensions, no way for a new company to raise money or for savers to share in growth. The useful question isn't "is finance good or bad?" but "is this particular service worth what it costs?"

## In real life

- **Check any firm** you deal with on the FCA register before handing over money.
- **Know who's who:** a platform holds your investments; a fund manager runs the fund; an adviser recommends (and must be authorised).
- **Complain first to the firm**, then for free to the Financial Ombudsman if you're not happy.
- **Red flag:** a firm that isn't on the FCA register, or a cloned firm using a real firm's name with different contact details.

## Think about it

:::reveal Why might the same banking group have both a retail bank and an investment bank?
They can serve the same corporate clients in many ways and share costs. But it also means risky trading could threaten ordinary customers' deposits, which is why the UK now ring-fences everyday banking in the biggest groups (module 32).
:::

:::reveal Why are pension funds and insurers such important investors?
They hold huge sums for decades (pensions until retirement, insurers until claims are paid), so they can invest for the long term in shares, bonds and infrastructure. Their choices shape which companies and projects get funded.
:::
