A country bans new petrol cars and people switch to electric ones.

:::callout idea
Markets are linked, so a change in one spreads to others through goods bought together, goods that replace each other, goods used for several things, and goods produced together.
:::

## Markets don't stand alone

A change in demand, supply or price in one market usually affects others. You need to know five links.

## Joint demand

**[[joint demand|Joint demand]]** means two goods are demanded **together**. They're **complements**, with a negative XED.

Printers and ink, phones and cases, cars and petrol. If the price of cars falls, more cars are bought, so demand for petrol shifts right.

## Competitive demand

**[[competitive demand|Competitive demand]]** means goods **compete** for the same customers. They're **substitutes**, with a positive XED.

Tea and coffee, buses and trains. If the price of coffee rises, demand for tea shifts right. The closer the substitutes, the bigger the effect.

## Composite demand

**[[composite demand|Composite demand]]** means one good is demanded for **several different uses**.

Milk is used for drinking, cheese, butter and yoghurt. Land is used for homes, farming and shops. Oil is used for fuel and plastics.

If more milk is used for cheese, less is available for butter, so the supply of butter falls and its price tends to rise. Remember the opportunity cost: one use is at the expense of another.

## Derived demand

**[[derived demand|Derived demand]]** means a good, service or factor is demanded because of what it **helps to produce**, not for its own sake.

Nobody wants steel for its own sake: they want the cars and bridges it makes. Labour is the classic example. Firms hire workers to make goods and services, so demand for labour depends on demand for the final product. If demand for new houses rises, so does demand for bricks and bricklayers.

## Joint supply

**[[joint supply|Joint supply]]** means producing one good also produces **another**, as a by-product.

Beef and leather come from the same cow. Refining crude oil gives petrol, diesel and other products. If demand for beef rises, more cattle are farmed, so the supply of leather shifts right and its price tends to fall.

## Tracing the effects

Exam questions often give you a change in one market and ask for the effect on another. Follow it step by step, with a separate diagram for each market.

**Example:** a fall in demand for beef.

1. **Beef market:** demand shifts left. Price and quantity fall.
2. **Leather market (joint supply):** fewer cattle are reared, so the supply of leather shifts left. Its price rises.
3. **Chicken market (competitive demand):** if the fall in demand comes from people switching to chicken, demand for chicken shifts right.
4. **Butchers' labour (derived demand):** demand for workers in beef processing falls.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain how a rise in demand for new houses could affect the market for bricks and the wages of bricklayers. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Derived demand is the demand for a good or factor of production because of what it is used to produce.

**Application:** Bricks and bricklayers are not wanted for their own sake, but to build houses.

**Analysis:** As demand for new houses rises, builders want to build more, so demand for bricks shifts right. This raises the price and quantity of bricks. Demand for bricklayers also shifts right, so wages tend to rise, particularly if skilled bricklayers are in short supply, as training takes time and labour supply is inelastic in the short run.
:::

Why it scores: it names the link, follows it through both markets, and uses elasticity to explain how much wages might rise.
