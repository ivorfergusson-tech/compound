:::callout idea
Unemployment can come from weak demand or from problems on the supply side, and an economy has a natural rate that spending alone can't push below for long.
:::

## Demand-side and supply-side causes

**Demand-side:** a fall in AD means firms need fewer workers. This is cyclical unemployment, shown by a negative output gap. It can be reduced by raising AD.

**Supply-side:** problems in the labour market itself, which affect the position of the **LRAS** curve. These include frictional and structural unemployment, poor skills, immobility, weak incentives to work and real wages that are too high.

## Real wage unemployment

**[[real wage unemployment|Real wage unemployment]]** happens when real wages are held **above** the level at which the supply of labour equals the demand. There are then more people who want to work than there are jobs.

It's also called **classical** unemployment. Causes include:

- Trade unions pushing wages above equilibrium.
- A minimum wage set above the market wage.
- Wages that don't fall in a downturn, because workers resist pay cuts.

Set a wage floor here and see the gap open up.

## The natural rate of unemployment

The **[[natural rate of unemployment|natural rate of unemployment]]** is the rate that remains when the labour market is in equilibrium, once there's no cyclical unemployment. It's made up of frictional and structural unemployment.

It's the level at which, in the long run, inflation is stable. You'll see why with the Phillips curve in 4.2.3.4.

The natural rate isn't fixed. It depends on:

- **Skills and training,** and how far they match the jobs available.
- **Labour mobility,** including the housing market.
- **Benefits,** which affect the incentive to take a job.
- **Trade union power and employment law.**
- **Information** about jobs.
- **Taxes** on work.

So supply-side policies can lower it, by improving how the labour market works.

## Consequences of unemployment

**For individuals:**

- Lost income, and a lower standard of living.
- Loss of skills, confidence and motivation, the longer it lasts.
- Worse physical and mental health, and strain on families.
- Lower chances of getting a job in future.

**For the performance of the economy:**

- **Lost output:** the economy is inside its PPF, and this output can't be regained.
- **Higher government spending** on benefits and **less tax revenue,** so borrowing rises.
- **Lower AD,** as those out of work spend less, which can feed through the multiplier.
- **Lost human capital,** which can lower long-run growth.
- **Social costs,** such as crime and inequality. These tend to hit some regions and groups hardest.

For some firms, there's an upside: more people apply for jobs, and wage pressure is lower.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain two factors that could reduce the natural rate of unemployment. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** The natural rate of unemployment is the rate when the labour market is in equilibrium, and it includes frictional and structural unemployment.

**Application:** The UK could fund more technical training in areas where industries have declined.

**Analysis:** First, retraining helps structural unemployment, because workers gain the skills that firms need, so occupational immobility falls and vacancies get filled. Second, better job information cuts the time between jobs, which lowers frictional unemployment. Both improve the working of the labour market, which shifts LRAS to the right.
:::

Why it scores: it links each factor to a type of unemployment, then to the supply side of the economy.
