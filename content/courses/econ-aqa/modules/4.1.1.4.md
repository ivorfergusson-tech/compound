## The economic problem: scarcity

[[scarcity|Scarcity]] is the **fundamental [[economic problem]]**. It exists because:

- **resources are limited**: there's only so much land, labour, capital and enterprise at any time, and
- **wants are unlimited**: people always want more or better goods and services.

Scarcity isn't the same as being poor. A billionaire still faces scarcity: they have only 24 hours in a day and can't be in two places at once. The UK government faces it too: every pound spent on defence can't also be spent on the NHS.

A few goods aren't scarce. A **[[free good]]** is one with no opportunity cost, because there's enough for everyone without anyone going short: air in an open field, sunlight, sea water in mid-ocean. Most goods are **[[economic good|economic goods]]**: they use scarce resources, so making them means giving something else up.

## Choice and the allocation of resources

Because resources are scarce, **choices** have to be made about how they're **allocated** between different uses. That's what the three key questions from the last lesson are about: what to make, how, and for whom.

The [[allocation of resources]] means the way resources are shared between the different goods and services an economy could produce. Land used for housing can't also be used for farming. A doctor's hour spent in A&E can't also be spent in a GP surgery.

## Opportunity cost

Every choice has an **[[opportunity cost]]**: **the value of the next best alternative given up**.

Back to your Saturday. If your next best choice was the shift, the opportunity cost of football is the £40 (and what you'd have done with it). If your next best choice was revising, the opportunity cost is that revision. It isn't **all** the things you could have done: only the **single next best one**.

Three things to know for the exam:

- **It's measured in what's given up, not in money.** Money is just a convenient way of putting a value on it. The opportunity cost of a new hospital might be two secondary schools that could have been built instead.
- **"Free" things still have an opportunity cost.** A free concert ticket costs you the evening you could have spent another way. A "free" NHS appointment is paid for by taxes that could have funded something else.
- **It applies to everyone:** consumers (spend or save?), firms (a new factory or a new product?), governments (lower taxes or more spending?).

## Trade-offs

A **[[trade-off]]** is the balancing act behind a choice: getting more of one thing means accepting less of another. Governments trade off lower taxes against public spending; you trade off part-time work against study time. A trade-off is the choice; the opportunity cost is what you lose by making it.

In the next lesson you'll see all of this on one diagram: the production possibility diagram.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Using an example, explain why the government's decision to spend more on the NHS has an opportunity cost. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made. It exists because resources are scarce and have alternative uses.

**Application:** If the government spends an extra £5 billion on the NHS, that money, and the doctors' time, buildings and equipment it pays for, cannot be used elsewhere. For example, it could instead have been spent on building new schools.

**Analysis:** If schools were the government's next best use, then the schools not built are the opportunity cost of the extra NHS spending. Because the government's budget is limited, more spending in one area always means less in another, or higher taxes or borrowing, which themselves have opportunity costs for households and future taxpayers.
:::

Why it scores: it defines the term, links it to scarcity, names a specific next best alternative and follows the chain through to the consequences.
