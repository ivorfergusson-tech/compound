## Guess first

A town has one big employer: a factory that hires almost everyone. There are few other jobs, and moving away is hard.

:::guess {"prompt":"What can the factory get away with on pay? How is this like a monopolist selling to consumers?","kind":"text","id":"econ-aqa:4.1.6.4-guess"}
:::

:::callout idea
When a labour market is imperfect, wages aren't set by competition alone: a single large employer can push both pay and employment below their competitive levels.
:::

## What makes a labour market imperfect?

Three important factors are:

- **[[monopsony|Monopsony]] power:** one or a few employers dominate.
- **Trade unions:** workers act together, which gives them power over wages.
- **Imperfect information:** workers don't know what other jobs pay, or which jobs exist, and employers can't tell how good an applicant is.

Others include workers who can't easily move, whether between places or between jobs. All of these can keep wages away from the level that competition would set.

## What a monopsony is

A **monopsony** is a market with a single buyer. In a labour market, it's one employer. Examples close to this include a single large employer in a small town, or the main public employer of a type of worker.

The key difference from a competitive firm is that a monopsonist is **not** a wage taker. It faces the whole upward-sloping supply curve, so to hire more, it has to offer a higher wage.

## Why MCL lies above the supply curve

Assume the firm pays all its workers the same. To attract one more worker, it must raise everyone's pay. So hiring that worker costs more than their own wage.

| Workers | Wage | Total wage bill | Marginal cost of labour |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | £50 | £500 | |
| 11 | £52 | £572 | £72 |

The 11th worker gets £52, yet costs the firm £72, as the other ten each get £2 more.

So the **marginal cost of labour (MCL)** lies above the supply curve, which is the **average cost of labour (ACL)**.

## The monopsony diagram

A profit-maximising monopsonist hires where **MCL = MRP**, then pays the wage that the **supply curve** shows for that number of workers.

Set the slider at £50, and you'll see 30 workers paid £50. Competition would give 45 workers at £65. So the employer uses its market power to reduce **both** the wage and employment below their competitive levels.

Workers lose in two ways: fewer of them have jobs, and those in work are paid less than the value of what they produce. The firm gains a larger share of the income.

## Imperfect information

Poor information has its own effects. If workers don't know about better-paid jobs, they stay where they are, so wage gaps last. Employers who can't judge applicants may rely on things like qualifications, or on prejudice.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Using a diagram, explain why a monopsonist pays lower wages than a competitive labour market. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** A monopsony is a labour market with a single buyer of labour. It faces an upward-sloping supply curve, which is its average cost of labour.

**Application:** On the diagram, MCL lies above the supply curve, since hiring one more worker means raising the wage of all of them.

**Analysis:** The firm maximises profit where MCL = MRP. This is at a lower level of employment than where supply meets MRP, which would be the competitive outcome. The firm then pays only the wage needed to attract that number of workers, read off the supply curve, and this is below the competitive wage. So both pay and employment are lower.
:::

Why it scores: it follows the diagram step by step, and it says where to read the wage from, which many answers get wrong.
