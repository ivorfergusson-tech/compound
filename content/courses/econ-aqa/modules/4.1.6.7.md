## Wage discrimination

**[[wage discrimination|Wage discrimination]]** is paying different groups of workers different wages for the same work, where the difference doesn't reflect productivity. It's the labour market version of price discrimination.

The conditions needed are:

- **Wage-setting power:** the employer must have monopsony power. A wage taker can't choose.
- **Different elasticities of labour supply:** groups must differ in how they respond to pay.
- **Separated groups:** the firm must be able to keep the groups apart, so that those paid less can't simply take the better-paid role.

The employer pays less to the group with the **less elastic** supply, which has fewer alternatives. Real-world examples include:

- Lower rates for new starters than for existing staff.
- Paying more for hard-to-fill shifts in the same job.
- Lower pay for younger workers doing the same work, as in the youth rates of the minimum wage.

## Advantages and disadvantages of wage discrimination

| | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| **Workers** | Some groups may get work they wouldn't otherwise get | Those paid less lose income, and it can feel unfair |
| **Employers** | Lower wage bills and higher profit | Lower morale, and legal risk if it's unlawful |
| **Economy** | More employment is possible, and profits can fund investment | Wider inequality, and talent may be wasted |

## Discrimination by gender, ethnicity and other factors

Discrimination also happens when employers treat workers differently because of who they are, not what they can do. This could relate to gender, ethnicity, age, disability, religion or sexuality.

The law bans this in the UK, under the Equality Act 2010. Equal pay for equal work is a legal right. It can still happen in less visible ways.

Discrimination can affect:

- **Wages:** being paid less for the same work.
- **Levels of employment:** being hired less often.
- **Types of employment:** being kept out of better-paid jobs or promotion, which is known as **occupational crowding** when a group ends up in a small range of jobs.

On a diagram, if employers discriminate against a group, they act as though that group's MRP were lower. Demand for those workers is too low, so their wage and employment are both below what they'd otherwise be. If they're crowded into fewer jobs, supply to those jobs rises, which pushes wages down further.

## The gender pay gap

The gender pay gap isn't the same as unequal pay for the same job. It compares the average pay of all men and all women. The ONS put the UK gap for all employees at 12.8% in April 2025, and at 6.9% among full-time employees, with the full-time gap at 6.8% in April 2026.

Discrimination is one possible cause. Others include women being more likely to work part time, the effect of career breaks for caring, and differences in occupation. Economists debate how much each one matters, and some of those patterns may reflect discrimination in themselves.

## The costs to the economy

- **Workers** lose income, and may invest less in their own skills if they expect to be held back.
- **Employers** miss out on talented people, so productivity is lower.
- **The economy** produces less: it's inside its PPF when talent isn't well used, and inequality is wider.
- **The government** collects less tax and may pay more in benefits.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain how discrimination could lower the wages of a group of workers. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Labour market discrimination occurs when workers are treated differently for reasons not related to their productivity.

**Application:** If employers wrongly undervalue the work of an ethnic minority group, they may be less likely to hire them.

**Analysis:** Employers act as if the group's MRP is lower than it is, so the demand curve for its labour lies to the left of where it should be. This gives a lower wage and lower employment. If those workers are also kept out of some jobs, they're crowded into others, which raises supply there and pushes wages down further.
:::

Why it scores: it uses MRP and demand, and then adds occupational crowding as a second chain.
