## Budget balance and national debt

- A **[[budget deficit|budget deficit]]** means the government spends more than it raises in tax in a year, so it has to borrow.
- A **budget surplus** means tax revenue is more than spending.
- The **[[national debt|national debt]]** is the total of all past borrowing that hasn't been repaid.

The deficit is a **flow** over a year. The debt is a **stock** at a point in time. Each deficit adds to the debt, and each surplus reduces it.

## Cyclical and structural deficits

- A **[[cyclical deficit|cyclical deficit]]** arises from the economic cycle. In a recession, tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises. It goes away as the economy recovers.
- A **[[structural deficit|structural deficit]]** would remain even with no output gap. Spending is simply higher than taxes over the cycle.

## Consequences of deficits

**Possible benefits:**

- Supports AD and jobs in a recession.
- Pays for investment that raises long-run growth.

**Possible costs:**

- **Debt interest:** money that can't be spent on services. This has an opportunity cost.
- **Crowding out:** government borrowing may raise interest rates, which reduces private investment.
- **Inflation,** if the economy is near full capacity.
- **Worse current account,** as extra spending pulls in imports.
- **A burden on future taxpayers,** who may have to pay more.
- **Loss of confidence,** which could raise the cost of borrowing.

## Consequences of surpluses

A surplus reduces debt and interest payments, and it gives room to respond to a future crisis. But it can slow growth, as it takes spending out of the economy, particularly if it comes from cuts in public investment.

## Does the size of the debt matter?

It depends on several things:

- **Size against GDP:** a larger economy can carry more debt.
- **Interest rates against growth:** if the economy grows faster than the interest paid, the debt can fall as a share of GDP.
- **Who holds it:** debt owed abroad takes more income out of the country.
- **What it paid for:** debt that funds productive investment can pay for itself.
- **Credibility:** markets must be willing to lend.

## Rebalancing the budget

Governments can reduce a deficit by cutting spending, raising taxes or growing the economy. Each has its problems. **Austerity**, like the cuts in the UK from 2010, can reduce deficits, but it may also lower demand and growth, which reduces tax revenue. How large the effect is remains a matter of debate. Keynesians warn of cutting spending in a downturn, while others stress the risks of rising debt.

## The Office for Budget Responsibility

The **[[office for budget responsibility|Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR)]]** was set up in 2010. It's an independent body that produces official forecasts of the economy and public finances, and it judges whether the government is on course to meet its fiscal rules. It doesn't set policy. It exists to stop governments from using forecasts that are too optimistic.

At the time of writing, the UK's rules require the current budget to be in balance by the third year of the forecast, and debt to be falling as a share of GDP by that year.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain how a budget deficit could harm economic performance. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** A budget deficit means that government spending is above tax revenue, so the government must borrow.

**Application:** UK public sector net debt was 94.0% of GDP in September 2026, so debt interest is a large cost.

**Analysis:** Heavy borrowing can raise interest rates, since the government competes for funds. This makes it dearer for firms to borrow, so private investment falls, which is crowding out. Lower investment reduces long-run growth. Debt interest also takes up money that could fund schools or roads. If the economy has little spare capacity, the deficit could also add to inflation.
:::
