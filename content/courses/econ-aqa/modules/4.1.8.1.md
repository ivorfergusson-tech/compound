## The three functions of prices

The **[[price mechanism]]** is the way prices allocate resources in a market economy. Prices do three jobs.

**Rationing.** When a good is scarce, a higher price limits demand to those willing and able to pay. The price decides who gets it.

**Signalling.** Prices carry information. A rising price tells producers that consumers want more, and a falling price says the opposite.

**Incentive.** Higher prices and profits encourage firms to make more, and they encourage workers to move to where they're needed. Lower prices encourage consumers to buy more.

Here's how they work together. If demand for electric cars rises, the price rises. That **rations** the cars that exist, **signals** that people want more of them, and gives firms an **incentive** to shift resources into making them.

## What, how and for whom

Every economy must answer three questions. In a market economy, incentives answer them:

- **What to produce:** what consumers are willing to pay for, since that's what earns a profit.
- **How to produce:** the cheapest method, since that raises profit.
- **For whom:** those with the income to pay.

No central planner is needed. Buyers and sellers each act in their own interest, and prices coordinate them.

## An impersonal method

The price mechanism is often called **impersonal**: it doesn't ask who you are or why you want something. There are two sides to that.

**In favour:** it can't play favourites, and no official decides who deserves what. It needs no one to collect vast amounts of information.

**Against:** it responds to ability to pay, not need. A rich person's want for a second home counts for more than a poor person's need for a first one. Those with little money have few "votes" in the market.

## Advantages and disadvantages

**Advantages:**

- It's automatic and needs little administration.
- It gives firms a reason to be efficient and to innovate.
- Consumers have choice and influence over what's produced.
- It adjusts quickly to change.

**Disadvantages:**

- It can give very unequal outcomes.
- Some goods wouldn't be provided at all, or not in the right amounts, as you'll see in the next lessons.
- It ignores effects on third parties, such as pollution.
- Prices can be unstable, which makes life hard for producers and consumers.

## Extending markets into new areas

Should everything have a price? Some people argue that bringing markets into new areas of life isn't always a good thing, because it can **change the nature of the activity**.

The classic case is **blood**. In the UK, donors give blood without being paid. Paying for it would make the gift a sale. That might attract more donors, or it might:

- Weaken the feeling of giving freely, so some donors stop.
- Change the incentives, so that people have a reason to hide health problems.
- Raise questions about whether it's right to buy and sell parts of the body.

Similar debates apply to selling organs, paying children for good marks and charging for a place in a queue. In favour, a price can increase supply and reward people. Against, some things may lose their value when they're bought.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain how the price mechanism could respond to a rise in demand for a product. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** The price mechanism allocates resources through the rationing, signalling and incentive functions of prices.

**Application:** Suppose demand for electric cars rises, as more drivers want to switch from petrol.

**Analysis:** At the old price there's excess demand, so the price rises. This rations the existing supply to those most willing to pay. It also signals to firms that consumers want more, and the higher profits give them an incentive to move resources from other uses into making electric cars. So supply extends until a new equilibrium is reached, and more resources are allocated to what consumers want.
:::

Why it scores: it uses all three functions in order, within one real example.
