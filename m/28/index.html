Before you read

An airline knows it will need to buy millions of litres of jet fuel next year. A sudden jump in oil prices could wipe out its profit.

The idea in one sentence

A derivative is a contract whose value comes from something else (a share, a commodity, an interest rate); they let people transfer risk to someone willing to take it, which is useful for hedging, but because they're often leveraged, they also make it easy to take huge, hidden bets.

What a derivative is

A derivative is a contract whose value is derived from something else, called the underlying: a share price, an index, a currency, a commodity like wheat or oil, an interest rate, or even the chance a company defaults.

You don't own the underlying. You own a promise about it: to buy, to sell, or to swap payments depending on what it does. Derivatives are traded on exchanges (standardised contracts) or privately between banks and companies, called "over the counter".

The three main types

Futures (and forwards)

A future is an agreement to buy or sell something at a fixed price on a future date. A forward is the same idea, agreed privately rather than on an exchange.

  • A wheat farmer worried that prices will fall before harvest can sell wheat futures now, locking in today's price.
  • A bakery worried prices will rise can buy futures, locking in its cost.

Back to the question at the start: the airline can buy oil or jet-fuel futures (or similar contracts) now, fixing much of next year's fuel cost. If prices soar, the gain on the futures offsets the higher cost of fuel. If prices fall, it loses on the futures but pays less for fuel. Either way, it has swapped uncertainty for a known cost.

Options

An option gives the buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell at a set price (the strike) by a set date. The buyer pays a premium for that right.

  • A call option is the right to buy. It pays off if the price rises above the strike.
  • A put option is the right to sell. It pays off if the price falls below the strike.

For the buyer, the most they can lose is the premium. That makes options work like insurance: a put on shares you own limits how much you can lose, in exchange for a premium, just as car insurance costs a premium whether or not you crash.

The seller (writer) of the option collects the premium but takes the other side. Someone who sells a call without owning the shares faces losses with no upper limit if the price shoots up.

Swaps

A swap is an agreement to exchange streams of payments. The most common is an interest rate swap: one side pays a fixed rate and receives a floating rate (one that moves with market rates), and the other does the opposite.

Example: a company with a floating-rate loan fears rates will rise. It enters a swap to pay fixed and receive floating. The floating payments it receives cover its loan's floating interest, so effectively it now pays a fixed rate: like switching from a tracker mortgage to a fix, without changing the loan.

There are also currency swaps and credit default swaps (CDS), which pay out if a borrower defaults: effectively insurance on a bond.

Hedging vs speculating

The same contract can be used two ways:

  • Hedging means using a derivative to reduce a risk you already have: the airline's fuel, the farmer's harvest, a company's loan rate, an exporter's foreign currency income.
  • Speculating means using it to bet on a price move, often with leverage, to make money from being right.

Both are legal and markets need both: speculators take the other side of hedgers' trades. The danger comes when speculation is huge, leveraged, hidden or misunderstood.

When derivatives blow up

  • Barings Bank (1995): a single trader in Singapore, Nick Leeson, built up huge hidden futures positions and hid the losses. When the bets went wrong, the losses (around £800 million) broke Britain's oldest merchant bank, which was sold for £1.
  • The 2008 crisis: credit default swaps let firms sell vast amounts of insurance on mortgage-linked bonds without holding enough capital to pay out. When US house prices fell, the insurer AIG needed a huge US government rescue, and nobody knew who owed what to whom (module 32).
  • 2022 UK pensions: the LDI strategies in module 24 used derivatives and borrowing linked to gilts; when yields jumped, collateral calls forced fire sales.

Common threads: leverage (small moves, huge losses), complexity (hard to see the risk), and counterparty risk (the other side can't pay). After 2008, rules pushed many derivatives into central clearing houses and required more collateral and reporting.

Derivatives and you

Ordinary people meet derivatives more than they think:

  • CFDs and spread bets (module 7) are derivatives. Most retail accounts lose money on them.
  • Fixed-rate mortgages are often funded by banks using swaps.
  • Pensions and funds may use derivatives to hedge currency or interest rate risk.
  • Options trading on some apps is offered to retail customers. It's complex and risky; most beginners should avoid it.

The common mistake

"Derivatives are just gambling."

They can be used to gamble, but their original and still main job is moving risk from people who don't want it to people willing to carry it: farmers, airlines, exporters, mortgage lenders and pension schemes all rely on them. Like leverage, they're a tool. The harm comes from using them without understanding, at too large a size, or hiding the risk.

In real life

  • Treat options and CFDs on apps as high risk. Most retail traders lose, and losses on sold options can exceed what you put in.
  • When you read about a company's "hedging", it usually means derivatives used to reduce currency, fuel or interest rate risk.
  • In interviews, be ready to explain a call, a put, a future and a swap in one sentence each.
  • Red flag: "guaranteed income" from selling options, or complex products you can't explain in plain words.

Think about it

Why would anyone sell insurance-like options if they could face big losses?

Most options expire worthless, so sellers collect premiums most of the time. It's a steady income with occasional big losses, like an insurance company. Done carefully, with capital and limits, it can work; done with leverage and no limits, one bad move can wipe out years of premiums.

Why did regulators push derivatives into central clearing houses after 2008?

In 2008, nobody could see who owed what across a web of private contracts, so one failure threatened many others. A clearing house stands between both sides, collects collateral daily and makes exposures visible, reducing the chance of a chain reaction.
