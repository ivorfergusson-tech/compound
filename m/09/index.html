Recap

In module 5 you followed a £25,000 salary down to about £1,793 a month of net pay. In module 1 you saw that money left in a low-paying account loses spending power to inflation. This module is about what happens next: planning where that £1,793 goes, and where the part you save should live.

Before you read

You take home £1,800 a month and want £1,500 for a car in a year's time. You can save £150 a month.

The idea in one sentence

A budget decides where your money goes before you spend it; savings then need a home that's safe, easy to reach when you need it, and paying as much interest as you can get.

What a budget is

A budget is a plan for your money: what comes in, what goes out, and what's left. It isn't a diet. It's a way of making sure the things you care about get paid for before the money drifts away on things you don't.

Every budget has three parts:

  1. Income: your net pay, plus anything else regular (a second job, a side hustle after tax).
  2. Spending: split into needs (rent or board, bills, travel to work, food, minimum debt payments) and wants (eating out, subscriptions, clothes, holidays).
  3. What's left: saving, investing, or paying off debt faster.

The trick is the order. Most people spend, then save whatever's left, which is often nothing. A budget flips it: decide the saving first, on payday, and live on the rest. People call this paying yourself first. A standing order that moves money to savings the day after payday does it automatically.

A starting split: 50/30/20

A common starting point is the 50/30/20 split: about 50% of take-home pay on needs, 30% on wants, 20% on saving or extra debt payments.

On £1,793.30 a month that's roughly £897 for needs, £538 for wants and £359 for saving.

It's a rule of thumb, not a law. Average UK private rent is £1,406 a month (September 2026, provisional (England £1,466, Wales £848, Scotland £1,011)), which on its own is more than most first salaries' "needs" share, so people renting alone in expensive areas often can't hit 50%. Living at home and paying board, the numbers look very different. Use the split as a check, not a target to feel bad about.

Tracking: where does it actually go?

You can't plan spending you can't see. Two ways to find out:

  • Look back. Download a month or two of your bank statement and sort each payment into needs, wants, saving. Most people find one or two surprises: subscriptions they forgot, or small daily spends that add up.
  • Look forward. Many banking apps sort spending into categories automatically and let you set up "pots" or "spaces" for bills, spending and saving.

Small regular spends matter because they repeat. £3.50 a day on coffee and snacks on workdays is about £70 a month, or £840 a year. That might be worth it to you, which is fine. The point is to choose it.

Current accounts

A current account is your everyday account: pay goes in, bills and card payments go out. Things to know:

  • It usually pays little or no interest, so it's a poor place for savings.
  • Many come with an arranged overdraft: an agreed amount you can go below £0. It's borrowing, and it's expensive: big banks typically charge about 40% EAR (2026; some app banks charge 15–39% depending on your credit record). Module 13 covers it.
  • You can switch banks for free using the Current Account Switch Service, which moves your payments and direct debits over for you. Banks sometimes pay you to switch.
  • Banks offer student and graduate accounts with interest-free overdrafts. Useful, but plan how you'll clear the overdraft before the interest-free period ends.

Where to keep savings

Savings accounts come in a few main types. The trade-off is nearly always access versus rate: the more you promise to leave the money alone, the more the bank may pay.

  • Easy-access: pay in and take out whenever you like. The rate is usually variable, so it can change. Top rates are about 4.5–5.0% AER (late October 2026; several only with a temporary bonus).
  • Notice accounts: you must give notice (say 30, 60 or 95 days) before taking money out.
  • Fixed-rate savers (bonds): you lock money away for a set time (often 1 to 5 years) at a fixed rate. Usually you can't take it out early, or there's a penalty. Good for money you definitely won't need.
  • Regular savers: high headline rates, but you can only pay in a limited amount each month (say £200), usually for a year. Read the small print below.
  • Cash ISAs: any of the above, but the interest is tax-free (module 17).

AER: comparing rates fairly

Banks quote savings rates as an AER, the Annual Equivalent Rate. It shows what you'd earn in a year including compounding, if you left the interest in. That lets you compare an account that pays interest monthly with one that pays yearly.

For example, 4.4% paid monthly (that's 4.4% ÷ 12 each month) works out at an AER of about 4.49%, because each month's interest earns interest too. If two accounts show the same AER, they pay the same over a year, however often they add the interest.

Watch out for bonuses. Some top easy-access rates include a temporary bonus for the first months. When it ends, the rate can drop sharply, so put a reminder in your calendar.

Watch out for regular savers. Say an account pays 7% on up to £200 a month for 12 months. Because you pay in gradually, only the first £200 earns interest for the full year; the last £200 earns it for just one month. On average only about half your money is in the account. Pay in £2,400 over the year and you earn about £90, which is about 3.75% of what you paid in, not 7%. Still decent, but not what the headline suggests.

Is my money safe in a bank?

Yes, up to a limit. If a UK-authorised bank or building society fails, the Financial Services Compensation Scheme (FSCS) pays back your deposits up to £120,000 (per person, per banking licence, from 1 December 2025 (was £85,000)).

The limit is per banking licence, not per brand. Some brands share a licence, so check before spreading large sums across "different" banks. For most people starting out, this never matters. Module 8 explains why banks can fail in the first place.

Rates matter more as the pot grows

Back to the question at the start. Saving £150 a month to reach £1,500 takes 10 months either way. You'd earn about £25 of interest at 4.5% and about £6 at 1%. So the better account earns roughly £19 more.

That might surprise you. For a small pot over a few months, the habit of saving matters far more than the rate. But rates add up as the pot grows: £5,000 sitting for a year earns about £225 at 4.5% and £50 at 1%, a £175 difference for five minutes' work switching account.

The emergency fund

Before saving for a car, a holiday or investing, most guidance says to build an emergency fund: money set aside for the unexpected, like a broken phone, a vet bill or a gap between jobs.

MoneyHelper, the government-backed guidance service, suggests aiming for three to six months of essential costs (MoneyHelper guidance). "Essential costs" means needs only: rent, bills, food, travel, debt payments. On £900 a month of needs, that's £2,700 to £5,400.

That can feel huge. Start smaller: even £500 to £1,000 stops most surprises turning into debt. Keep it in an easy-access account so you can reach it the same day, and keep it separate from your spending money so it doesn't quietly get spent.

If you have expensive debt such as a credit card or an unarranged overdraft, it often makes sense to keep a small buffer and put the rest towards clearing the debt first, because the debt costs far more than savings earn (module 13).

The common mistake

"I'll save whatever's left at the end of the month."

There's rarely anything left. Spending tends to expand to fill whatever's in the account. People who save consistently usually do it the other way round: a fixed amount moves to savings automatically on payday, and they live on the rest. Even £25 a month builds the habit, and you can raise it with every pay rise.

In real life

  • Pay yourself first: set a standing order to savings for the day after payday.
  • Check your savings rate against inflation (3.5%) and against top easy-access rates. If it's far below, switching takes minutes.
  • Diary any bonus end dates.
  • Keep the emergency fund separate and easy to reach.
  • Read regular saver and bonus small print before choosing on headline rate.
  • Red flag: any "savings" product promising a fixed high return that isn't from an FSCS-protected bank or building society. Check firms on the FCA register.

Think about it

Why do fixed-rate savers sometimes pay less than easy-access accounts?

Banks set fixed rates based on where they expect interest rates to go. If markets expect Bank Rate to fall, banks don't want to promise today's high rate for years, so fixed rates can be lower than easy-access ones. When rates are expected to rise, it's often the other way round. Module 12 explains Bank Rate.

Is it ever sensible to keep savings in a current account paying nothing?

A small buffer for bills and timing gaps, yes. But anything beyond a month or so of spending is usually better in a savings account, where it earns interest and is less tempting to spend.
