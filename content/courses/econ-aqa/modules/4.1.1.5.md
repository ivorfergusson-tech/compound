## Guess first

An economy can make only two things: food and machines. If it puts every resource into food, it makes 100 tonnes of food and no machines.

:::guess {"prompt":"If it then moves half its resources into machines, will it still make 50 tonnes of food, or more, or less? Why?","kind":"text","id":"econ-aqa:4.1.1.5-guess"}
:::

:::callout idea
A production possibility diagram shows the most an economy can make of two goods with its resources and technology, so on one picture you can see scarcity, choice, opportunity cost, unemployment and economic growth.
:::

## What the diagram shows

A **[[production possibility frontier]]** (PPF, also called a production possibility boundary or curve) shows **every combination of two goods an economy can make** when it uses **all its resources** fully and efficiently, with the technology it has.

To keep it simple, we pretend the economy makes only two goods (or two groups of goods, like consumer goods and capital goods). Each point on the diagram is one combination of the two.

- **On the boundary:** all resources are in use. The economy is getting the most it can. These points are attainable.
- **Inside the boundary:** some resources are unused or wasted: workers unemployed, factories idle. Attainable, but the economy could make more of both.
- **Outside the boundary:** not possible with today's resources and technology. That's **scarcity** on a diagram.

## Choice and opportunity cost

Moving **along** the boundary means **reallocating** resources from one good to the other. To get more consumer goods, the economy must give up some capital goods. What it gives up is the **opportunity cost**.

You can read it off the diagram. Say an economy moves from 80 capital goods and 40 consumer goods to 60 capital goods and 60 consumer goods. It gained 20 consumer goods and gave up 20 capital goods, so the opportunity cost of each extra consumer good is 20 ÷ 20 = **1 capital good**.

**Why is the boundary usually bowed out (concave)?** Because resources aren't equally good at making everything. The first workers moved into making consumer goods are the ones best at it and worst at making machines, so little is lost. As more move across, you're moving people and machines that were really good at the other job, so each extra consumer good costs more and more capital goods. This is **increasing opportunity cost**. Watch the "Cost of 1 more" box rise as you slide right.

If resources were equally good at both, the boundary would be a **straight line**: the opportunity cost would be the same at every point (constant opportunity cost).

## Unemployment and growth

**Unemployment of resources:** a point **inside** the boundary means some resources (labour, land or capital) are idle, for example in a recession. Moving from inside towards the boundary gives **more of both goods**, with **no opportunity cost**, because the resources weren't being used for anything.

**Economic growth** is shown by the whole boundary **shifting outwards**. It happens when the economy gets:

- **more resources:** more workers (migration, a rising population), new natural resources, more capital (investment in factories and machines);
- **better resources:** a better trained and healthier workforce;
- **better technology:** new ways of making more with the same resources.

The boundary can shift **inwards** too, after a war, a natural disaster or a big loss of resources.

The capital and consumer goods choice matters here. An economy that makes **more capital goods today** (fewer consumer goods now) will usually see its boundary shift out **faster** in future, because it has more machines to produce with. The opportunity cost of future growth is consumption today.

## Productive and allocative efficiency

This is the part students most often get wrong.

- **[[productive efficiency]]** means making the most output possible from the resources available, with nothing wasted. **Every point on the boundary is productively efficient**, because you can't make more of one good without making less of the other.
- **[[allocative efficiency]]** means resources are allocated to make the combination of goods that **people value most**: the mix that best matches what consumers want. Of all the points on the boundary, usually **only one** is allocatively efficient.

So **all points on the boundary are productively efficient, but not all are allocatively efficient.** An economy making only capital goods and no food is on its boundary, so productively efficient, but nobody would call that the best use of its resources: people would starve. A point inside the boundary is neither.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** With the help of a production possibility diagram, explain how investment in new capital goods today can lead to economic growth. (9 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** A production possibility boundary shows the maximum combinations of capital goods and consumer goods an economy can produce when all its resources are fully and efficiently employed. Economic growth is an increase in the economy's productive capacity, shown by an outward shift of the boundary.

**Application:** On the diagram, an economy that chooses point B, with more capital goods and fewer consumer goods than point A, is investing more in machinery, factories and infrastructure.

**Analysis:** The opportunity cost of this choice is the consumer goods given up today, so living standards may be lower in the short run. However, the extra capital increases the economy's stock of productive resources. Workers with more and better machines can produce more output per hour, so productive capacity rises and the boundary shifts outwards from PPF1 to PPF2. In future, the economy can then produce more of both capital and consumer goods than it could before.

**Evaluation:** How much growth results depends on whether the investment is well chosen, and on whether there are enough skilled workers to use the new capital. If demand is weak, the economy may also not use its new capacity, staying inside the new boundary.
:::

Why it scores: it defines both terms, uses the diagram's labels (A, B, PPF1, PPF2) in the writing, links the choice to opportunity cost, builds a clear chain from more capital to more output to an outward shift, and ends with conditions that weaken the argument.
