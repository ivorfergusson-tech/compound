:::callout idea
An exchange rate is a price, set by demand and supply when it floats and by governments when it's fixed, and each system trades flexibility against stability.
:::

## Exchange rates in a floating system

An **[[exchange rate|exchange rate]]** is the price of one currency in terms of another. In a **[[floating exchange rate|freely floating system]]**, it's set by market demand and supply.

**Demand for pounds** comes from:

- Foreign buyers of UK exports.
- Foreign investment in the UK.
- Speculators who expect the pound to rise.

**Supply of pounds** comes from:

- UK buyers of imports.
- UK investment abroad.
- Speculators who expect the pound to fall.

**What moves the rate?**

- **Interest rates:** higher UK rates attract money, so the pound rises.
- **Inflation:** higher UK inflation makes exports less competitive, so the pound falls.
- **Trade performance:** stronger exports raise demand for pounds.
- **Confidence and speculation.**
- **Foreign investment.**

A rise in a floating rate is an **appreciation**, and a fall is a **depreciation**.

## How governments can intervene

Governments and central banks can influence the rate by:

- **Buying or selling currency:** selling reserves of foreign currency to buy pounds raises the pound. Selling pounds lowers it.
- **Changing interest rates.**
- **Using capital controls,** which limit money flows.

## Fixed exchange rates

In a **[[fixed exchange rate|fixed system]]**, the government sets the value against another currency or a group of currencies, and defends it. A fall in the fixed rate is a **devaluation**, and a rise is a **revaluation**.

| | Fixed | Floating |
|---|---|---|
| **Advantages** | Certainty for traders and investors | Automatic adjustment of the current account |
| | Discipline against inflation | Free to set interest rates at home |
| | Less speculation | No need for large reserves |
| **Disadvantages** | Interest rates can't be used for home goals | Uncertainty for firms |
| | Needs large reserves | Can swing sharply through speculation |
| | Can be attacked if it isn't credible | Depreciation can add to inflation |

**Managed floating** is a middle way, where a currency mostly floats but the central bank sometimes acts.

The UK tried a fixed system in the European Exchange Rate Mechanism, and left in 1992 when it could no longer defend the pound. The pound has floated since.

## Currency unions

A **[[currency union|currency union]]** means that a group of countries shares one currency and one central bank. The **eurozone** is the main example, with the European Central Bank setting one interest rate.

**Advantages:**

- **No exchange rate risk** between members, which helps trade and investment.
- **Lower costs:** no changing money.
- **Price transparency:** easier to compare prices, so competition rises.
- **Stability:** less risk of speculative attack.

**Disadvantages:**

- **Loss of monetary policy:** one interest rate must suit all members, even if their economies are different.
- **No exchange rate adjustment:** a country that loses competitiveness can't devalue, so wages and jobs must take the strain.
- **Limits on fiscal policy,** through agreed rules.
- **Costs of changing over.**

The eurozone debt crisis from about 2010 showed these problems. Countries such as Greece couldn't devalue, and they faced deep recessions.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of a floating exchange rate. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** In a floating system, the exchange rate is set by the demand for and supply of the currency.

**Application:** The pound floats against the dollar and the euro.

**Analysis:** One advantage is automatic adjustment: if the UK runs a large deficit, the supply of pounds rises, so the pound falls, which makes exports cheaper and imports dearer, and helps to correct the deficit. One disadvantage is uncertainty: a sudden fall makes it hard for firms to plan, as they can't be sure of the price they'll pay or receive, which can reduce trade and investment.
:::
