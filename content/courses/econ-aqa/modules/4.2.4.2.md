## Commercial and investment banks

A **[[commercial bank|commercial bank]]** serves households and firms. It takes deposits and makes loans.

An **[[investment bank|investment bank]]** works in financial markets. It helps firms and governments issue shares and bonds, advises on mergers and trades in financial assets.

Many banks do both. That can increase **systemic risk**, because losses from risky trading could threaten ordinary savers' deposits. Since 2019, the UK's largest banks have had to **ring-fence** their retail banking from their riskier investment banking.

Other institutions, such as insurers and pension funds, also operate in financial markets.

## The main functions of a commercial bank

- Accepting deposits and keeping money safe.
- Lending to households and firms.
- Providing payment services.
- Providing other financial services, such as insurance and foreign currency.

## A bank's balance sheet

A balance sheet shows what a bank owns and what it owes.

| Liabilities (what it owes) | Assets (what it owns or is owed) |
|---|---|
| Customers' deposits | Cash and balances at the central bank |
| Borrowing from other banks | Short-term loans to other banks |
| Shareholders' funds (capital) | Bonds and other securities |
| | Loans to customers, such as mortgages |

Your deposit is a **liability** for the bank, as it owes you the money. Your loan is an **asset**, as you owe it to the bank.

Assets are often listed from most to least liquid. **Liquid** means that an asset can be turned into cash quickly, with little loss of value.

## The objectives of a bank

- **Liquidity:** having enough cash to meet withdrawals.
- **Profitability:** earning a return for shareholders.
- **Security:** avoiding losses, so that it stays solvent.

These conflict. The **most profitable** assets, such as long-term loans, are the **least liquid** and often the **riskiest**. Holding lots of cash is safe, but it earns little. A bank has to balance these aims. Leaning too far towards profit is how many have got into trouble.

## How banks create credit

Most money in the UK isn't notes and coins: it's bank deposits. And banks create most of it, **when they lend**.

When a bank makes you a £10,000 loan, it adds £10,000 to your account. It hasn't taken the money from somebody else's deposit. A new deposit has been created, so the money supply has risen. When the loan is repaid, the money disappears again.

Because of this:

- Each loan creates a deposit that can be spent.
- That spending ends up as a deposit at another bank, which helps it to lend as well.

Banks can't lend without limit. They're limited by:

- Whether people want to borrow, and whether borrowers look able to repay.
- Their need to stay profitable and to keep enough liquidity.
- Rules on capital and liquidity.
- The interest rate set by the central bank.

You won't be asked to calculate the credit multiplier.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain the conflict between profitability and liquidity for a commercial bank. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Liquidity is the ability to turn assets into cash quickly, with little loss. Profitability is earning a good return for shareholders.

**Application:** A bank holds some of its assets as cash, and many of them as long-term mortgages.

**Analysis:** Mortgages earn much more interest than cash, so lending more raises profit. But the money is tied up for years, and the bank can't get it back quickly if savers want to withdraw. Holding more cash meets withdrawals, but earns almost nothing. So more of one tends to mean less of the other, and the bank must find a balance.
:::

Why it scores: it compares two real assets and shows how the trade-off works.
