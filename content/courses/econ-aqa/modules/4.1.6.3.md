## Guess first

A surgeon earns many times more than a care worker, and both do work that matters.

:::guess {"prompt":"Using demand and supply, why do you think their pay is so different?","kind":"text","id":"econ-aqa:4.1.6.3-guess"}
:::

:::callout idea
In a perfectly competitive labour market, wages are set by demand and supply, so relative wages reflect workers' productivity and how many people can do the job.
:::

## The perfectly competitive labour market

This model assumes:

- Many employers and many workers, none of whom can affect the wage.
- Workers with the same skills.
- Free movement between jobs, and no barriers such as unions.
- Perfect information about jobs and wages.

So the **market** sets the wage, and each firm is a **wage taker**. It can hire all the workers it wants at that wage, so it hires up to where **MRP = wage**.

## How the wage is set

The wage is set where the demand for labour meets the supply of labour.

- If the wage is **above** equilibrium, there are more workers than jobs. Unemployed workers accept less, so the wage falls.
- If the wage is **below** equilibrium, firms can't fill their jobs, so they offer more.

## Relative wages

**[[relative wages|Relative wages]]** are the wages of one job compared with another. In this model, market forces explain them:

- **High pay:** high demand, from a high MRP, and low supply.
- **Low pay:** low demand, or a large supply of people who could do the job.

Back to the surgeon and the care worker. The difference isn't a judgement on who matters more. Few people can become surgeons, so supply is small, and their work brings in a high MRP. Many people can do care work, and funding limits what employers can pay.

Market forces also move workers. If one job pays more, workers move towards it, which raises supply and narrows the gap, provided they're able to move.

## Real labour markets

All real-world labour markets are imperfectly competitive, to a greater or lesser extent. Skills differ, workers can't always move, unions and big employers have power, and information is poor. Non-monetary factors matter too, so wage gaps can persist.

The model is still useful: it shows the forces at work, and it's a yardstick for judging real markets. The next lessons relax its assumptions.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain how market forces could cause footballers to earn more than nurses. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** In a competitive labour market, the wage is set by the demand for and supply of labour. Demand depends on marginal revenue product.

**Application:** Top footballers attract large audiences, whereas the number of qualified nurses is far larger.

**Analysis:** A top player brings in a lot of revenue from tickets, television and sponsorship, so their MRP is very high. Their rare talent means supply is small and inelastic. Nurses have valuable skills, but there are many more of them, and much of their pay is set by a public employer. So demand and supply put the footballer's wage far higher, whatever the value of each job to society.
:::

Why it scores: it uses MRP and supply, and it separates the market wage from social value.
