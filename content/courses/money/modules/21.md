## Three layers of retirement money

In the UK, retirement income usually comes from up to three places:

1. **The [[state pension|State Pension]]**, paid by the government from State Pension age, based on your National Insurance record.
2. **Workplace pensions**, which you and your employer pay into while you work.
3. **Personal savings and pensions**: ISAs, a SIPP (a personal pension you run yourself), property and so on.

The State Pension on its own is a floor, not a comfortable retirement. The full new State Pension is {{fig:statePension+}}. For many people that's well under half of what they earned before retiring. Workplace pensions fill the gap.

State Pension age is currently {{fig:statePensionAge+}}, so most people starting work now won't get it until 67 or later.

## Auto-enrolment

Since 2012, employers have had to **automatically enrol** eligible staff into a workplace pension. This is **[[auto-enrolment]]**. You're enrolled if you:

- are aged **22** or over and under State Pension age,
- earn over {{fig:aeTrigger+}},
- and work in the UK.

If you're younger or earn less, you can usually **ask** to join, and if you earn over £6,240 your employer must pay in too.

Minimum contributions are {{fig:aeMinimum+}} of your **qualifying earnings**: pay from {{fig:aeBand+}}. Many employers pay more than the minimum, and some will match extra contributions from you. Always ask.

You can **opt out**, but you'll be re-enrolled about every three years and can rejoin any time.

## Free money: the employer contribution

The **[[employer contribution]]** is extra pay you only get if you stay in. Opting out doesn't put that money in your pocket; it just disappears.

On £25,000 with the minimums:

- Qualifying earnings: £25,000 − £6,240 = **£18,760**.
- You pay 5% = **£938 a year** (£78.17 a month).
- Your employer pays 3% = **£562.80 a year** (£46.90 a month).

## Tax relief: the government adds too

Money you pay into a pension gets **[[tax relief]]**: the government gives back the income tax you'd have paid on it. How it shows depends on the scheme:

- **Net pay arrangement:** your contribution is taken **before** income tax, so you pay less tax. (That's how the take-home calculator in module 5 models it.)
- **Relief at source:** your contribution is taken after tax, and the pension company claims 20% back from HMRC and adds it to your pot. You pay £80, your pot gets £100.

Either way, for a basic-rate taxpayer each £1 in your pot costs you about **80p**. Higher-rate taxpayers get 40% relief (some of it claimed back through their tax return).

Back to the question at the start. Your £938 a year costs you about £938 × 0.8 = **£750 a year, about £62.53 a month**. For that, **£1,500.80 a year** goes into your pension. Each pound you give up becomes about **£2** in the pot before any growth.

(Pension contributions don't save National Insurance unless your employer uses **salary sacrifice**, where you agree to a lower salary in return for a bigger employer contribution. From April 2029, the National Insurance saving on salary sacrifice will be capped at {{fig:salarySacrificeCap+}}.)

## Compounding over a working life

Pensions are usually invested in funds that hold shares and bonds (modules 2, 10 and 14), so they can grow over decades. The calculator below shows a pot in **today's money**, assuming pay keeps up with inflation and the pot grows 3% a year above inflation. On those assumptions, with £1,500.80 a year going in:

- starting at **22** and stopping at 67: about **£143,000** in today's money;
- starting at **32**: about **£93,000**.

Ten years' delay costs around **£50,000**, the same lesson as module 1. The early years' contributions get the longest to grow.

## Getting it out

You can't usually take money from a workplace or personal pension until {{fig:pensionAccessAge+}}. When you do:

- Usually **25%** can be taken **tax-free**.
- The rest is taxed as income when you take it, like a salary.
- You can take it as a regular income (an **annuity** you buy with the pot, or **drawdown** where the pot stays invested), as lump sums, or a mix.

The lock-up is the price of the tax relief and employer money. It also protects your future self from spending it early.

## Your State Pension record

To get the full new State Pension you usually need **35 qualifying years** of National Insurance, and at least 10 to get anything. You get a qualifying year by:

- paying NI on earnings above the threshold,
- **or** getting NI **credits**, for example while claiming certain benefits, caring for a child under 12 with Child Benefit, or caring for someone.

Gaps can sometimes be filled by paying **voluntary contributions**. You can check your record and forecast for free on gov.uk.

## SIPPs and the annual allowance

A **[[sipp|SIPP]]** (Self-Invested Personal Pension) is a pension you open yourself, choosing your own investments. Useful for the self-employed, who have no employer pension, or for people who want more control. Contributions get the same tax relief.

There's a limit on how much can go into pensions with tax relief each year: the **annual allowance**, {{fig:annualAllowance+}}. You also can't get relief on more than you earn in a year. Few people starting out come near either.

## Lost pensions

People change jobs many times, and each job can leave a small pot behind. Keep a list of every scheme. You can combine old pots into one (check for fees or valuable guarantees first). If you lose track, the government's free Pension Tracing Service can help find them.

## The common mistake

**"I'm young, I'll sort out a pension later."**

Later is expensive. A pound saved at 22 has 45 years to grow; one saved at 42 has 25. And opting out costs you the employer's money and the tax relief right now, not just growth later. Staying in at the minimum from the first job is one of the easiest good money decisions there is.

## In real life

- **Stay in** your workplace pension unless you truly can't afford the contributions.
- **Ask about matching:** if your employer adds more when you pay more, take as much as you can afford.
- **Check where it's invested.** Most people are in a default fund. That's fine for many, but look at the fees and what it holds.
- **Keep a list** of every pension, with the provider and policy number.
- **Check your State Pension forecast** on gov.uk once in a while.
- **Red flag:** anyone offering to "unlock" your pension before 55 (57 from 2028), or cold calls about pensions. These are almost always scams, and you can lose most of the pot to tax charges and fraud. Check firms on the FCA register.

## Think about it

:::reveal Why do governments give tax relief on pensions but lock the money up until later life?
They want people to save enough for retirement so fewer depend only on the State Pension. Tax relief is the carrot; the access age is the condition. Without the lock, many people would spend the money early and the relief wouldn't achieve its aim.
:::

:::reveal Should someone with expensive credit card debt still stay in their workplace pension?
Usually yes for the minimum, because the employer contribution and tax relief roughly double their money straight away, which beats almost any debt interest. But they should avoid paying extra into the pension until the expensive debt is cleared.
:::

## Try it

1. If you have a job, find your pension provider and log in. What's your pot worth, and what's it invested in?
2. Ask your employer: do they pay more than 3%? Will they match extra?
3. Put your own numbers in the pension calculator. What happens if you add 1% more?
4. Check your State Pension forecast at gov.uk.
