## Inflation, deflation and disinflation

- **[[inflation|Inflation]]** is a sustained rise in the general price level.
- **[[deflation|Deflation]]** is a sustained **fall** in the general price level, so inflation is negative.
- **[[disinflation|Disinflation]]** is a fall in the **rate** of inflation. Prices are still rising, only more slowly.

Be careful with one phrase. **Deflationary policies** are policies that reduce AD. They don't necessarily cause deflation. They may just slow inflation.

## Demand-pull inflation

**[[demand-pull inflation|Demand-pull inflation]]** happens when AD grows faster than the economy can supply goods. Too much spending chases too few goods, so firms raise prices. It's most likely with a positive output gap.

Causes include low interest rates, tax cuts, rising confidence, strong exports and rapid growth in credit.

On a diagram, AD shifts right, so real output and the price level both rise.

## Cost-push inflation

**[[cost-push inflation|Cost-push inflation]]** happens when firms' costs rise and they pass these on as higher prices.

Causes include rising wages, higher prices for imported raw materials, a fall in the exchange rate and higher taxes on firms.

On a diagram, SRAS shifts left, so the price level rises and real output falls.

## The Fisher equation and monetarism

**Fisher's equation of exchange** is:

**MV = PQ**

- **M** is the money supply.
- **V** is the velocity of circulation: how often each pound is spent.
- **P** is the average price level.
- **Q** is real output, so PQ is nominal national income.

This is an identity: it must be true, as total spending equals the value of what's bought.

The **Quantity Theory of Money** adds two assumptions: V is fairly stable, and Q is set by the supply side and doesn't depend on M. So:

**If the money supply grows faster than real output, prices rise.**

This is the **monetarist** view: inflation is caused by too much money. Milton Friedman was its best-known supporter.

Try it. Raise M, and keep V and Q still.

Critics argue that V isn't stable, and that when there's spare capacity, more money may raise output and not prices.

## Expectations

Expectations can make inflation build on itself. If people expect prices to rise:

- Workers ask for higher pay to protect their real wages.
- Firms raise prices, expecting their costs and rivals' prices to rise.
- Consumers may buy now, before prices go up.

All this pushes prices up, so the expectations come true. This is the **wage-price spiral**. That's why central banks work hard to keep expectations anchored near the target.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Using the Fisher equation, explain the monetarist view of inflation. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** The Fisher equation is MV = PQ, where M is the money supply, V the velocity of circulation, P the price level and Q real output.

**Application:** Suppose the money supply grows by 10% in a year, while real output grows by 2%.

**Analysis:** Monetarists assume that V is fairly stable, and that Q is determined by the supply side. So if M rises, the extra spending must show up in P. With output growing by only 2%, most of the 10% rise in money goes into higher prices, so inflation is around 8%. So in this view, inflation is caused by the money supply growing faster than output.
:::

Why it scores: it defines the terms, states the assumptions and uses numbers.
