## Guess first

A stall sells 10 cakes a day at £3. To sell an 11th, it must cut the price of all its cakes to £2.90.

:::guess {"prompt":"How much extra money does the 11th cake really bring in? Is it £2.90?","kind":"text","id":"econ-aqa:4.1.4.6-guess"}
:::

:::callout idea
Revenue is what a firm earns from sales: average revenue is the price, so it's the firm's demand curve, and if a firm must cut its price to sell more, marginal revenue is less than the price.
:::

## Total, average and marginal revenue

- **[[total revenue|Total revenue]] (TR):** all the money from sales. **TR = price × quantity.**
- **[[average revenue|Average revenue]] (AR):** revenue per unit. **AR = TR ÷ quantity.** That's just the price.
- **[[marginal revenue|Marginal revenue]] (MR):** the **extra** revenue from selling **one more** unit. **MR = change in TR ÷ change in quantity.**

| Quantity | Price (AR) | Total revenue | Marginal revenue |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 10 | 10 | 10 |
| 2 | 9 | 18 | 8 |
| 3 | 8 | 24 | 6 |
| 4 | 7 | 28 | 4 |
| 5 | 6 | 30 | 2 |
| 6 | 5 | 30 | 0 |
| 7 | 4 | 28 | −2 |

## Why AR is the demand curve

The demand curve shows the price a firm can charge for each quantity. Average revenue **is** that price. So the **average revenue curve is the firm's demand curve**.

## How MR relates to AR

If a firm has to cut its price to sell more, it cuts the price on **all** units, not just the extra one. So:

- The extra unit brings in its own price.
- But the firm loses money on all the units it could have sold for more.

That's why **MR is below AR** for a firm with a downward-sloping demand curve. With a straight-line demand curve, **MR falls twice as fast as AR**.

In the opening example, the 11th cake brings in £2.90, but the first 10 cakes each lose 10p, so MR = £2.90 − £1 = **£1.90**.

## How MR relates to TR

Look at the table:

- While **MR is positive**, TR rises.
- When **MR is zero**, TR is at its **maximum**.
- When **MR is negative**, TR falls.

This links to price elasticity: on a straight-line demand curve, demand is elastic in the upper half, where MR is positive, and inelastic in the lower half, where MR is negative. TR is highest at the midpoint, where PED is −1.

## A price taker

The other case: a firm in perfect competition (4.1.5) can sell all it wants at the market price, but nothing above it. It doesn't need to cut its price to sell more, so its demand curve is horizontal, and **price = AR = MR**.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain why marginal revenue is less than average revenue for a firm facing a downward-sloping demand curve. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Average revenue is revenue per unit, which equals the price. Marginal revenue is the change in total revenue from selling one more unit.

**Application:** For example, a firm that sells 10 units at £5 earns £50. If it must cut its price to £4.80 to sell 11 units, it earns £52.80.

**Analysis:** The firm must lower the price on all units to sell one more. So the extra revenue from the 11th unit (£4.80) is partly offset by the 20p lost on each of the first 10. MR is therefore £2.80, which is less than the new price of £4.80.
:::

Why it scores: it defines both terms, proves the point with numbers, and explains the reason: the lower price applies to every unit.
