## Private and public goods

A **private good**, such as a sandwich, has two features:

- **Rival:** if you eat it, I can't.
- **Excludable:** the seller can stop you having it unless you pay.

A pure **[[public good]]** has the opposite features:

- **Non-rival:** one person's use doesn't reduce what's left for others.
- **Non-excludable:** nobody can be stopped from benefiting, whether they've paid or not.

Examples include national defence, street lighting, flood defences and lighthouses.

## The free-rider problem

The **[[free-rider problem]]** arises from non-excludability. A free rider enjoys a good without paying for it.

If you can benefit from the flood wall whether you pay or not, it makes sense not to pay and to hope that others do. If everyone thinks this way, little money is raised. Firms can't make a profit, so the market doesn't provide the good. This is a **missing market**, and an example of complete market failure.

Non-rivalry matters too. If an extra person can use a good at no extra cost, charging them would be inefficient, so the efficient price is zero, and no firm would supply at that price.

## Quasi-public goods

Few goods are pure public goods. A **[[quasi-public good]]** has some of the features of a public good, without all of them.

- **Roads** are usually free to use and open to all, but they get congested, so they become rival. Tolls can make them excludable.
- **Beaches** are mostly open to all, but they fill up in summer.
- **Parks** are similar. They could be fenced off with an entry charge.

## Technological change

Technology can change the nature of a good. **Television broadcasting** used to be non-excludable: anyone with an aerial could watch. Encryption and subscriptions now let firms stop non-payers from watching, so it's excludable, and it can be sold through the market.

Similarly, number-plate cameras make it easier to charge for using roads.

## The tragedy of the commons

Some resources are **rival** but **non-excludable**: common land, fish in the sea, or clean air. Nobody owns them, and nobody can be stopped from using them.

The **[[tragedy of the commons]]** is what happens next. Each user gains the full benefit of taking more, while the cost of depletion is shared by all. So each one has a reason to take more, even though everyone would be better off with limits. The resource is overused and may be destroyed.

This is relevant to environmental market failure:

- **Overfishing:** each boat wants a large catch, so stocks collapse.
- **Deforestation:** land is cleared without regard to the wider loss.
- **Climate change:** the atmosphere is a shared resource that nobody owns.

Compare this with a public good: there, the market provides too little. Here, the resource is used too much.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain why a market would fail to provide a pure public good. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** A pure public good is non-rival and non-excludable. The free-rider problem occurs when people benefit from a good without paying.

**Application:** Once a flood defence is built, it protects every home in the area.

**Analysis:** Because it's non-excludable, a firm can't stop people who haven't paid from benefiting. Each household has a reason to free ride, so few pay. The firm can't earn a profit, so it won't supply the good, although people value it. This results in a missing market, which is complete market failure, and so the government may provide it.
:::

Why it scores: it links non-excludability to free riding, then to the lack of profit and the missing market.
