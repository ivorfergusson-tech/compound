## Guess first

A government wants to cut carbon emissions by a fixed amount, as cheaply as it can.

:::guess {"prompt":"Would you tell each firm how much to cut, or find a way to let them trade? Why might it matter?","kind":"text","id":"econ-aqa:4.1.8.9b-guess"}
:::

:::callout idea
Besides using prices, governments can provide things directly, set rules, create property rights or build a market in pollution, and the best tool depends on the type of market failure.
:::

## State provision

**State provision** means the government supplies a good or service, usually funded by taxes.

It's the main answer to **public goods**, which the market wouldn't provide, such as defence and street lighting. It's also used for **merit goods**, such as NHS care and state schools.

**Strengths:** it provides what's missing, gives access whatever people's income, and can reach the socially best level of output.

**Weaknesses:** it's costly and has an opportunity cost, it's hard to know how much people want, there's little competition, so it can be inefficient, and with a zero price, demand can exceed supply, which leads to waiting lists.

## Regulation

**Regulation** means laws and rules that direct behaviour. Examples include bans on smoking in enclosed public places, age limits on alcohol and limits on emissions.

**Strengths:** it's simple, clear and works well when a harm is serious, because a ban leaves little room for doubt.

**Weaknesses:** it needs monitoring and enforcement, it treats all firms alike even if their costs differ, and it gives no reason to do better than the standard. Bans may create black markets.

## Extending property rights

The absence of property rights causes externalities. So one solution is to **extend them**: to give people clear ownership of a resource, so that they can charge for its use or go to court.

If a river is owned, the owner can stop polluters or demand payment. The external cost becomes a private one. This approach can also tackle the tragedy of the commons. Quotas that give fishing boats a right to a certain catch are one example.

**Weaknesses:** rights are hard to set for the air or the sea. Legal action is slow and costly, and many small victims may not be able to act together.

## Pollution permits

A **[[pollution permit]]** system creates a market in the right to pollute.

1. The government sets a **cap** on total emissions.
2. It issues permits up to that cap, by giving or selling them.
3. Firms that pollute must hold enough permits.
4. Firms can **trade** permits.

Firms that can cut emissions cheaply do so, and sell their spare permits. Firms for whom it's costly buy them. So the total cut is met at the **lowest overall cost**, and the cap can be lowered over time. The UK Emissions Trading Scheme works on this basis.

**Strengths:** total pollution is capped, firms have a reason to cut emissions, and the market finds the cheapest cuts.

**Weaknesses:** the cap may be set too high, the price of permits can swing, and monitoring costs money. Firms could also move abroad, which shifts pollution elsewhere.

## Choosing the right tool

| Failure | Possible tools |
|---|---|
| Public goods | State provision |
| Negative externalities | Taxes, regulation, permits, property rights |
| Positive externalities | Subsidies, state provision |
| Merit and demerit goods | Subsidies, taxes, regulation, information |
| Monopoly | Competition policy, regulation |

You should be able to judge both sides: the strengths and weaknesses of markets, and the case for and against intervention. Ask how big the failure is, whether the tool targets its cause, what it would cost and whether it could make matters worse. Often, a mix of tools works better than one alone.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain how tradable pollution permits can reduce pollution. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Tradable permits give the holder a right to emit a set amount of pollution. The government caps the total, and firms can buy and sell permits.

**Application:** A system like the UK Emissions Trading Scheme covers heavy emitters such as power generators.

**Analysis:** The cap directly limits total pollution, and it can be reduced each year. A firm that cuts its emissions can sell its spare permits, so it has a reason to invest in cleaner methods. Firms that pollute more must buy permits, which raises their costs and internalises some of the external cost. So pollution falls, and the cuts are made where they cost the least.
:::

Why it scores: it covers both the cap and the trading, which are the two parts of how it works.
