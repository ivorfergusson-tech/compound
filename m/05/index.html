Recap

In module 1 you saw that interest makes money grow and inflation shrinks what it buys. Before you can save or invest anything, though, the money has to reach you. This module follows a pay packet from your employer's bank account to yours, and shows what gets taken out on the way and why.

Before you read

You start a full-time job paying £25,000 a year. No student loan, no pension for now.

The idea in one sentence

What you're paid (gross) isn't what you get (net): income tax and National Insurance only bite on the part of your pay above a tax-free amount, and each band only taxes the slice of pay inside it.

Gross pay and net pay

Your gross pay is what your employer agrees to pay you before anything is taken off: "£25,000 a year" or "£12.71 an hour". Your net pay, or take-home pay, is what actually reaches your account.

The gap between them is made up of deductions. For most people the main ones are:

  • Income tax, which pays for public services.
  • National Insurance (NI), which builds your entitlement to the State Pension and some benefits.
  • Workplace pension contributions, if you're in one (module 21).
  • Student loan repayments, if you went to university and earn enough (module 25).

All of these show on your payslip, which your employer must give you each time you're paid. Payslips look different from firm to firm, but they all show gross pay, each deduction and net pay. Check yours every time: mistakes happen, and they're easier to fix early.

PAYE: tax taken before you see it

If you're an employee, you don't send tax to the government yourself. Your employer works it out and pays it to HMRC (the UK's tax authority) for you, every time you're paid. This system is called PAYE, Pay As You Earn.

It means most employees never fill in a tax return. It also means the employer needs to know how much tax-free pay you're entitled to, which is what your tax code tells them.

The Personal Allowance and your tax code

Most people can earn a certain amount each tax year before paying any income tax. This is the Personal Allowance: £12,570 (2026/27 tax year; shrinks by £1 for every £2 of income over £100,000).

The UK tax year runs from 6 April to 5 April, so "2026/27" means 6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027.

Your tax code tells your employer how much tax-free pay to give you. The most common code is 1257L:

  • 1257 means £12,570 of tax-free pay a year (drop the last digit).
  • L means you get the standard Personal Allowance.
  • An S at the front means Scottish rates, and a C means Welsh rates (which are currently the same as England's).

If you start a job without a P45 from a previous one, you might get an emergency code such as 1257L W1 or 1257L M1. That just means each pay period is taxed on its own until HMRC sorts out your record. Any overpaid tax normally comes back automatically, but it's worth checking your code in the HMRC app or your personal tax account if it looks odd.

Income tax: bands, not a flat rate

Above the Personal Allowance, income tax is charged in bands. In England, Wales and Northern Ireland they are 20% up to £50,270, 40% up to £125,140, 45% above (2026/27 tax year).

  • Basic rate, 20%, on pay from £12,571 to £50,270.
  • Higher rate, 40%, on pay from £50,271 to £125,140.
  • Additional rate, 45%, on pay above £125,140.

Each rate only applies to the slice of pay inside its band. Think of filling buckets: the first £12,570 goes in the 0% bucket, the next £37,700 in the 20% bucket, and only what spills over goes into the 40% bucket.

The rate on your next pound of pay is your marginal tax rate. The share of your whole pay that goes in tax is your average rate, which is always lower.

Scotland is different. If you live in Scotland, income tax on wages uses six Scottish bands: 19%, 20%, 21%, 42%, 45% and 48% (2026/27 tax year). National Insurance is the same across the UK. Use the calculator below and switch "Where you live" to see the difference.

Also: if you earn over £100,000, the Personal Allowance shrinks by £1 for every £2 above that, which creates a very high marginal rate between £100,000 and £125,140. That's a problem most people will never have, but it's why you'll hear about it.

National Insurance

National Insurance is a separate deduction from your wages. For employees in 8% (2026/27: 8% on pay between £12,570 and £50,270 a year, 2% above).

So on £25,000 you pay NI on £25,000 − £12,570 = £12,430, and 8% of that is £994.40 a year.

Your NI record matters more than it looks. Each year you pay (or are credited with) enough NI counts as a qualifying year towards the State Pension. You need 35 qualifying years for the full new State Pension, which is £241.30 a week (2026/27, about £12,548 a year; needs 35 qualifying years of National Insurance). Module 21 covers it.

Your employer also pays NI on your wages (employer's NI). It doesn't come out of your pay, but it's part of what employing you costs them.

Your first payslip, worked out

Back to the question at the start. £25,000 a year, England, no student loan, no pension:

A year A month
Gross pay £25,000.00 £2,083.33
Income tax: 20% of (£25,000 − £12,570) £2,486.00 £207.17
National Insurance: 8% of (£25,000 − £12,570) £994.40 £82.87
Net pay £21,519.60 £1,793.30

So about £1,793 a month. If you guessed £2,083 you forgot deductions; if you guessed much lower you probably taxed the whole £25,000.

Your average tax rate here is £2,486 ÷ £25,000 ≈ 10%, even though your marginal rate is 20%. Add NI and about 14% of your pay goes in deductions.

Part-time jobs and minimum wage

Lots of people's first pay comes from a Saturday or holiday job. Two things to know.

Minimum wage. Employers must pay at least the legal minimum for your age, from 1 April 2026:

  • 21 and over (National Living Wage): £12.71 an hour
  • 18 to 20: £10.85 an hour
  • Under 18: £8.00 an hour
  • Apprentices under 19, or in their first year: £8.00 an hour

Tax on small jobs. The Personal Allowance is yearly, so a Saturday job of 8 hours a week at £8 an hour (about £3,200 a year) is well under it: no income tax. Weekly pay of £64 is also below the NI thresholds, so no NI either. If you get a payslip showing tax on a small job, your tax code is probably wrong. You can usually get it back.

Self-employed income and side hustles

Not all money comes through PAYE. If you sell things you've made, tutor, do odd jobs, make content or run a small online business, you're trading, and there's no employer to take the tax off for you.

The trading allowance lets you earn up to £1,000 (a year, unchanged since April 2017; rising to £3,000 from 6 April 2027 (announced in the October 2026 Budget)) from trading without telling HMRC or paying tax on it. That's income before costs.

Go over £1,000 in a tax year and you need to register for self assessment: you tell HMRC what you earned on a tax return and pay the tax yourself.

  • Register by 5 October after the end of the tax year you first went over. (Over £1,000 in 2026/27? Register by 5 October 2027.)
  • File your online return and pay by 31 January the following year.
  • You can deduct either the £1,000 allowance or your actual business costs, not both. If your costs are higher than £1,000, claiming costs usually wins.
  • Profits are taxed with any wages, using the same Personal Allowance and bands. If you make enough profit, you also pay NI as a self-employed person.

The most common mistake is not keeping records. Note every sale and every cost as you go: a spreadsheet is fine.

Selling your own unwanted things (old clothes, a games console) isn't trading. Buying things to sell on for profit is.

Tax on savings

Interest on savings counts as income too, but most people pay none on it. As well as the Personal Allowance, there's the Personal Savings Allowance: £1,000 (2026/27, basic-rate taxpayers (£500 higher-rate, £0 additional-rate); from 6 April 2027 tax on savings above it rises to 24%, 44% and 49% (Budgets 2025 and 2026)). People on low incomes can also use the starting rate for savings, up to £5,000 of interest tax-free.

Interest in a Cash ISA is always tax-free and doesn't use up these allowances. Module 17 covers ISAs.

The common mistake

"I don't want a pay rise, it'll put me in a higher tax band and I'll take home less."

It sounds logical, and people repeat it at work all the time. But bands only tax the slice of pay inside them. A £1,000 rise that takes you from £50,270 to £51,270 costs you 40% tax and 2% NI on that £1,000: you keep £580 of it. You're never worse off from income tax bands alone.

(There are a few real "cliff edges" from other rules, like the Personal Allowance taper over £100,000 or some benefits and childcare support. They're the exception, not how tax bands work.)

In real life

  • Check every payslip: gross pay, hours, tax code, deductions. Spot an emergency code? Check your record in the HMRC app.
  • Keep your P60 (your yearly summary, given after the tax year ends) and P45 (given when you leave a job). You'll need them for loans, mortgages and fixing mistakes.
  • Side income over £1,000? Keep records from day one and register by 5 October.
  • Scotland? Your take-home will differ a little from England's on the same pay.
  • Red flag: being paid "cash in hand" with no payslip. It may mean no NI record for your State Pension, no proof of income and no employment rights.

Think about it

Why might someone earning £40,000 say they're "in the 40% tax bracket" when they aren't?

They're mixing up the higher-rate band with their own pay. At £40,000, every taxable pound is in the 20% band. Even people who do earn over £50,270 only pay 40% on the slice above it. The confusion makes people fear pay rises for no reason.

Two people each earn £24,000: one from one job, one from three part-time jobs at £8,000 each. Do they pay the same tax?

Over the year, yes: tax is on total income. But PAYE gives the whole Personal Allowance to one job (the "main" one) and taxes the others at 20% from the first pound (code BR). If codes are set up wrongly, the second person might pay too much or too little during the year and get it sorted out later.
