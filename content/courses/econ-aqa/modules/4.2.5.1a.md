## What fiscal policy is

**[[fiscal policy|Fiscal policy]]** is the manipulation of government spending, taxation and the budget balance.

It has two kinds of function:

- **Macroeconomic:** influencing the level of AD, growth, unemployment and inflation.
- **Microeconomic:** influencing particular markets, such as a tax on sugary drinks or a subsidy for training.

## Influencing aggregate demand

- **Expansionary (reflationary) fiscal policy:** raising government spending or cutting taxes. AD shifts right. It's used in a downturn.
- **Contractionary (deflationary) fiscal policy:** cutting spending or raising taxes. AD shifts left. It's used to cool an overheating economy.

Government spending is part of AD directly, so the effect is often larger than from a tax cut of the same size, as some of a tax cut is saved. The multiplier adds to the effect.

## Discretionary policy and automatic stabilisers

**Discretionary fiscal policy** is a deliberate change in spending or tax rates.

**[[automatic stabilisers|Automatic stabilisers]]** work with no decision being made. In a recession, tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises by itself, which supports AD. In a boom, the reverse happens.

## Influencing aggregate supply

Fiscal policy can also shift AS.

- **Spending:** on education, training, health and infrastructure raises the quality and quantity of labour and capital.
- **Taxes:** lower taxes can improve incentives to work, save, invest and take risks.

Some policies affect both sides. New rail lines add to AD as they're built, and to LRAS once they're in use.

## The pattern of economic activity

Fiscal policy doesn't only change the total. It also changes **who** and **what** gains:

- **Between industries:** subsidies for green energy or taxes on tobacco.
- **Between regions:** spending on transport in poorer areas.
- **Between groups:** benefits and progressive taxes shift income.

## Public expenditure

Public expenditure is spending by government. Its main types are:

- **Current spending:** day-to-day costs, such as nurses' pay and medicines.
- **Capital spending:** long-term investment, such as hospitals and roads.
- **Transfer payments:** money moved from taxpayers to others, such as pensions and benefits. Nothing is produced in return, so these aren't part of G in AD.

Reasons for spending include:

- Providing public goods and merit goods.
- Correcting market failure.
- Redistributing income and reducing poverty.
- Supporting growth, through infrastructure and skills.
- Managing the economy.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain how a rise in government spending on infrastructure could affect both AD and LRAS. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Government spending is a part of AD. LRAS shows the productive capacity of the economy.

**Application:** The UK might spend more on railways and broadband in the North of England.

**Analysis:** Building them creates jobs and income, so AD shifts right, and the multiplier adds to this. Once they're complete, firms and workers can move goods, people and information more quickly, so productivity rises. This adds to productive capacity, so LRAS shifts right as well, which allows more growth without so much inflation.
:::

Why it scores: it separates the short-run effect on AD from the long-run effect on capacity.
