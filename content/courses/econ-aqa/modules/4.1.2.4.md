## Choice architecture and framing

**[[choice architecture|Choice architecture]]** is the way choices are set out in front of people. There's no neutral way to do it: something has to go first on the menu, something has to be at eye level in the shop, and one option has to be what happens if you do nothing. All of these affect what people pick.

A supermarket puts sweets by the till. A canteen can put fruit at the front and chips at the back. Same food, different choices.

**[[framing]]** is the way information is worded. People respond differently to the same facts depending on how they're put:

- "90% survival rate" sounds better than "10% death rate".
- "90% fat free" sounds better than "contains 10% fat".
- A charge sounds worse when it's called a "fee" than when it's described as a "discount for paying early".

Rational theory says wording shouldn't matter, because the facts are the same. In practice, it does.

## Nudges

A **[[nudge]]** is a small change to choice architecture that steers people towards a better choice **without banning anything or significantly changing incentives**. People stay free to choose differently.

The idea was made famous by Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein in their 2008 book *Nudge*. The UK government set up the Behavioural Insights Team, known as the "nudge unit", in 2010.

Examples:

- **Social norms in tax letters:** telling people that most others in their area have paid their tax on time, which HMRC has used to get more people to pay.
- **Reminders:** text messages before GP appointments to reduce missed visits.
- **Salience:** putting calorie counts on menus, so the information is hard to miss.
- **Placement:** keeping cigarettes out of sight in shops, so they don't prompt a purchase.

Nudges are usually cheap compared with taxes, subsidies or new laws. They work with the way people really behave, and they keep freedom of choice.

## Default, restricted and mandated choice

**[[default choice|Default choice]]:** the option you get if you do nothing. Because of inertia and bounded self-control, many people stick with it. So setting a good default is a powerful tool.

- **Pensions:** under UK automatic enrolment, eligible workers are put into a workplace pension unless they opt out. Most stay in.
- **Organ donation:** since May 2020, adults in England are treated as agreeing to donate unless they opt out, with some exceptions.

**[[restricted choice|Restricted choice]]:** the options are limited to steer people, often because too many options make decisions harder or some options are harmful. Some choices remain, but not all of them. A school canteen that doesn't offer fizzy drinks restricts choice. So does a limit on the number of pension funds a saver is shown.

**[[mandated choice|Mandated choice]]:** people **must** make an active decision. There's no default, but you can't skip it. For example, someone applying for a driving licence could be required to say yes or no to organ donation. It forces people past inertia, without deciding for them.

## Do nudges work?

**In favour:**

- They're cheap and quick to bring in.
- They keep freedom of choice, unlike bans.
- They can correct the problems caused by bounded rationality and bounded self-control.

**Against:**

- Effects can be small or fade over time, and may not be enough for big problems like obesity or climate change.
- Some see them as **paternalistic**: who decides what's "best" for people? This is a value judgement.
- They can be seen as manipulation if people don't know they're being steered.
- Governments have biases and imperfect information too, so a nudge may push people the wrong way.
- Firms use the same tricks for profit, for example a hard-to-cancel subscription.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain how a default choice can be used by a government to increase saving for retirement. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** A default choice is the option that applies if a person takes no action. Behavioural economics suggests people often stick with it because of inertia.

**Application:** Under UK automatic enrolment, eligible workers are put into a workplace pension unless they choose to opt out.

**Analysis:** Bounded self-control means many people intend to save but keep putting it off. With an opt-out default, doing nothing now leads to saving, so the same inertia that once stopped people from joining now keeps them in. Saving rises even though no one is forced to take part and the incentive to save has not been changed.
:::

Why it scores: it defines the term, uses a real UK policy, and explains the mechanism (inertia working in the saver's favour), which is what "explain how" asks for.
