## Guess first

You get a 5% pay rise, and prices rise by 4%.

:::guess {"prompt":"Are you better off? By how much? What would you need to know to be sure?","kind":"text","id":"econ-aqa:4.2.1.2-guess"}
:::

:::callout idea
A few headline figures show how an economy is doing: output, prices, jobs, productivity and the current account, and each one has to be read with care.
:::

## Real GDP

**[[gross domestic product|Gross domestic product (GDP)]]** is the total value of goods and services produced in a country in a year.

- **Nominal GDP** is measured at current prices, so it rises if prices rise.
- **[[real gdp|Real GDP]]** is adjusted for inflation, so it shows the change in actual output.

**Real GDP per capita** is real GDP divided by the population. It's a better guide to average living standards, because it allows for a change in population.

**[[economic growth|Economic growth]]** is usually measured as the percentage change in real GDP.

:::check
{"type":"number","q":"Nominal GDP rises by 6% in a year, and prices rise by 4%. What is the approximate rise in real GDP, in %?","answer":"2","tol":0.01,"unit":"%","dp":0,"why":"Real growth is about nominal growth minus inflation: 6% − 4% = 2%."}
:::

## Measuring inflation

**[[inflation|Inflation]]** is a sustained rise in the general price level. The UK has two well-known measures:

- **Consumer Prices Index (CPI):** the measure used for the Bank of England's 2% target.
- **Retail Prices Index (RPI):** an older measure that includes some housing costs, such as mortgage interest. It's usually higher than the CPI.

A **fall** in the rate of inflation doesn't mean that prices are falling, only that they're rising more slowly.

:::check

:::

## Measuring unemployment

Unemployment is measured in two ways.

- **Labour Force Survey (LFS):** a survey that uses the **International Labour Organization (ILO)** definition. People count as unemployed if they're out of work, have looked for work in the last four weeks and could start within two weeks.
- **Claimant count:** the number claiming unemployment-related benefits. It's usually lower, because some unemployed people don't claim or can't.

The **unemployment rate** is the number unemployed as a percentage of the labour force, which is those employed plus those unemployed.

:::check

:::

## Productivity

**[[productivity|Productivity]]** usually means **output per worker** or **output per hour worked**. It matters a lot, because higher productivity is the main source of higher living standards over the long run.

## The balance of payments on current account

The **current account** records a country's trade and income flows with the rest of the world. It includes trade in goods and services, as well as income flows. A **deficit** means more is flowing out than in, and a **surplus** means the reverse. You'll study it in depth in 4.2.3.

## Reading data carefully

- **Check real or nominal.** Nominal figures can flatter.
- **Look at rates and levels.** Falling inflation still means a rising price level.
- **Watch for revisions.** Early estimates often change.
- **Use more than one indicator,** as each shows only part of the picture.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain why real GDP per capita is a better measure of changes in living standards than nominal GDP. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Nominal GDP is the value of output at current prices. Real GDP per capita is output adjusted for inflation and divided by the population.

**Application:** If nominal GDP rises by 5%, inflation is 3% and the population grows by 1%, then output per person has risen by only about 1%.

**Analysis:** Nominal GDP can rise just because prices rise, with no extra goods or services. Adjusting for inflation shows the change in actual output. Dividing by population shows the output available for each person, as a bigger population must share the total. So real GDP per capita gives a closer picture of what people can actually consume.
:::

Why it scores: it deals with both adjustments and uses a short calculation.
