## Barriers to growth and development

- **Corruption:** money is diverted, investors are put off and people lose trust in institutions.
- **Institutional factors:** weak legal systems, unstable government and poor tax collection.
- **Poor infrastructure:** bad roads, unreliable power and little internet access raise firms' costs.
- **Inadequate human capital:** low skills and poor health limit productivity.
- **Lack of property rights:** people without legal title to their land can't use it to borrow, and they have less reason to invest.

Others include debt, conflict, reliance on primary products with volatile prices, and the loss of skilled workers abroad.

## Market-based strategies

These aim to free up markets and attract private investment:

- **Trade liberalisation:** lower tariffs and more exports.
- **Attracting FDI:** to bring in capital and technology.
- **Privatisation and deregulation.**
- **Stable macroeconomic policy:** low inflation and sound budgets.
- **Microfinance:** small loans to people without access to banks.

**Strengths:** efficient resource use, competition and access to world markets.

**Weaknesses:** infant industries can be wiped out, gains can be unequal, and profits may leave the country.

## Interventionist strategies

These use the state to direct development:

- **Spending on education, health and infrastructure.**
- **Protecting infant industries** with tariffs.
- **Industrial policy:** supporting key sectors.
- **Buffer stock schemes,** to steady commodity prices.
- **Land reform.**

**Strengths:** tackles market failures, such as under-provision of merit goods and public goods.

**Weaknesses:** government failure, corruption and a lack of competition.

Many successful economies have used a mix. South Korea, for example, combined a push for exports with heavy state direction.

## The role of aid

**[[aid|Aid]]** is help given to poorer countries, as money, goods or expertise. It can be **humanitarian** (emergency) or **development** aid (long-term).

**For aid:**

- Fills the gap in savings and foreign currency.
- Funds health, education and infrastructure.
- Saves lives in emergencies.

**Against aid:**

- It can be lost to corruption.
- It may create reliance, and weaken the link between governments and taxpayers.
- **Tied aid** must be spent on the donor's goods, which can raise costs.

## The role of trade

**"Trade not aid"** is the view that access to world markets does more than aid does.

**For trade:**

- Brings in foreign currency and jobs.
- Allows countries to specialise, in line with comparative advantage.
- Brings technology and competition.

**Against relying on trade:**

- Rich countries' tariffs and farm subsidies limit access.
- Reliance on primary products leaves countries exposed to falling or unstable prices.
- Gains can go to a few.

Aid and trade can work together. Aid for roads and ports can help countries to trade more. This links back to globalisation, comparative advantage, and inequality in earlier lessons.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain how corruption can act as a barrier to economic development. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Corruption is the misuse of public power for private gain. Development means wider improvements in living standards.

**Application:** Officials might demand bribes for permits, or take money meant for building schools.

**Analysis:** Money that should pay for schools and clinics is lost, so human capital remains low. Bribes add to the cost of doing business, and the risk puts off domestic and foreign investors, so there's less capital and fewer jobs. People may avoid paying tax if they don't trust the state, which leaves still less for public services. So growth and development are both held back.
:::

Why it scores: it follows several routes from corruption to lower development.
