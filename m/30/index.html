The idea in one sentence

The most important investing decision isn't which share to buy but how to split your money between shares, bonds and cash (your asset allocation); get that right for your goal and nerves, keep costs low, rebalance now and then, and leave it alone.

Asset allocation: the big decision

Asset allocation is how you split money between types of asset, mainly shares, bonds and cash. It decides most of how your portfolio behaves: how much it's likely to grow, and how hard it'll fall in a bad year. Which particular fund you choose matters far less.

Three things set your allocation:

  1. Timeframe. Money needed within about five years shouldn't be in shares at all (module 10). For long goals, more in shares can make sense.
  2. Need. How much growth do you actually need to hit the goal? Don't take more risk than necessary.
  3. Nerve. How you'd really behave in a crash. If you'd sell after a 30% fall, hold less in shares, because a portfolio you abandon is worse than a duller one you keep.

Common starting points, not advice:

Mix (shares / bonds) Often described as Might suit
100 / 0 Adventurous Long timeframe, strong nerves, steady income
80 / 20 Growth Long timeframe, some wish for a smoother ride
60 / 40 Balanced Medium-long timeframe, moderate nerves
40 / 60 Cautious Shorter timeframe or low tolerance for falls

Building blocks

For each part of your allocation, choose simple, low-cost, diversified funds (module 14):

  • Shares: a global index fund covers thousands of companies in many countries in one go. Some people add a UK fund or a smaller-companies fund, but that's optional.
  • Bonds: a global or UK government bond index fund (often "hedged" to pounds, which removes most currency swings).
  • Cash: the emergency fund and short-term money, in savings, not the portfolio.

Or use a single multi-asset fund that holds shares and bonds in a fixed mix (say 60/40) and rebalances for you. One fund, one decision, often the simplest choice for beginners.

Check, for each fund: what index it tracks, its ongoing charge, accumulation units for growth, and that it's available in your ISA or pension.

Where it lives

Wrappers matter as much as funds (modules 17 and 21):

  1. Workplace pension first, at least up to any employer match.
  2. Stocks and Shares ISA for long-term money you might want before retirement.
  3. Lifetime ISA if you're saving for a first home under the price cap.
  4. A general (taxable) account only once the ISA allowance is used.

Rebalancing

Over time, your mix drifts. If shares have a great year, a 60/40 portfolio might become 66/34, carrying more risk than you chose. Rebalancing means selling some of what's grown and buying what's lagged, to get back to your target.

It feels backwards, selling winners, but it has two benefits: it keeps your risk where you decided, and it makes you buy low and sell high by rule, not emotion (module 26).

Simple ways to do it:

  • Once a year, on a fixed date, or
  • when a holding drifts more than about 5 percentage points from its target, or
  • by directing new money into whatever is underweight, so you rarely need to sell.

Inside an ISA or pension, rebalancing has no tax to pay. Multi-asset funds do it for you.

Your investment plan

Professionals write an investment policy statement: a short document that sets the rules before emotions get a say. Write yours now.

The common mistake

"I need lots of different funds to be properly diversified."

Ten funds that all hold the same big global companies isn't diversification. It's the same thing ten times, with more fees and more to manage. One or two broad funds can hold thousands of companies across the world. Simple usually beats complicated: fewer decisions, lower costs and less room for biases to creep in.

In real life

  • Start simple: one global share fund, or one multi-asset fund, in a Stocks and Shares ISA or pension.
  • Automate contributions, and raise them with each pay rise.
  • Rebalance on a schedule, not on news.
  • Review the plan once a year, alongside your money plan from module 29.
  • Red flag: advice to put most of your money into one theme, one country or one hot asset. Concentration is the opposite of the free lunch.

Think about it

Why might two people of the same age hold very different portfolios, and both be right?

Their goals, timeframes and nerves differ. One may be saving for a house in four years (mostly cash); the other for retirement in 40 years (mostly shares). One may sleep through crashes, the other not. The right portfolio fits the person, not the age.

Should you change your allocation when the news says a crash is coming?

Usually not. Forecasts of crashes are often wrong, and even right ones rarely get the timing right. Your allocation was set for your goal and nerve, and already expects falls. Changing it on headlines is recency bias and herding (module 26).
