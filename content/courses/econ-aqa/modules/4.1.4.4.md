:::callout idea
Firms have fixed costs that don't change with output and variable costs that do, and because of diminishing returns, short-run average and marginal costs fall and then rise, giving U-shaped curves.
:::

## Fixed and variable costs

- **[[fixed costs|Fixed costs]]** don't change with output in the short run: rent, insurance, loan interest. You pay them even if you make nothing.
- **[[variable costs|Variable costs]]** change with output: raw materials, energy for production, hourly wages.
- **Total cost = fixed costs + variable costs.**

In the long run, all costs are variable, as all factors can change.

## Total, average and marginal costs

- **Total cost (TC):** the cost of making all the output.
- **[[average cost|Average cost]] (AC or ATC):** cost per unit. **ATC = TC ÷ output.**
- **Average fixed cost (AFC) = fixed costs ÷ output.** **Average variable cost (AVC) = variable costs ÷ output.**
- **[[marginal cost|Marginal cost]] (MC):** the **extra** cost of making **one more** unit. **MC = change in TC ÷ change in output.**

## Why the curves have their shapes

Move the sliders and watch what shifts.

:::chart costs {"fc":60,"w":1}
:::

- **MC** falls at first, as workers specialise, then **rises** because of **diminishing returns**: each extra unit takes more labour.
- **AFC** keeps falling, as the same fixed cost is spread over more units.
- **AVC** is U-shaped, for the same reasons as MC.
- **ATC** is U-shaped: falling AFC and falling AVC pull it down, then rising AVC wins.
- **MC cuts AVC and ATC at their lowest points.** It's simple maths: if the extra unit costs less than the average, the average falls. If it costs more, the average rises. Think of a test score: a mark above your average pulls your average up.

Total cost rises with output: slowly at first, then faster as diminishing returns set in.

## Short-run and long-run costs

In the **short run**, firms are stuck with a fixed factor, so the short-run average cost curve is U-shaped because of diminishing returns.

In the **long run**, firms can choose any size. The **long-run average cost (LRAC)** curve shows the lowest cost per unit at each output. Its shape comes from economies and diseconomies of scale, not diminishing returns. That's the next lesson.

## Factor prices, productivity and input choice

Costs per unit depend on two things:

- **Factor prices:** wages, rent, interest and raw material prices. If these rise, costs rise.
- **Productivity:** if workers make more per hour, labour cost per unit falls.

**Unit labour cost = wage ÷ output per worker.** A worker paid twice as much who makes three times as much is cheaper per unit.

These decide the choice of inputs. If wages rise relative to the cost of machines, firms have an incentive to replace labour with capital, such as self-checkouts in shops.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain why a firm's short-run average total cost curve is U-shaped. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Average total cost is total cost divided by output, and it is the sum of average fixed cost and average variable cost.

**Analysis (falling):** As output rises, fixed costs are spread over more units, so AFC falls. Average variable cost also falls at first, as workers can specialise. So ATC falls.

**Analysis (rising):** With capital fixed in the short run, the law of diminishing returns eventually applies. Each extra worker adds less output, so marginal cost rises and pulls AVC up. Once this outweighs the fall in AFC, ATC rises, which gives the U shape.
:::

Why it scores: it deals with both sides of the U and names the cause of each, including the short-run fixed factor that makes diminishing returns apply.
