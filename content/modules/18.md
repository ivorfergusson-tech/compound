## Recap

Module 2 said a [[share]] is part-ownership of a company, and module 6 that prices move on expectations of future profits. But what *are* a company's profits, and how would you check? Every listed company must publish its accounts. This module teaches you to read the three main statements, using a made-up company so the numbers stay simple.

## Before you read

A company's accounts show a **profit of £20 million** this year, yet it's struggling to pay its suppliers and staff on time.

:::guess {"prompt":"How can a company be profitable and still run short of cash? Write your best explanation.","kind":"text","id":"18-guess"}
:::

:::callout idea
The income statement shows whether a company made a profit, the balance sheet shows what it owns and owes, and the cash flow statement shows whether real money actually came in; you need all three, because profit and cash aren't the same thing.
:::

## Where accounts come from

Listed companies publish an **annual report** each year (and shorter results every six months or quarter). It includes the **financial statements**, which are checked by independent **auditors**. You can find them free on the company's "investors" web page. UK private companies file simpler accounts at **Companies House**, also free to search.

Accounts follow strict rules, but they still involve judgement, so reading the notes and comparing years matters. Our example is **Brightside Bakeries plc**, a **made-up** company: it sells bread and cakes through shops and supermarkets.

## The income statement: did it make a profit?

The **[[income statement]]** (also called the profit and loss account, or P&L) covers a period, usually a year. It starts with sales and takes off costs, step by step:

| Brightside Bakeries (made up), year to 31 March | £m |
|---|---|
| **Revenue** (sales) | 200 |
| Cost of sales (flour, ingredients, bakery wages) | (120) |
| **Gross profit** | **80** |
| Operating costs (shop rents, marketing, head office) | (50) |
| **Operating profit** | **30** |
| Interest on loans | (4) |
| **Profit before tax** | **26** |
| Tax | (6) |
| **Net profit** (for shareholders) | **20** |

(Brackets mean a cost being taken away.)

- **[[revenue|Revenue]]** is the money from sales, before any costs. It's sometimes called turnover or the "top line".
- **[[profit|Profit]]** comes in layers. **Gross profit** is after the direct cost of making what was sold. **Operating profit** is after running the whole business. **Net profit** (the "bottom line") is after interest and tax: what's left for shareholders.

If Brightside has 100 million shares, its **earnings per share** (EPS) is £20m ÷ 100m = **20p**. You'll use EPS in module 22.

## Margins: how good is the business?

Raw profit doesn't tell you much on its own: £20m is brilliant for a small firm and poor for a giant. **[[margin|Margins]]** compare profit with revenue:

> margin = profit ÷ revenue

- **Gross margin:** 80 ÷ 200 = **40%**. For every £1 of sales, 40p is left after the direct costs.
- **Operating margin:** 30 ÷ 200 = **15%**. After all running costs, 15p in every £1 is profit.

Margins let you compare companies of different sizes and see trends. Rising margins usually mean pricing power or efficiency; falling margins can mean rising costs or tougher competition. Different industries have very different normal margins: software firms often have high ones, supermarkets famously thin ones.

## The balance sheet: what it owns and owes

The **[[balance sheet]]** is a snapshot on one date. It lists:

- **Assets:** what the company owns or is owed: bakeries, ovens, vans, stock of flour, cash, money customers owe it.
- **Liabilities:** what it owes: bank loans, money owed to suppliers, tax due.
- **Equity:** what's left for shareholders: assets minus liabilities.

It always balances, by definition:

> assets = liabilities + equity

| Brightside Bakeries (made up), 31 March | £m |
|---|---|
| Bakeries, ovens and vans | 150 |
| Stock (ingredients, packaging) | 20 |
| Owed by customers (supermarkets) | 30 |
| Cash | 10 |
| **Total assets** | **210** |
| Bank loans | (80) |
| Owed to suppliers | (40) |
| **Total liabilities** | **(120)** |
| **Equity (shareholders' funds)** | **90** |

Things to look at: how much **debt** there is compared with equity (here 80 of loans against 90 of equity, a fair amount), and whether there's enough **cash** and money coming in soon to pay bills due soon.

## The cash flow statement: did real money come in?

Profit uses **accounting rules**: a sale counts when it's made, even if the customer hasn't paid yet. The **[[cash flow statement]]** shows the actual cash coming in and going out, in three parts:

1. **Operating:** cash from the everyday business.
2. **Investing:** cash spent on (or received from selling) long-term things like new bakeries and ovens.
3. **Financing:** cash from or paid to lenders and shareholders: loans taken or repaid, dividends paid, shares issued.

Back to the question at the start. A company can be profitable but short of cash when:

- customers are slow to pay (sales are booked as profit, but the money hasn't arrived),
- it's tied up cash in stock, or spent heavily on new equipment,
- it has big loan repayments due.

Brightside's supermarket customers owe it £30m: that's profit on paper but not yet money in the bank. Companies rarely go bust because they're unprofitable on paper. They go bust when they **run out of cash**.

Investors often look at **free cash flow**: operating cash flow minus spending on equipment. It's the cash the business genuinely generates, which could pay dividends, cut debt or fund growth. Module 22 values companies using it.

## How the three fit together

- The income statement's **net profit** adds to the balance sheet's **equity** (unless paid out as dividends).
- The cash flow statement explains why **cash** on the balance sheet changed from last year to this.
- So one company's story runs across all three: did it make money (income statement), what does it own and owe (balance sheet), and did the money actually arrive (cash flow)?

## Reading a real annual report

Annual reports are long. A beginner's route:

1. **The strategic report or chair's statement:** the company's own story. Expect it to be upbeat.
2. **The three statements:** revenue, profit and margins over two years; debt and cash; operating cash flow versus profit.
3. **The principal risks:** what the directors themselves worry about.
4. **The auditor's report:** usually standard wording. Anything unusual is worth noticing.

Always compare at least two years, and if you can, a competitor.

## The common mistake

**"Revenue went up, so the company is doing well."**

Revenue is only the top line. A company can grow sales by cutting prices or spending heavily on marketing, and end up making **less** profit, or burning cash. Look at what happened to margins, profit and cash flow too. Growth that costs more than it earns destroys value.

## In real life

- **Use annual reports**, free on company websites and at Companies House.
- **Check three things fast:** are margins steady or rising? Is debt manageable? Does operating cash flow roughly match profit over time?
- **Compare like with like:** same industry, same period.
- **Red flag:** profits rising while cash flow keeps falling, or a company relying on new loans to pay dividends.

## Think about it

:::reveal Why might a fast-growing company show a loss even though it's healthy?
It may be spending heavily now (on marketing, staff or new sites) to win customers who'll be profitable later. The income statement shows the costs today. Investors then look at whether each new customer or site earns more than it cost, and whether there's enough cash to last until profits arrive.
:::

:::reveal Why do auditors exist, and do they guarantee the accounts are right?
They independently check that the accounts give a fair view and follow the rules, which gives investors confidence. But they test samples, not every transaction, and can be misled. There have been major accounting scandals that auditors missed, so they reduce risk rather than remove it.
:::
