## Why governments intervene

The existence of market failure, in its various forms, is an argument for government intervention. Governments influence how resources are allocated through **public spending, taxation and regulation**.

They also have a range of **objectives**, such as growth, fairness, health and the environment. These can conflict, and they affect how a government chooses to intervene in a **[[mixed economy]]**, where both markets and the state allocate resources.

## Indirect taxes

An **indirect tax** on a good with external costs raises its price, which reduces consumption. If the tax equals the external cost, firms pay the full social cost. This is called **internalising the externality**.

**Strengths:** it uses the market, keeps choice, raises revenue and gives firms a reason to find cleaner methods.

**Weaknesses:**

- The size of the external cost is hard to measure.
- It may do little if demand is inelastic, though it then raises more revenue.
- It's **regressive** when poorer people spend a larger share of their income on the good.
- It can encourage smuggling or buying abroad.

The UK's **Soft Drinks Industry Levy**, a tax on producers, began in 2018. Many firms cut the sugar in their drinks to fall below the threshold, so it changed what was produced as well as the price.

## Subsidies

A **subsidy** is a payment from the government to producers or consumers. It lowers costs, so it raises consumption of a good with external benefits. It's the reverse of a tax, and a subsidy equal to the external benefit moves output to the social optimum.

**Strengths:** it encourages merit goods and clean technology, and it can help those on low incomes.

**Weaknesses:** it has an opportunity cost, the right size is hard to judge, firms can become reliant on it, and it may reward firms for what they'd have done anyway.

## Price controls

**[[maximum price|Maximum prices]]** are ceilings set **below** equilibrium, to keep a good affordable. Rent caps are an example.

- **Benefit:** those who get the good pay less.
- **Problem:** excess demand. Shortages, queues and black markets can result, and supply may fall in the long run.

**[[minimum price|Minimum prices]]** are floors set **above** equilibrium, to reduce consumption or to protect producers. Minimum unit pricing for alcohol in Scotland is an example.

- **Benefit:** it discourages a demerit good, and targets the cheapest, strongest drinks.
- **Problem:** excess supply, and it hits poorer consumers. Unlike a tax, it raises no government revenue, so the extra money goes to sellers.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain how an indirect tax could reduce a negative externality. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** A negative externality is a cost to a third party. An indirect tax is a tax on spending on a good, which adds to a firm's costs.

**Application:** A tax on carbon emissions would raise the costs of firms that burn fossil fuels.

**Analysis:** The tax shifts the private cost curve upward, so that it moves towards the marginal social cost curve. Firms now pay for some of the external cost, so the price rises and quantity falls towards the social optimum. If the tax equals the external cost, the externality is fully internalised. It also gives firms a reason to switch to cleaner methods.
:::

Why it scores: it uses the idea of internalising, and it adds a long-run effect on behaviour.
