Recap

Module 4 introduced asset managers, the buy side. Module 14 compared index funds with active funds and showed how fees eat returns. Module 16 covered the deal-making sell side. This module goes deeper into the buy side: traditional fund managers, hedge funds and private equity. Who manages the world's savings, how they're paid, and what they really do with the money.

Before you read

A private equity firm buys a company for £200 million. It pays £80 million of its fund's money and borrows the other £120 million. Five years later, it sells the company for 28% more than it paid.

The idea in one sentence

The buy side manages money for savers and institutions in very different ways (cheap index-tracking, active stock picking, flexible hedge fund strategies and private equity buyouts), and how each is paid shapes how it behaves and what's left for the investors.

Assets under management

The size of an asset manager is measured by AUM (assets under management): the total value of the money it invests for clients. UK investment managers run £11.1 trillion (2025, up 11% on 2024; about 53% for clients overseas), making the UK one of the world's biggest centres for managing money.

Most managers earn a fee as a percentage of AUM. That means they earn more by gathering more assets and when markets rise, whether or not they beat anything. That's why fees, and how they're structured, are central to judging any manager.

Traditional asset managers: active and passive

Most of the industry runs funds, pension portfolios and insurance money:

  • Passive managers run index funds and ETFs (module 14): low fees, no attempt to beat the market. They've grown fast because, after costs, most active funds lag them: only about 30% of active funds beat a tracker over ten years.
  • Active managers pick investments to beat a benchmark. They research companies, meet management teams and vote at company meetings. They charge more and must earn it by outperforming after fees.

Asset managers are also owners: they vote on directors, pay and takeovers, and engage with companies on strategy and on issues like climate and governance. Because they hold so much of the stock market, this stewardship role matters for how companies are run.

Hedge funds

A hedge fund is a lightly restricted investment fund for professional and wealthy investors. Unlike ordinary funds, hedge funds can:

  • short sell (bet on prices falling, module 7),
  • use leverage (borrowed money, module 7),
  • trade derivatives (module 28),
  • move quickly between markets and strategies.

The name comes from "hedging": the original idea was to hold some positions that rise if others fall, reducing market risk. Today strategies vary hugely: long/short shares, global macro (betting on interest rates and currencies), event-driven (around takeovers), quantitative (computer models) and more.

They're usually only available to professional or high-net-worth investors, not ordinary retail savers, because they're riskier, less transparent and harder to sell out of.

Private equity

private equity (PE) firms buy whole companies (usually private ones, or listed ones they take private), run them for several years to improve them, then sell, aiming for a big gain.

Their classic tool is the leveraged buyout (LBO): buying a company with a mix of the fund's money (equity) and a lot of borrowed money (debt), secured on the company itself. The company's cash flow then repays the debt.

Debt magnifies the result, like leverage in trading (module 7). Back to the question at the start. In the calculator's example, a company bought for £200m with 60% debt, whose EBITDA grows 5% a year and is sold at the same multiple, returns about £182m on the fund's £80m: about 2.3 times the money, roughly 18% a year. With no debt, the same company would return about 1.5 times, roughly 9% a year.

But leverage cuts both ways. If profits fall 5% a year and the sale multiple drops, the fund with 60% debt gets back under half its money, while the no-debt version loses about a fifth. Heavily indebted companies are also more likely to fail in a downturn, which can hurt workers and suppliers.

How PE firms try to add value, beyond the debt:

  • tighter management, new leaders, clearer incentives (managers often own shares),
  • cutting costs, buying smaller rivals and combining them ("buy and build"),
  • pushing growth into new markets.

Critics argue some returns come mainly from debt, fees and cost-cutting rather than lasting improvement. Supporters point to businesses that grew under PE ownership. Both happen.

How they're paid: 2 and 20

Hedge funds and private equity have traditionally charged 2 and 20:

  • a 2% management fee each year on the money managed, and
  • a 20% performance fee (called carried interest, or "carry", in private equity) on the profits, often above a minimum return called a hurdle.

Example: a £100m hedge fund makes 10% before fees (£10m). The management fee takes £2m. The performance fee takes 20% of the remaining £8m, £1.6m. Investors get £6.4m: 6.4% instead of 10%.

Compare an index fund charging about 0.1%. For the higher fees to be worth it, the manager has to deliver much better returns, or something valuable like lower risk, consistently. Fees have come under pressure, and many funds now charge less than 2 and 20.

Careers on the buy side

Roles include analysts researching companies, portfolio managers making the decisions, traders executing them, and risk, operations and client teams. Private equity firms often hire people with investment banking experience (module 16) because the deal and modelling skills overlap. Many asset managers also run graduate and apprenticeship schemes.

The common mistake

"Hedge funds are hedged, so they're safe."

Many hedge funds use leverage, short selling and complex derivatives, and some have collapsed spectacularly. "Hedge fund" describes a legal structure and a flexible set of tools, not a level of safety. Their risks and fees are why they're mostly limited to professional investors.

In real life

  • For your own money, low-cost diversified funds (module 14) usually beat paying high fees.
  • Read company news through a buy-side lens: who owns it, is it PE-backed, what debt does it carry?
  • Check how any manager is paid: a percentage of assets, performance fees, or both.
  • Red flag: "exclusive hedge fund" or "private equity" offers to ordinary people via cold calls or social media. Genuine ones aren't sold that way, and these are common scams. Check the FCA register.

Think about it

Why might an asset manager paid a percentage of AUM care more about gathering assets than beating the market?

Fees grow with assets whether or not the fund outperforms. A big fund earning average returns can earn the manager more than a small fund doing brilliantly. That's why investors should judge managers on returns after fees, and why low-cost index funds put pressure on the whole industry.

Who benefits most from the 2 and 20 fee structure?

The managers earn 2% every year regardless and a share of any gains, but don't share losses in the same way. Investors only come out ahead if the gross returns are high enough to cover the fees and still beat cheaper alternatives.
