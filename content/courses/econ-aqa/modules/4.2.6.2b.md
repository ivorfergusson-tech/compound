## Protectionist policies

**[[protectionism|Protectionism]]** means government policies that restrict imports or help home producers against foreign competition.

- **[[tariff|Tariffs]]:** taxes on imports.
- **[[quota|Quotas]]:** limits on the quantity of imports.
- **[[export subsidy|Export subsidies]]:** payments that help home firms sell abroad at lower prices.
- **Other barriers:** complex rules, product standards or "buy local" policies.

## The effect of a tariff

A tariff raises the price of imports, and so the price in the home market.

Start at the world price, with no tariff. Then add one.

:::chart tariff {"t":0}

The diagram shows these effects:

- **Price rises,** so consumers pay more and buy less.
- **Domestic supply rises,** as home firms can sell more at the higher price.
- **Imports fall,** by more than either.
- **The government gains** the tariff revenue, shown by the box.
- **Welfare is lost,** shown by the two triangles. One is the cost of producing at home what could be imported more cheaply. The other is the loss from consumers buying less.

## Why countries use protectionism

- **Infant industries:** new firms may need time to grow.
- **Protecting jobs** in declining industries.
- **Dumping:** stopping foreign firms from selling below cost.
- **Strategic industries:** such as defence, food or energy.
- **Revenue,** in some poorer countries.
- **Correcting a current account deficit.**
- **Retaliation** against other countries' barriers.

## Consequences

- **Higher prices** and less choice for consumers.
- **Less competition,** so home firms may be less efficient.
- **Retaliation,** which can start a **trade war** that harms exporters.
- **Misallocation of resources,** as countries move away from comparative advantage.
- **Higher costs** for firms that use imported parts.
- **Lower world trade and growth.**

Recent years have seen a rise in tariffs in some major economies, including the United States, which shows that protectionism remains a live issue.

## Customs unions

Countries can reduce barriers among themselves. A **[[customs union|customs union]]** has two main features:

- **Free trade** between its members.
- **A common external tariff** on goods from outside.

The second feature is what makes it different from a **free trade area**, where each member keeps its own tariffs with the rest of the world.

Trade within a union can rise as cheaper partners replace dearer home producers, which is **trade creation**. It can also fall, if members switch from cheap outside suppliers to dearer members, which is **trade diversion**.

## The Single European Market

The **[[single european market|Single European Market (SEM)]]** goes further than a customs union. Its main characteristics are the **four freedoms**, which allow goods, services, people and capital to move freely, plus common rules and standards.

**Benefits:** a large market with fewer barriers, economies of scale, more competition and more choice.

**Costs:** rules that all must follow, and a lack of national control.

The UK left the EU in 2020. It's now outside the SEM and the customs union, and it trades with the EU under the Trade and Cooperation Agreement. This generally has no tariffs, but it does have more paperwork and checks than before.

## The World Trade Organisation

The **[[world trade organisation|World Trade Organisation (WTO)]]** is the international body that deals with the rules of trade. It:

- Provides a forum for negotiating lower trade barriers.
- Sets rules, such as treating members alike.
- Settles disputes between countries.

Critics say that it's slow, that talks tend to stall, and that the rules can favour richer countries. Its dispute system has also been weakened in recent years.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Using a diagram, explain the effects of a tariff on imports. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** A tariff is a tax on imports. On a diagram, it raises the price above the world price.

**Application:** Suppose the UK put a tariff on imported steel.

**Analysis:** At the higher price, UK firms supply more, moving up the domestic supply curve, and consumers buy less, moving up the demand curve. So imports fall from both sides. The government gains revenue, shown by a box equal to the tariff times the imports. Home producers gain, and consumers lose. The two triangles are a welfare loss, as resources are used inefficiently and consumption falls.
:::

Why it scores: it reads each effect off the diagram, and it says who gains and who loses.
