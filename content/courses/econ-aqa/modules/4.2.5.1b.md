## Why governments tax

- **To raise revenue** for spending.
- **To redistribute income** from richer to poorer people.
- **To correct market failure,** such as taxing pollution or demerit goods.
- **To manage demand** in the economy.

## Direct and indirect taxes

**[[direct tax|Direct taxes]]** are taken directly from income or wealth, and the person who owes them pays them. Examples are income tax and corporation tax.

**[[indirect tax|Indirect taxes]]** are taxes on spending. The seller pays the government, and the buyer bears some of the cost. Examples are VAT and fuel duty.

## Progressive, proportional and regressive

These terms compare the share of **income** paid in tax at different income levels.

- **[[progressive tax|Progressive]]:** the share rises as income rises. UK income tax is an example.
- **[[proportional tax|Proportional]]:** the share is the same for all.
- **[[regressive tax|Regressive]]:** the share falls as income rises. Many indirect taxes are regressive, since poorer people spend more of their income.

Don't confuse this with the amount paid. A regressive tax can still take more pounds from a rich person, but it takes a smaller share of their income.

Two rates matter:

- **Average rate:** total tax ÷ total income.
- **Marginal rate:** the tax on the next £1 earned.

## Principles of taxation

Adam Smith set out principles of a good tax, which are still used:

- **Equitable:** fair, based on ability to pay.
- **Certain:** clear about how much is due and when.
- **Convenient:** easy to pay.
- **Economical:** cheap to collect.

Modern additions are that taxes should be **efficient** (not distorting choices more than necessary) and **flexible** (easy to change).

## The main UK taxes

| Tax | What it's on | Merits | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Income tax | Income, at 20%, 40% and 45% in England, Wales and Northern Ireland | Raises a lot, and it's progressive | High rates may weaken incentives |
| National Insurance | Earnings, paid by workers and employers | Raises a lot and it's cheap to collect | A tax on jobs, for employers |
| VAT | Most spending, at a standard rate of 20% | Raises a lot, and hard to avoid | Regressive |
| Corporation tax | Company profits | Taxes firms' earnings | May reduce investment, and firms can move profits abroad |
| Fuel, alcohol and tobacco duties | Particular goods | Corrects externalities | Regressive |
| Council tax | Homes, by band | Hard to avoid | Based on old valuations |

In 2026 to 2027, the first £12,570 of income is free of tax for most people. The 40% rate starts at £50,270, and the 45% rate above £125,140.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain why indirect taxes are often regressive. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** An indirect tax is a tax on spending. A regressive tax takes a smaller share of income as income rises.

**Application:** VAT in the UK is 20% on most goods, whatever the income of the buyer.

**Analysis:** People on low incomes spend a larger share of their income, and those on high incomes save more. So if two people buy the same item, they pay the same tax, but it's a larger share of the lower income. Across all spending, the poor pay a higher share of income in VAT, so the tax is regressive, even if the rich pay more pounds overall.
:::

Why it scores: it relates tax to income, not spending, which is the point of the question.
