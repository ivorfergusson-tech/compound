## Guess first

Your savings pay 2% interest, and prices are rising by 5%.

:::guess {"prompt":"Are your savings really growing? Who gains from inflation, and who loses? Would falling prices be better?","kind":"text","id":"econ-aqa:4.2.3.3b-guess"}
:::

:::callout idea
Inflation and deflation both do harm, in different ways and to different people, and in an open economy like the UK, much of it can come from abroad.
:::

## Consequences of inflation for individuals

**Losers:**

- **Savers,** if interest rates are below inflation.
- **People on fixed incomes,** whose money buys less.
- **Workers,** if their pay rises more slowly than prices. Their **real wages** fall.
- **The poorest,** who spend more of their income on essentials like food and energy.

**Winners:**

- **Borrowers,** as inflation reduces the real value of their debts.
- **Workers with strong bargaining power,** who can win bigger rises.

## Consequences of inflation for the economy

- **International competitiveness:** if UK prices rise faster than those abroad, exports fall and imports rise, which worsens the current account.
- **Uncertainty:** firms find it harder to plan, so they may invest less.
- **Costs of changing prices** and the time spent shopping around.
- **Wage-price spirals,** if expectations rise.
- **Policy response:** high inflation leads to higher interest rates, which can slow growth.

A low, stable rate of inflation isn't a problem. It can even help, by making it easier for firms to adjust real wages and by giving a margin above deflation.

## Consequences of deflation

Falling prices sound good, and they can be, if they come from productivity gains or cheaper imports. Falling prices driven by weak demand are a serious problem:

- **Delayed spending:** consumers wait for lower prices, so demand falls further.
- **Higher real debts:** the real burden of debt rises, so borrowers cut back.
- **Lower profits:** firms cut costs, wages and jobs.
- **Limited policy room:** interest rates can't go far below zero, and real interest rates may be high.
- **A deflationary spiral:** falling demand leads to falling prices, then lower income, then still lower demand.

For individuals, those with savings or a secure income can gain, as their money buys more. Those with debts, or who lose their jobs, suffer.

Japan is the usual example of a country that struggled with low inflation and periods of deflation for many years.

## World commodity prices

The UK imports a lot of its energy, food and raw materials. So when **world commodity prices** rise, UK costs rise too, which is **imported cost-push inflation**.

After Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022, energy prices jumped, and UK CPI inflation reached 11.1% in the year to October 2022, well above the 2% target. Rising fuel prices are also the largest upward driver of the rise in inflation to 3.5% in September 2026, according to the ONS.

The Bank of England can't lower world oil prices. Raising interest rates may hurt growth, without tackling the cause.

## Changes in other economies

Other economies affect UK inflation in several ways:

- **Growth abroad** raises the demand for UK exports and for commodities, which adds to demand-pull and cost-push pressure.
- **Low-cost producers,** such as China in recent decades, can hold down the prices of imported goods.
- **Inflation abroad** makes imports dearer.
- **The exchange rate:** a weaker pound raises import prices. Interest rate changes abroad can move the pound.
- **Global supply chains:** breaks in supply, as happened during the pandemic, raise costs.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain how high inflation could harm a country's international competitiveness. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level. International competitiveness is the ability of a country's firms to sell in world markets.

**Application:** Suppose that UK inflation is higher than in the euro area, while the exchange rate stays the same.

**Analysis:** UK goods get dearer compared with foreign goods, so foreign buyers switch away, and exports fall. UK buyers turn to cheaper imports. Net exports fall, so the current account worsens and AD falls. Firms may also invest less because of the uncertainty, which reduces competitiveness in the long run.
:::

Why it scores: it compares with other countries, which is the key point, and follows the chain through to exports and imports.
