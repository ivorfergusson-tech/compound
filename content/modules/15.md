## Candlesticks

The most common chart for traders is the **[[candlestick]]** chart. Each candle covers one period: a minute, an hour, a day or a week. It shows four prices:

- **Open:** the first price in the period.
- **Close:** the last price.
- **High** and **low:** the extremes reached.

The thick part is the **body**, from open to close. The thin lines above and below are the **wicks** (or shadows), reaching up to the high and down to the low.

On this course's charts, **hollow** candles closed **higher** than they opened (buyers won the period) and **filled** candles closed **lower** (sellers won). Many apps use green and red instead.

What a candle hints at:

- **A long body** means strong buying or selling.
- **Long wicks** mean the price went far but got pushed back: a long lower wick shows sellers pushed it down and buyers pushed it back up.
- **A tiny body** (a "doji") means open and close were almost the same: indecision.

## Timeframes

The same market looks different on different **[[timeframe|timeframes]]**. A minute chart is full of noise; a daily chart shows the swings of weeks; a weekly chart shows the bigger trend. A move that looks huge on a 5-minute chart may be invisible on a weekly one.

Pick a timeframe that matches how long you plan to hold. Swing traders who hold for days often use daily charts, with weekly charts for the bigger picture. In the chart below, tick "Weekly candles" and see the same data simplified.

## Trends

A **[[trend]]** is the general direction of prices:

- **Uptrend:** higher highs and higher lows. Each rally goes further, and each dip stops above the last one.
- **Downtrend:** lower highs and lower lows.
- **Sideways (a range):** prices bounce between a ceiling and a floor.

Trends are easiest to see afterwards. In real time, every trend looks like it might be ending, and every range might be about to break.

## Support and resistance

- **[[support|Support]]** is a price level where falls have repeatedly stopped. Buyers have stepped in there before.
- **[[resistance|Resistance]]** is a level where rises have repeatedly stalled. Sellers have stepped in there before.

Why might they work? Because people remember prices: traders who missed buying at £100 may buy if it comes back; people who bought at £110 and watched it fall may sell to "get out even" when it returns there. Round numbers and previous highs and lows often matter for the same reason.

When a price pushes clearly through resistance (or below support), that's a **[[breakout]]**. Old resistance often becomes new support, and vice versa.

## How traders use these ideas

Charts mostly help with **where** to act, not **whether** the price will rise:

- **Entries:** buying near support in an uptrend, or after a breakout above resistance.
- **Stops:** placing a stop-loss just beyond the level that would prove you wrong: below support for a long trade (module 23).
- **Targets:** aiming for the next resistance level.

That last point matters most. A chart gives you a logical place for your stop, which defines your risk. The edge, if any, comes from keeping losses small when you're wrong and letting winners run when you're right.

## Fooling yourself

Charts are brilliant at making randomness look meaningful. Some traps:

- **Hindsight.** Patterns are obvious on old charts because you know what happened next. In real time, half of them fail.
- **Seeing what you want.** If you want to buy, you'll find "support"; if you want to sell, "resistance".
- **False breakouts.** Prices often poke through a level and then fall straight back, catching traders who jumped in.
- **Random data looks patterned.** Even made-up random walks show trends, ranges and "head and shoulders". The chart above looks meaningful, but a computer generated it.

Back to the question at the start. A price bouncing off £100 three times suggests buyers have been active there, so another bounce is possible. But it's equally possible that this time it breaks below. No one knows. A disciplined trader might buy near £100 with a stop just below (say £98.50), so that if they're wrong the loss is small and defined.

## The common mistake

**"This pattern always works."**

No chart pattern works always. Most work slightly more often than chance at best, and many not at all after costs. Traders who survive think in probabilities: "this setup gives me a reasonable chance, and if it fails I lose 1R." Traders who think a pattern is certain tend to skip stops and take big losses.

## In real life

- **Start with a daily chart** and the bigger trend; zoom out before you zoom in.
- **Mark support and resistance** before you trade, and write in your journal why.
- **Use levels to place stops**, not just entries.
- **Test patterns honestly** on many past examples, including the failures (module 27).
- **Red flag:** anyone selling a "secret pattern" or indicator with a near-perfect win rate.

## Think about it

:::reveal Why might support and resistance work partly because so many traders believe in them?
If enough traders place buy orders at a well-known level, their buying itself can stop the fall: a self-fulfilling effect. But big players know this too, and sometimes push prices through obvious levels to trigger everyone's stops before reversing.
:::

:::reveal Why can't you use the made-up chart above to prove that breakouts work?
It's one example, made up by a computer, and you can see the ending. Proving anything needs many real examples, tested by a rule decided in advance, including all the breakouts that failed. Module 27 explains how to test honestly.
:::

## Try it

1. Open a daily chart of your chosen market. Is it trending up, down or sideways over the last three months?
2. Mark one support and one resistance level. Write in your journal what you'd do if price reached each one, including where your stop would go.
