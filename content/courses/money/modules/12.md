## What a central bank does

The **Bank of England** is the UK's central bank. It doesn't run accounts for the public. Its jobs:

- **Monetary stability:** keeping inflation low and stable, around its target.
- **Financial stability:** keeping the banking system safe (through the PRA and its financial policy committee).
- **Issuing banknotes** in England and Wales, and acting as the banks' bank.

It's operationally independent: the government sets the target, but the Bank decides how to hit it, so interest rates aren't set to win elections.

## The inflation target and the MPC

The government gives the Bank an **[[inflation target]]**: CPI inflation of {{fig:inflationTarget}}. Module 1 explained why it's 2%, not 0%: falling prices can be harmful, and a small positive rate gives room to cut rates in a downturn.

Decisions are made by the **[[mpc|Monetary Policy Committee]]** (MPC): nine members, including the Governor, deputy governors, the Bank's chief economist and four external experts. It meets {{fig:mpc+}} and each member votes. The votes and minutes are published, so you can see the split.

## How Bank Rate works

**[[bank rate|Bank Rate]]** is the interest rate the Bank pays commercial banks on the money they hold with it, and it influences the rate it lends to them. Because it sets the cost of money at the centre of the system, other rates follow:

- **Variable mortgages and tracker loans** often move within weeks.
- **Savings rates** usually follow, often more slowly (module 8).
- **New fixed-rate mortgages** follow markets' **expectations** of future Bank Rate, so they can move before the Bank does.
- **Bond yields** (module 24) and the value of the pound respond too.

The chain from a rate change to inflation is called the **[[transmission mechanism]]**:

:::

When the Bank **raises** rates:

- Borrowing costs more, so people and firms borrow and spend less. Mortgage holders coming off a fix have less left over.
- Saving pays more, so some people save rather than spend.
- Asset prices like houses and shares tend to cool, so people feel less wealthy.
- The pound often strengthens, making imports cheaper.

Less spending means less pressure on prices and, over time, lower inflation. The catch: it can also mean slower growth and fewer jobs, which is the trade-off the MPC weighs every time. **Cutting** rates works the other way.

These effects are slow. The Bank describes the delays as "long and variable", and studies of inflation-targeting central banks put the strongest effect on prices at roughly one to two years after a change, sometimes longer. One reason: most UK mortgages are fixed for two to five years and only feel a change when the fix ends.

Back to the question at the start. With inflation well above 2%, the usual answer is to **raise** rates (or keep them high), knowing the effect will take a year or two to come through, and that pushing too hard risks a recession.

## Quantitative easing (QE)

When Bank Rate is already near zero, as after the 2008 crisis and in the 2020 pandemic, the Bank can't cut much further. So it used **[[qe|quantitative easing]]** (QE): creating new central bank money to **buy government bonds** (gilts) in huge amounts.

How QE is meant to work:

- Buying gilts pushes up their prices and pushes down their **yields**, so long-term interest rates fall (module 24).
- Lower long-term rates make borrowing cheaper for companies and households.
- Investors who sold gilts to the Bank put the money into other assets, lifting their prices and encouraging spending.

At its peak, around the start of 2022, the Bank held about **£895 billion** of gilts. It's now doing the reverse, **quantitative tightening** (QT), by selling gilts and letting them mature. It still holds {{fig:qeStock+}}.

QE is controversial. Supporters say it prevented deeper recessions. Critics say it inflated asset prices (helping people who already owned homes and shares), and when rates rose, the Bank's losses on the gilts it held fell to the Treasury, so ultimately to taxpayers.

## Why this matters to you

- **Mortgages and rent:** rate rises feed into mortgage payments and, over time, rents. On a £200,000 25-year repayment mortgage, going from 4.5% to 5.5% adds about **£117 a month**.
- **Savings:** higher Bank Rate usually means better savings rates (module 9).
- **Jobs:** higher rates slow the economy, which can mean fewer vacancies and smaller pay rises.
- **Your investments:** share and bond prices react to every surprise in rate expectations (module 22).

## The common mistake

**"The Bank of England sets mortgage and savings rates."**

It sets **Bank Rate**, the rate at the centre of the system. Banks then set their own mortgage and savings rates, influenced by Bank Rate, market expectations, competition and their own costs. That's why your savings rate can stay low after Bank Rate rises, and why fixed mortgage rates can move before the Bank does anything.

## In real life

- **Watch MPC dates** (eight a year) if you have a variable mortgage, a tracker or savings to move.
- **Fixed rates follow expectations**, not just today's Bank Rate.
- **Read the minutes:** the vote split hints at what's coming next.
- **Link to Economics:** this is the monetary policy part of A-level and university economics, with real numbers.
- **Red flag:** headlines that say "rates will definitely fall"; even the MPC doesn't know.

## Think about it

:::reveal Why does the government give the Bank of England independence over interest rates?
Politicians might be tempted to cut rates before elections to boost the economy, even if it stores up inflation for later. An independent Bank with a clear target is more credible, and when people believe inflation will stay near 2%, it's easier to keep it there.
:::

:::reveal Why might the MPC hold rates even when inflation is above target?
Because rate changes work with a long delay. If members expect inflation to fall back on its own (say, as a one-off energy price rise drops out), raising rates further could hurt jobs just as the problem is fading. They set policy for where inflation is heading, not where it is today.
:::
