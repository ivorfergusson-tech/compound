## What globalisation is

**[[globalisation|Globalisation]]** is the growing integration of the world's economies, through trade, investment, the movement of people and the spread of ideas.

## The main characteristics

- More **international trade** in goods and services.
- Larger flows of **foreign direct investment** and money.
- **Global supply chains:** a product is designed in one country and made from parts from many others.
- More **migration** of workers.
- The spread of technology, brands and culture.
- Growing power of **multinational corporations**.

## The causes

- **Cheaper transport:** container ships and air freight.
- **Better communication:** the internet and cheap phone calls.
- **Trade liberalisation:** lower tariffs, through the WTO and trade deals.
- **Freer flows of capital:** fewer controls on moving money between countries.
- **The opening up of economies,** such as China and the countries of Eastern Europe.
- **Growth of multinationals,** which look for lower costs and new markets.

## Multinational corporations

A **[[multinational corporation|multinational corporation (MNC)]]** is a firm that operates in more than one country. MNCs drive globalisation by moving production to where it's cheapest, spreading technology and building global brands.

**In host countries, they can:**

- Create jobs and pay better wages than local firms.
- Bring in capital, technology and skills.
- Raise tax revenue and exports.

**But they can also:**

- Take profits back home.
- Push out local firms.
- Exploit weak labour and environmental rules.
- Use **transfer pricing** to move profits to low-tax countries.
- Gain political influence.

## Consequences for less-developed countries

**Benefits:** more jobs and exports, foreign investment and technology, faster growth, and hundreds of millions of people have left extreme poverty.

**Costs:** low wages and poor conditions in some industries, damage to the environment, reliance on a few exports, and the loss of skilled workers abroad. Gains may be unevenly shared.

## Consequences for more-developed countries

**Benefits:** cheaper goods, more choice, larger export markets, returns on investment abroad and access to global talent.

**Costs:** loss of manufacturing jobs to lower-cost countries, downward pressure on the wages of low-skilled workers, and exposure to shocks from elsewhere. Structural unemployment has been concentrated in particular regions.

Globalisation also makes countries more **interdependent**, so a crisis or a supply problem in one country spreads quickly, as seen in 2008 and in the pandemic.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain how multinational corporations could benefit a less-developed country. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** A multinational corporation is a firm that operates in more than one country. Foreign direct investment is investment in another country's productive assets.

**Application:** A car maker might build a factory in a country with low wages.

**Analysis:** This creates jobs, often at higher wages than local firms pay, so incomes and spending rise, and the multiplier adds to the effect. The firm also brings new technology and trains workers, which raises human capital and productivity. Exports rise, and taxes bring in revenue that can pay for schools and roads, which supports long-run growth.
:::

Why it scores: it follows both the demand-side and the supply-side effects.
