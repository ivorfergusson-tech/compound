## Why information matters

To make a rational decision, you need to know your options, their prices, their quality and what they'll cost you later. In the real world, that information is often missing, wrong, too complicated or too slow and costly to find.

**[[imperfect information|Imperfect information]]** means buyers, sellers or both don't have all the information they need to make a rational decision.

Examples:

- **Choosing a university course:** you can't really know what it's like or what it'll lead to until you've done it.
- **Pensions and investments:** products can be too complicated to compare, and the results come decades later.
- **Health:** people may not fully see the long-term harm of smoking or the long-term benefit of exercise.
- **Prices:** you can't check every shop for the lowest price, so you may pay more than you need to.

With imperfect information, people can **consume too much** of things that harm them and **too little** of things that help them. That makes it a potential source of **[[market failure]]**: the market doesn't allocate resources in the best way. You'll return to this in 4.1.8.

## Asymmetric information

**[[asymmetric information|Asymmetric information]]** is a special case: **one party to a deal knows more than the other**.

- **Used cars:** the seller knows if the car has a fault; the buyer doesn't.
- **Insurance:** the buyer knows more about their own health or driving habits than the insurer does.
- **Car repairs and dentists:** the expert tells you what you need, and also sells it to you.
- **Bank loans:** the borrower knows more about whether they'll repay than the bank does.

The side with more information can use it to their advantage. A garage could recommend repairs you don't need, and you wouldn't know.

## Why asymmetric information matters

Asymmetric information can damage a whole market, not just one deal. Take used cars:

1. Buyers can't tell good cars from bad ones, so they'll only pay an average price.
2. Owners of good cars find that price too low, so they don't sell.
3. Fewer good cars are left on sale, so the average quality falls.
4. Buyers trust the market less and offer less, and so on.

Good products get pushed out, and people who would have happily traded don't. Resources are misallocated, which is market failure.

The problem can be reduced by:

- **Regulation:** food labelling, the FCA's rules on financial products, consumer protection law.
- **Providing information:** warnings on cigarette packets, government comparison data.
- **Trust signals:** warranties, reviews, MOT certificates, brand reputation.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain why asymmetric information may lead to market failure in the market for used cars. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Asymmetric information exists when one party to a transaction has more information than the other. Market failure occurs when the price mechanism allocates scarce resources inefficiently.

**Application:** In the used car market, the seller knows the car's history and faults, but the buyer does not.

**Analysis:** Because buyers cannot judge quality, they are only willing to pay a price based on average quality. This is too low for owners of good cars, so they withdraw from the market. The average quality of cars for sale then falls, and buyers offer even less. Some mutually beneficial trades do not happen, so too few good used cars are bought and sold, and resources are misallocated.
:::

Why it scores: it defines both terms, applies them to the car market, and develops a chain ending in the link to misallocated resources, which is what the question asks for.
