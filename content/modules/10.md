## What "risk" means

In everyday speech, risk means "something bad might happen". In investing it has two main meanings:

1. **The chance of a permanent loss:** a company goes bust, or you sell after a fall and never get back in.
2. **Uncertainty:** how far the result could be from what you expected, in either direction.

The second is measured by **[[volatility]]**: how much an investment's price swings around. Cash in a bank barely moves. A global fund of shares might rise 20% one year and fall 15% the next. A single small company can double or halve in months.

Volatility matters because of **you**. If you might need the money at a bad moment, or might panic and sell when prices fall, big swings can turn into real, permanent losses.

## Risk and return go together

Here's the core trade-off: **investors demand to be paid for taking risk**. If two investments offered the same expected return, everyone would buy the safer one. So the riskier one has to offer more on average, or nobody would hold it.

That extra expected return is called the **[[risk premium]]**. For shares over safe cash, it's called the **equity risk premium**. In the US, shares beat inflation by {{fig:usLongRun+}}: the gap is the reward for living with the swings.

But notice the word **expected**. A risk premium is an average over long periods. In any single year, or even a decade, riskier investments can do worse than safe ones. That's what makes them risky.

## Drawdowns: how bad can it get?

A **[[drawdown]]** is a fall from a previous high to a low. Some real UK examples:

- In the **2008 financial crisis**, the FTSE 100 fell by {{fig:ftse2008+}}.
- In the **Covid crash**, it fell {{fig:ftse2020+}}.

Both recovered in time, though the recovery after 2008 took years. Falls like these are normal for shares: anyone investing for decades should expect to live through several.

Losses and gains aren't symmetrical. A 50% fall needs a **100% gain** to get back to where you started, because the recovery starts from half the money.

## Diversification: the one free lunch

Owning one company exposes you to two kinds of risk:

- **Company risk** (also called specific risk): things that hit just that firm: a failed product, a scandal, a new competitor, a bad boss.
- **Market risk** (systematic risk): things that hit almost every company at once: recessions, interest rate jumps, pandemics.

**[[diversification|Diversification]]** means spreading your money over many investments so that company risk largely cancels out. When one company has a bad year, others have good ones. Market risk doesn't go away, because it affects everyone.

Here's the crucial point. Investors **aren't paid** for taking company risk, because anyone can remove it cheaply by diversifying. So a single share has **more risk but no more expected return** than a whole market of similar shares. That's why diversification is called the only "free lunch" in investing: less risk, same expected return.

Back to the question at the start. The 500 companies and the one company have roughly the **same expected return**, but the single company is **far riskier**: you could lose most of your money, and you aren't paid extra for that chance.

## Correlation: why spreading works

How much diversification helps depends on **[[correlation]]**: how closely two investments move together.

- **Correlation of 1:** they move in lockstep. Spreading between them does nothing.
- **Correlation of 0:** their moves are unrelated. Spreading cuts risk a lot.
- **Negative correlation:** one tends to rise when the other falls. Combining them can smooth the ride a lot.

Shares in different companies are usually positively correlated (they all react to the economy), but not perfectly, so owning many still helps. In the calculator, set correlation to 1 and watch diversification stop working.

This is also why people mix **different kinds** of investment. Bonds have often (though not always) held up when shares fell, so a mix of shares and bonds swings less than shares alone. Spreading across **countries** helps too: the UK market is only a small part of the world's.

## Time and risk

The longer you hold a diversified investment, the more the good and bad years tend to even out. Over one year, shares might rise or fall a lot. Over 20 years, history shows far fewer periods where a broad spread of shares lost money after inflation, though there have still been some.

That's why the usual rule ties investments to timeframes:

- **Under 5 years:** cash, where the amount can't fall.
- **5 to 10 years:** a mix, perhaps leaning to bonds as the date nears.
- **10+ years:** more in shares can make sense, if you can live with the swings.

**Your risk tolerance** isn't just a number: it's how you'd actually behave if your investments fell by a third. If you'd sell in a panic, you're taking more risk than you can handle, whatever a questionnaire says.

## The common mistake

**"I'll only buy companies I know well, so I'm not taking much risk."**

Knowing a company well feels safe, but it doesn't remove company risk. Plenty of well-known companies have collapsed, and many investors have lost money by putting too much in one familiar name, often their own employer. Knowing a company doesn't change the fact that one company is much riskier than many.

## In real life

- **Diversify widely:** across many companies, industries and countries. A global index fund does this in one go.
- **Match risk to timeframe.** Money needed soon shouldn't be in shares.
- **Expect big falls.** Plan for a 30–50% drop in shares at some point, and decide now that you won't sell in a panic.
- **Watch for hidden concentration**, like a big chunk of your wealth in your employer's shares.
- **Red flag:** anyone promising high returns with "no risk". It breaks the basic rule of investing.

## Think about it

:::reveal If diversification cuts risk without cutting expected return, why don't all investors hold a whole-world fund?
Many do, and it's a sensible default. Some people believe they can pick winners (most can't, module 14); some feel more comfortable with what they know (module 26); some have specific reasons, like ethical choices or needing income.
:::

:::reveal Why might someone close to retirement hold more bonds than a 25-year-old?
They have less time to recover from a big fall and may need to start taking money out soon. Selling shares after a crash locks in the loss. Bonds and cash reduce the swings in the years when a fall would hurt most.
:::
