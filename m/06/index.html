How stock markets work

Recap

In module 2 you met shares: small slices of ownership in a company, which can pay dividends and rise or fall in price. This module goes behind the price: where shares are bought and sold, why the price moves every second, and what people mean when the news says "the FTSE 100 fell 2% today".

Before you read

A company announces that its profit this year was up 10%. Its share price falls 8% that morning.

The idea in one sentence

A stock market is a meeting place for buyers and sellers of shares, and prices move because people keep changing their minds about what companies will earn in the future, not what they earned in the past.

The stock exchange

A stock exchange is a regulated marketplace where shares in companies are bought and sold. The UK's main one is the London Stock Exchange (LSE), whose roots go back to traders meeting in City of London coffee houses in the 1600s and 1700s. Today it's entirely electronic.

Its main market is open on weekdays from 8am to 4.30pm UK time. Other big exchanges include the New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq in the US, and exchanges in Tokyo, Hong Kong and across Europe.

Two kinds of market happen on an exchange:

  • The primary market: a company sells new shares to raise money. This is when the company itself gets cash.
  • The secondary market: investors buy and sell existing shares with each other. The company doesn't get any money from these trades; it's just changing owners. Almost all daily trading is secondary.

You don't trade on the exchange directly. You use a broker or investment platform, which places your order for you (module 7 covers how orders work).

Going public: the IPO

Most companies start private: owned by founders, staff and private investors, with shares that can't be bought on an exchange. When a company wants to raise a lot of money, or let its early owners sell, it can float on a stock exchange through an IPO (Initial Public Offering).

In an IPO, the company (with help from investment banks, module 16) sets a price, sells new shares to investors, and then its shares start trading publicly. From then on, it's a listed or public company. It has to publish its accounts (module 18), follow stricter rules and keep shareholders informed.

Why prices move

A share price is simply the last price at which a buyer and a seller agreed to trade. If more people want to buy than sell at today's price, the price rises until enough sellers appear. If more want to sell, it falls.

What makes people want to buy or sell? Mostly their view of the company's future:

  • Profits and growth. Investors are really buying a share of future profits. Anything that changes expected profits moves the price.
  • Surprises versus expectations. The price already reflects what investors expect. Back to the question at the start: if everyone expected profit to be up 20%, a 10% rise is a disappointment, and the price can fall. Prices react to the gap between results and expectations, not to the results themselves.
  • Interest rates. When rates rise, savings and bonds pay more, so shares look less attractive by comparison, and future profits are worth less today (module 22).
  • The wider economy, politics and news. Recession fears, wars, elections and new rules all shift expectations.
  • Mood. Fear and greed: crowds sometimes push prices far above or below what the numbers justify (module 26).

Over a day or a month, these swings can look random. Over many years, share prices have tended to follow companies' profits.

Market cap: how big is a company?

A company's market cap (market capitalisation) is its total value on the stock market:

market cap = share price × number of shares

A company with 2 billion shares at £5 each has a market cap of £10 billion. A high share price on its own doesn't mean a company is big: a £100 share in a company with 10 million shares is worth only £1 billion in total.

People group companies by size: large caps (the biggest), mid caps and small caps. Smaller companies tend to be riskier, with bigger price swings, but can grow faster.

Indices: measuring the market

An index (plural: indices) tracks the value of a group of shares, so you can see how a market is doing in one number.

The UK's best-known index is the FTSE 100 (said "footsie"): the 100 largest companies listed on the London Stock Exchange's main market, by market cap. It started on 3 January 1984 at a value of 1,000, and its members are reviewed every quarter, so companies that grow join and those that shrink drop out.

Other indices you'll hear about:

  • FTSE 250: the next 250 UK companies by size, often seen as more focused on the UK economy.
  • FTSE All-Share: most of the UK stock market in one index.
  • S&P 500: about 500 large US companies.
  • MSCI World: large and mid-sized companies across many developed countries.

Most big indices are market-cap weighted: bigger companies count for more. That means a few giants can drive the whole index. Try it below: nudge Big Co's price and the index jumps; double Small Co's and the index hardly moves.

Price index vs total return. The FTSE 100 number you hear on the news only tracks prices. It leaves out dividends. For an index that pays out a lot in dividends, the total return (prices plus dividends reinvested) can be much higher than the headline number suggests. When comparing investments, always compare total returns.

Who's buying and selling?

On any day the market is a mix of:

  • Individual investors like you, mostly through platforms and pensions.
  • Institutions: pension funds, insurers and fund managers (module 20), which own most shares and move the biggest amounts.
  • Traders and market makers, who buy and sell constantly and help make sure there's always someone to trade with (module 7).

Because institutions are so big, and much trading is done by computers in fractions of a second, individuals are rarely the ones who move prices. That's one reason trying to outguess the market is hard (modules 3 and 14).

The common mistake

"A £1 share is cheaper than a £50 share, so it has more room to grow."

The price per share tells you nothing about whether a company is cheap or expensive. A £1 share in a company with 50 billion shares is a £50 billion company; a £50 share with 10 million shares is a £500 million one. What matters is the company's total value compared with its profits and prospects (module 22). Companies can even split their shares, say turning one £50 share into ten £5 shares, without changing anything real.

In real life

  • The news headline is the price index. "FTSE 100 down 2%" leaves out dividends and tells you about the biggest UK companies only.
  • Daily moves are mostly noise for a long-term investor.
  • Check an index's make-up before investing in a fund that tracks it. A UK index is heavy in a few industries; a global index spreads much wider.
  • Red flag: "pre-IPO" shares offered by cold callers, or tips on social media about a "certain" price jump. These are common scams.

Think about it

Why might a company's share price rise on the day it announces job cuts?

Investors may expect lower costs to mean higher future profits. Prices reflect expected future profits, not whether the news is good for everyone. That's uncomfortable, but it's how markets price companies.

If the FTSE 100 is at roughly the same level as 20 years ago, does that mean UK investors made nothing?

No. The FTSE 100 headline number leaves out dividends, which for UK large companies have been a big part of returns. A total return index, with dividends reinvested, would show a much better result over that time.
