Recap

Module 18 taught you to read a company's accounts using our made-up firm, Brightside Bakeries: revenue of £200m, operating profit of £30m, net profit of £20m, earnings per share (eps) of 20p, borrowings of £80m and cash of £10m. Module 1 showed that money grows with compound interest. This module runs that idea backwards to answer the question investors argue about every day: what is a company actually worth?

Before you read

Someone offers you £100 in five years' time. You could instead put money in an investment you're confident will earn 9% a year.

The idea in one sentence

A company is worth the cash it will produce in the future, shrunk back to today's money; ratios like P/E are quick shortcuts for comparing, and a DCF is the full calculation, but both are only as good as the guesses that go into them.

Price is what you pay, value is what you get

A share's price is whatever buyers and sellers agree today. Its value is what it's really worth to an owner, based on the cash the business will earn. Investors buy when they think value is above price and sell when it's below. They disagree all the time, which is why markets exist.

Analysts use two kinds of tool:

  1. Ratios (relative valuation): compare a company's price with its profits, and with similar companies.
  2. Discounted cash flow (absolute valuation): estimate all its future cash and work out what that's worth today.

The P/E ratio

The most famous ratio is the price/earnings ratio (P/E):

P/E = share price ÷ earnings per share

If Brightside's shares cost £3.00 and its EPS is 20p, its P/E is 3.00 ÷ 0.20 = 15. You're paying 15 times one year's profit per share. Put another way, if profits stayed flat forever, it would take 15 years of earnings to "earn back" the price.

What P/E tells you:

  • A high P/E (say 30+) usually means investors expect strong growth, or see the company as very safe.
  • A low P/E (say under 10) can mean the market expects trouble, or slow growth, or that the shares are cheap. It's not automatically a bargain.
  • Compare like with like: the same industry, similar companies, and the company's own history.

The upside-down version, EPS ÷ price, is the earnings yield: 0.20 ÷ 3.00 = 6.7%. It's handy for comparing with bond and savings rates.

EV/EBITDA: allowing for debt

P/E only looks at shareholders. But if you bought a whole company, you'd also take on its debt (and get its cash). Enterprise value (EV) is the price of the whole business:

EV = market cap + debt − cash

Brightside: 100 million shares × £3.00 = £300m market cap, plus £80m of debt, minus £10m of cash, gives an EV of £370m.

EBITDA is earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation: roughly operating profit before the yearly cost of wearing out equipment. Say Brightside's equipment wears out at £10m a year: EBITDA = £30m + £10m = £40m.

EV/EBITDA = 370 ÷ 40 = about 9.3

Analysts like EV/EBITDA for comparing companies with different amounts of debt, because it values the whole business, not just the shares. It's widely used in deal-making (module 16). Its weakness: EBITDA ignores real costs like replacing equipment, so it can flatter companies that need to spend a lot to stand still.

Present value: money later is worth less

Back to the question at the start. If you can earn 9% a year elsewhere, £100 in five years is worth what you'd need to invest today to get £100 then:

present value = 100 ÷ 1.09⁵ = £64.99

Paying more than about £65 would mean accepting less than 9% a year. This is compounding (module 1) in reverse, called discounting. The rate you use is the discount rate: the return you require to make the wait and the risk worthwhile.

Three things raise the discount rate, and so lower the value of future money:

  • Higher interest rates. If safe savings pay more, you demand more from riskier investments.
  • More risk. Uncertain cash deserves a bigger discount than safe cash.
  • Longer waits. Each extra year divides the value by the rate again.

This is why share prices often fall when interest rates rise (module 12): the same future profits are worth less today.

The DCF: valuing a whole company

A discounted cash flow (DCF) valuation adds up the present value of all a company's future free cash flow:

  1. Forecast free cash flow for the next few years (often five).
  2. Estimate a terminal value for everything after that, usually by assuming slow steady growth forever.
  3. Discount each year's cash, and the terminal value, back to today at the discount rate.
  4. Add them up: that's the company's value. Divide by the number of shares for value per share.

Try a company with £100m of free cash flow growing 8% a year for five years, then 2% forever, discounted at 9%. It comes out at about £1.88bn, and roughly three-quarters of that is the terminal value: the years after the forecast.

Now change the discount rate to 10%: the value drops to about £1.64bn. Change it to 8%: it jumps to £2.2bn. One percentage point either way moves the answer by over 10%.

That's the honest lesson of DCF. It forces you to think clearly about what drives value (cash, growth, risk, time), but the answer is very sensitive to guesses about the distant future. Professionals use it to test their assumptions ("what growth would justify today's price?") rather than as a precise truth.

Putting it together

Good valuation work combines both approaches:

  • Ratios tell you how the market prices similar companies right now.
  • A DCF tells you what the business might be worth on your own assumptions.
  • If they disagree wildly, find out why: maybe the market knows something, or maybe it's wrong.

And remember module 14: professional analysts do this full time, and most active funds still don't beat the index. Understanding valuation makes you a smarter investor and a stronger candidate for finance jobs; it doesn't make beating the market easy.

The common mistake

"A low P/E means the shares are cheap."

A low P/E can mean a bargain, but more often it means the market expects profits to fall: the business is shrinking, facing competition, or carrying risk. That's called a value trap. Always ask why it's cheap before deciding it's a bargain.

In real life

  • Find P/E ratios on any finance site; compare with the same industry and the company's history.
  • Check debt: a low P/E with lots of debt can be riskier than it looks. EV/EBITDA helps.
  • Treat any DCF as a range, not a single number.
  • Red flag: a tip that a share is "worth double its price" with no assumptions shown.

Think about it

Why might a fast-growing tech company have a P/E of 60 while a utility has a P/E of 12?

Investors expect the tech firm's profits to grow fast, so today's profit is a small part of its future value. The utility's profits are steady but slow-growing. P/E reflects expected growth and risk, not just current profit.

Why does most of a DCF's value often come from the terminal value, and why is that a problem?

Because a healthy company is expected to keep producing cash for decades beyond a 5-year forecast. But that's exactly the part we know least about, so small changes in the long-run growth or discount rate swing the whole valuation.
