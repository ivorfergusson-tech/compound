## Why economic activity happens

The central purpose of economic activity is to **produce goods and services that satisfy people's needs and wants**.

- **[[needs|Needs]]** are things we must have to survive: food, water, shelter, warmth.
- **[[wants|Wants]]** are things we would like but could live without: a new phone, a holiday, a takeaway.
- **[[goods|Goods]]** are physical products you can touch (a pizza, a car). **[[services|Services]]** are things done for you (a haircut, a bus journey, a lesson).

Economists treat wants as **unlimited**: once one want is met, people find another. That's what makes resources scarce, which is the next lesson.

## The three key questions

Every economy, from a village to the UK, has to answer three questions:

1. **What to produce?** More hospitals or more roads? More cars or more bikes?
2. **How to produce it?** With lots of workers or lots of machines? In the UK or abroad?
3. **Who benefits?** Who gets the goods and services: whoever can pay, everyone equally, those in most need?

Different economic systems answer them differently. In a **market economy**, prices and the choices of buyers and sellers do most of the deciding. In a **planned economy**, the government decides. The UK is a **mixed economy**: mostly markets, but the state provides things like the NHS and schools. You'll study this properly in 4.1.2 and 4.1.8.

## The four factors of production

Economists group every resource used in production into four **[[factors of production]]**:

| Factor | What it is | Pizza example | Reward |
|---|---|---|---|
| **[[land|Land]]** | Natural resources: the ground, water, minerals, oil, fish, wheat as it grows | The wheat in the flour, the gas in the oven, the land the shop is on | Rent |
| **[[labour|Labour]]** | People's work, both physical and mental | The chef, the delivery rider | Wages |
| **[[capital|Capital]]** | Man-made goods used to make other goods | The pizza oven, the delivery bike, the shop | Interest |
| **[[enterprise|Enterprise]]** | Organising the other three and taking the risk | The owner who started the takeaway | Profit |

Two traps:

- **Capital means physical things used in production**, like machines and buildings, not money. Money in the bank isn't capital in this sense; it's what you might use to buy capital.
- **Capital goods** (an oven) are used to make other things. **[[consumer goods|Consumer goods]]** (the pizza) are used up by the people who buy them. The same item can be either: a car bought for a taxi firm is a capital good, the same car bought for family use is a consumer good.

The person who supplies enterprise is an **[[entrepreneur]]**: they spot an opportunity, bring land, labour and capital together, and risk losing money if it fails. Profit is the reward for taking that risk.

## The environment is a scarce resource

Clean air, a stable climate, rivers and forests are part of **land**. For a long time they were treated as if they were free and unlimited. They aren't.

- Some resources are **[[non-renewable resource|non-renewable]]**: once used, they're gone (oil, gas, coal).
- Some are **[[renewable resource|renewable]]** but can still run out if used faster than they recover: fish stocks, forests, clean water.
- Using the environment to absorb pollution uses it up too: a polluted river can't also be used for fishing or drinking.

So the environment is a **scarce resource** with an [[opportunity cost]]: using it one way means losing other uses. This idea comes back when you study externalities (4.1.8).

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain what is meant by capital as a factor of production, and why it is different from money. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Capital is a man-made resource used to produce other goods and services, such as machinery, tools, vehicles and factories. Its reward is interest.

**Application:** A bakery's ovens and delivery van are capital, because they are used to produce and deliver bread, not consumed for their own sake.

**Analysis:** Money is not capital in this sense, because money itself produces nothing. It is a means of buying resources: a firm could borrow money to buy a new oven, and it is the oven that adds to the bakery's capacity to produce, so it is the oven that counts as capital.
:::

Why it scores: a precise definition, a clear example in a real setting, and a reasoned explanation of the difference rather than just stating it.
