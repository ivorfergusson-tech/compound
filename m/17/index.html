Before you read

You're 22 and saving for a first flat. You can put away £2,000 a year for 5 years. The government offers to add 25% to some kinds of saving for a first home.

The idea in one sentence

An ISA isn't an investment, it's a tax-free wrapper you put savings or investments inside; each type suits a different goal, and the yearly allowance is use-it-or-lose-it.

What an ISA is

An ISA (Individual Savings Account) is a wrapper: a container with special tax rules. Whatever you hold inside it, cash or investments, grows free of UK tax:

  • no income tax on interest,
  • no tax on dividends from shares,
  • no Capital Gains Tax when investments rise in value,
  • and you don't have to declare any of it on a tax return.

The wrapper itself isn't the investment. A Cash ISA is a savings account inside the wrapper; a Stocks and Shares ISA holds funds or shares inside it. It's like a shopping bag that's exempt from tax: what matters is what you put in the bag.

The allowance

Each tax year you can put up to the ISA allowance into ISAs: £20,000 (2026/27 tax year). You can split it however you like across the types, but the total across all of them can't go over £20,000.

  • It's use it or lose it: unused allowance doesn't roll over to next year. It resets on 6 April.
  • It counts what you pay in, not what the account grows to. Interest and growth don't use up allowance.
  • Since April 2024 you can pay into more than one ISA of the same type in a tax year, and move part of an ISA to another provider.
  • Taking money out of most ISAs doesn't give you allowance back, unless the ISA is flexible (some Cash ISAs are). If yours is flexible, you can take money out and put it back in the same tax year without using more allowance.

You must be 18 or over to open an adult ISA, and a UK resident.

The four adult ISAs

Cash ISA. A savings account inside the wrapper: easy-access, notice or fixed rate. Safe, with interest tax-free. Covered by the FSCS like other bank savings.

Stocks and Shares ISA. Holds investments: funds, shares, bonds. Can go up and down in value, so it's for money you won't need for at least five years. This is where most long-term investing in the UK happens (modules 14 and 30).

Lifetime ISA (LISA). For a first home or later life, with a government bonus. More below.

Innovative Finance ISA. Holds peer-to-peer loans and similar. Higher risk and not FSCS-protected in the same way; most beginners never need one.

There's also the Junior ISA for under-18s, opened by a parent or guardian. Up to £9,000 (2026/27, separate from the adult allowance) can go in each year. The child can't take money out until 18, when it becomes an adult ISA in their name.

Do you even need a Cash ISA?

Here's the honest part. Most people's savings interest is already tax-free outside an ISA, thanks to the Personal Savings Allowance: £1,000 (2026/27, basic-rate taxpayers (£500 higher-rate, £0 additional-rate); from 6 April 2027 tax on savings above it rises to 24%, 44% and 49% (Budgets 2025 and 2026)).

£1,500 at 4.5% earns £67.50 a year. Even £20,000 at 4.5% earns £900, still under a basic-rate taxpayer's £1,000. So for many people starting out, a normal savings account paying a better rate beats a Cash ISA paying a worse one.

Cash ISAs make more sense when:

  • you're a higher-rate taxpayer (only £500 tax-free) or additional-rate (£0),
  • your savings are large enough that interest would go over your allowance,
  • rates are high, so interest is large,
  • or you want to build up tax-free savings for the long term, because ISA money stays tax-free forever, whatever your future income.

A planned cut was dropped. The government had planned to limit how much under-65s could put into Cash ISAs from April 2027. The October 2026 Budget scrapped that, so you can still put the whole £20,000 into cash if you want to.

Stocks and Shares ISAs in brief

If you're investing for five years or more, a Stocks and Shares ISA is usually the first place to do it. Gains and dividends are tax-free, which matters more as the pot grows. Over decades, a large investment pot outside an ISA could face Capital Gains Tax and dividend tax; inside, none.

Things to know (modules 10, 14 and 30 go deeper):

  • The value can fall. Don't invest money you'll need soon.
  • Fees matter. The platform charges a fee, and each fund has its own charge. Low-cost index funds keep more of the return for you.
  • Any UK investment platform offers them: you open the ISA, pay in, then choose what to buy inside it.

The Lifetime ISA

The Lifetime ISA is designed for two goals: buying your first home, or saving for later life. The rules (£4,000 (2026/27; open at 18 to 39, pay in until 50, 25% government bonus up to £1,000 a year)):

  • You can open one from 18 until your 40th birthday.
  • You can pay in up to £4,000 a year until you're 50. This counts towards your £20,000 ISA allowance.
  • The government adds a 25% bonus: up to £1,000 a year on £4,000.
  • It can be a Cash or Stocks and Shares Lifetime ISA.

You can take money out without a charge to:

  • buy your first home, costing £450,000 or less, at least 12 months after opening, using a mortgage and a conveyancer;
  • or once you're 60;
  • or if you're terminally ill.

For anything else, you pay a 25% withdrawal charge on what you take out. That's more than the bonus. Put in £1,000, get £250 bonus, total £1,250. Take it out early and 25% of £1,250 is £312.50, leaving £937.50: you've lost £62.50 of your own money, not just the bonus.

Back to the question at the start. £2,000 a year for 5 years is £10,000 of your money, and the government adds £2,500. At 4% growth, the pot reaches about £14,082, against about £11,266 without the bonus.

The house price cap is a real limit. £450,000 has been the limit since the LISA launched in 2017, while house prices have risen. In expensive areas, many first homes cost more. Since 29 October 2026, using a LISA on one of those costs a 20% charge, not 25%. On £1,250 that's £250: you lose the bonus but get your own money back.

The LISA is being replaced. The government plans a new First Time Buyer ISA (confirmed in the October 2026 Budget: it replaces the Lifetime ISA for new savers from April 2029; the details and the law are still to come; existing Lifetime ISA holders can keep using theirs). If you're thinking of opening one, check gov.uk for the latest.

Choosing between them

A rough guide, not advice:

Goal Timeframe Often fits
Emergency fund any time easy-access savings or flexible Cash ISA
Car, holiday, course within ~5 years savings account or Cash ISA
First home under the price cap 1+ years Lifetime ISA (if you're 18–39)
Long-term wealth 5+ years Stocks and Shares ISA
Retirement decades workplace pension first (module 21), then ISAs

The order people often follow: emergency fund → clear expensive debt → take any employer pension match → ISAs for medium and long-term goals.

The common mistake

"I opened an ISA, so my money is invested."

Lots of people pay into a Stocks and Shares ISA and never choose an investment, so the money sits there as uninvested cash, often earning little or nothing. The ISA is just the wrapper. After paying in, you have to buy something inside it (for example, a low-cost global index fund) for it to be invested.

In real life

  • Check you're not paying tax you don't need to. If your interest is under your Personal Savings Allowance, choose the best rate, ISA or not.
  • Use the allowance before 5 April if you have money to protect for the long term. It doesn't roll over.
  • LISA for a first home? Check the price cap against prices where you want to live, open it early (the 12-month rule), and remember the charges (20% above the cap, 25% for anything else).
  • Watch the 2027 Cash ISA change and the LISA replacement on gov.uk.
  • Red flag: "high-return ISAs" promising fixed returns well above savings rates. Check whether it's really FSCS-protected cash, and check the firm on the FCA register.

Think about it

Why might a 25-year-old on a basic-rate salary still use a Stocks and Shares ISA, even though they pay no tax on small gains now?

Because the wrapper protects the pot forever. Twenty years of growth could make the pot large, and their income might rise into higher-rate tax. Inside the ISA, all future dividends and gains stay tax-free with no paperwork, whatever their income becomes.

Who might be better off with a workplace pension than a Lifetime ISA for retirement?

Most employees. A workplace pension gets employer contributions and tax relief, which usually beat the LISA's 25% bonus. The LISA can make sense for retirement for some self-employed people, who don't get employer contributions, but they should compare the options carefully.
