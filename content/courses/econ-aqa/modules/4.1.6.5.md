:::callout idea
A union's effect depends on the market it enters: in a competitive labour market a higher wage costs jobs, but in a monopsony it can raise both pay and employment.
:::

## What trade unions do

A **[[trade union]]** is an organisation of workers that aims to improve pay and conditions. Its main tool is **collective bargaining**: negotiating on behalf of all its members, not one at a time.

Unions can use **industrial action**, such as strikes or an overtime ban, if talks fail.

## What affects a union's power?

A union is better able to influence wages and employment when:

- **Membership is high,** so that the union speaks for most of the workers.
- **Workers are hard to replace,** because of their skills or the law.
- **Demand for labour is inelastic,** so higher wages cost few jobs.
- **The employer can afford to pay,** thanks to high profits.
- **Demand for the product is inelastic,** so higher costs can be passed on.
- **Strikes do serious harm** to the employer or the public.
- **The economy is strong,** with low unemployment.
- **The law allows it.** UK law on ballots and strikes limits what unions can do.

## A union in a perfectly competitive labour market

Suppose a union enters a competitive market and bargains for a wage **above** equilibrium. The wage becomes a floor.

- Firms hire **fewer** workers, moving up the MRP curve.
- **More** workers want the job at this wage.
- There's **excess supply** of labour, which means unemployment.

So members who keep their jobs gain, and those who lose out are the workers who no longer get hired. You can try this with the slider in lesson 4.1.6.3.

## A union in a monopsony

The result is very different here. In a monopsony, the employer holds wages and jobs down. A union can act as a counterweight, which is sometimes called **bilateral monopoly**.

With a union-set wage, the firm can hire more workers without raising the wage of those it already has. So MCL becomes **flat** at that wage, up to the supply curve.

So up to the competitive wage, a union can raise **both** wages and employment. Above it, jobs start to fall.

## Other effects

Unions can also improve things beyond pay. By giving workers a voice, they can raise morale and reduce staff turnover, which could raise productivity. If productivity rises, MRP rises, so the extra wage needn't cost jobs.

On the other hand, strikes can lose output, and they may raise costs and prices.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain how a trade union could raise both wages and employment in a labour market. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** A monopsonist is a single buyer of labour, which hires where MCL = MRP and so holds wages and employment below their competitive levels.

**Application:** Suppose a large employer in a town pays £50, when the competitive wage would be £65.

**Analysis:** If a union agrees a wage of, say, £60, the firm no longer has to raise everyone's pay to hire one more worker, so its MCL is flat at £60 up to the supply curve. Extra workers now cost the firm less than their MRP, so it hires more. At this higher wage, more workers are also willing to work. Both pay and employment rise, as long as the wage doesn't go above the competitive level.
:::

Why it scores: it explains the change in MCL, which is the reason employment rises.
