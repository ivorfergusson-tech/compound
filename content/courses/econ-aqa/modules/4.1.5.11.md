## Guess first

You'd happily pay £15 for a pizza, and it costs £10.

:::guess {"prompt":"What do you gain from buying it? And what does the shop gain, if it would have sold it for £6?","kind":"text","id":"econ-aqa:4.1.5.11-guess"}
:::

:::callout idea
Consumer and producer surplus measure what buyers and sellers gain from trade, so they show who wins and who loses when a market changes, and how much welfare is lost altogether.
:::

## Consumer surplus

**[[consumer surplus|Consumer surplus]]** is the difference between what consumers are willing to pay and what they actually pay. In the pizza example it's £5.

On a diagram, it's the area **below the demand curve and above the price**.

## Producer surplus

**[[producer surplus|Producer surplus]]** is the difference between the price firms receive and the lowest price they'd accept. In the pizza example it's £4.

On a diagram, it's the area **above the supply curve and below the price**.

**Economic welfare** is consumer surplus + producer surplus. In a competitive market with no externalities, it's highest at the equilibrium, since every unit that's worth more to buyers than it costs to make gets made.

## The deadweight loss of monopoly

A monopolist restricts output to raise its price. Two things happen:

- Consumers pay more, so part of their surplus is **transferred** to the firm.
- Some units that consumers value above their cost aren't made. This welfare goes to **nobody**.

That lost welfare is the **[[deadweight loss|deadweight loss]]**, shown as a triangle between demand and supply, from the monopoly output to the competitive output.

Move the output slider down from 40 to see all three areas change.

:::chart surplus {"q":40}

At an output of 30 here, consumer surplus falls from £800 to £450, while producer surplus rises from £800 to £1,050. The two together fall from £1,600 to £1,500. The missing £100 is the deadweight loss.

## Price discrimination and welfare

Consumer and producer surplus also show the effects of price discrimination.

- Compared with a single price, consumers who pay more **lose** surplus, which goes to the firm.
- Consumers who pay less, or can now buy, **gain** surplus.
- Output may be higher, which can reduce the deadweight loss.

In the extreme case, each consumer pays the most they're willing to pay. All consumer surplus goes to the firm, and output can reach the competitive level, so there's no deadweight loss, even though consumers gain nothing.

So welfare analysis separates two questions: **how much** welfare there is, and **who gets it**.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Using a diagram, explain the welfare loss caused by monopoly. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Consumer surplus is the difference between what consumers are willing to pay and what they pay. Producer surplus is the difference between the price and the lowest price firms would accept.

**Application:** On a diagram, the competitive equilibrium is where demand meets supply. A monopolist restricts output to the left of this point and charges a higher price.

**Analysis:** Consumer surplus falls. Part of it becomes extra producer surplus, which is a transfer. But the units between the monopoly output and the competitive output are no longer made, even though consumers value them above their cost. The triangle of welfare on those units goes to neither group, and that's the deadweight loss.
:::

Why it scores: it separates the transfer from the deadweight loss, which is the part that weaker answers miss.
