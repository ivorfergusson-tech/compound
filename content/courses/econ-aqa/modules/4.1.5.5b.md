## The kinked demand curve

The **[[kinked demand curve]]** illustrates interdependence. It's one model of oligopoly, not the only one. It assumes a firm believes that:

- **If it raises its price, rivals won't follow.** It loses many customers, so demand above the current price is **elastic**.
- **If it cuts its price, rivals will match it.** It gains few customers, so demand below the current price is **inelastic**.

So either change would lower revenue, which gives the firm a reason to leave the price where it is.

The kink creates a vertical gap in the MR curve. If marginal cost moves within that gap, the profit-maximising price and output stay the same. Raise the slider and see how large the cost rise has to be.

:::chart kinked {"c":10}

The model has limits. It doesn't explain how the price got there in the first place, and in real markets prices do change, especially when all firms' costs rise together.

## Cartels

A **[[cartel]]** is a formal agreement between firms to limit competition, for example by fixing prices or output. It lets them act like a monopolist. A well-known example between countries is OPEC, whose members agree oil output targets.

Cartels between firms are illegal in the UK and the EU. They're also hard to keep going, because:

- **Each member gains by cheating:** selling more than its quota at the high price.
- **More firms** make it harder to agree and to spot cheats.
- **Firms have different costs**, so they want different prices.
- **Falling demand** tempts firms to cut prices to protect sales.
- **Regulators** can find them and fine them.

## Price leadership, price agreements and price wars

- **[[price leadership|Price leadership]]:** one firm, often the largest, changes its price and the others follow. This can happen with no formal agreement, and the firms may avoid breaking the law.
- **Price agreements:** firms agree directly on the prices to charge. This is collusion, and usually illegal.
- **[[price war|Price wars]]:** firms repeatedly undercut each other to win market share. Consumers gain in the short run, while profits fall. A price war may also be used to drive weaker rivals out.

## Why compete in other ways?

A price cut can be copied at once, and it can lower everyone's profit. So oligopolists often use **non-price competition**:

- Advertising and branding, which build loyalty and make demand less elastic.
- Better products and research.
- Loyalty schemes, service and delivery.

A new feature or a strong brand is harder to copy than a price cut. Heavy advertising also acts as a **barrier to entry**, because a new firm would have to match it.

## Barriers to entry

Barriers protect the profits of oligopolists. They include:

- Economies of scale and high start-up costs.
- Brand loyalty and heavy advertising.
- Patents and control of supplies or outlets.
- Limit pricing: setting a low price so entry doesn't look worth it.

## What shapes prices, output and investment?

Decisions in oligopoly depend on:

- **Number and size of rivals**, and how likely they are to react.
- **Barriers to entry:** high barriers leave firms freer to raise prices.
- **Product differentiation:** this supports higher prices and advertising.
- **Costs and demand:** falling demand can start price wars.
- **Profits:** abnormal profit pays for research and investment.
- **Regulation:** the risk of fines limits collusion.

## Advantages and disadvantages of oligopoly

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Economies of scale can lower costs | Prices can be higher, especially with collusion |
| Abnormal profit can fund research and investment | Consumers lose out if firms avoid competing on price |
| Non-price competition brings better products and service | Spending on advertising adds to costs |
| Competition between a few firms can still be strong | Barriers can protect inefficient firms |

The result depends on how firms behave. A market of a few firms in fierce competition can serve consumers well, while one with a quiet understanding won't.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain why cartels often break down. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** A cartel is a formal agreement between firms to limit competition, for example by restricting output to keep prices high.

**Application:** If a cartel of four firms agrees to a high price, each is limited to a share of the market.

**Analysis:** Each firm can raise its own profit by secretly selling more than its quota at the high price. If one cheats and the others find out, they too may raise output, which pushes the price down and ends the agreement. It's harder to hold together when there are more firms or when demand falls.
:::

Why it scores: it explains the incentive to cheat and takes it through to the end of the cartel.
