## Guess first

A factory dumps waste into a river, and a fishing business downstream loses its catch. Nobody pays the fishing business anything.

:::guess {"prompt":"Is the factory producing the right amount? Who is paying for what it makes?","kind":"text","id":"econ-aqa:4.1.8.4-guess"}
:::

:::callout idea
Externalities are costs or benefits to third parties, so the market ignores them: too much is made when there are external costs, and too little when there are external benefits.
:::

## Private and social costs and benefits

An **[[externality]]** is a cost or benefit to a third party, who isn't involved in the transaction. An externality exists when there's a **divergence between private and social** costs and benefits.

- **Private cost:** what the producer or consumer pays.
- **External cost:** the cost to third parties.
- **Social cost = private cost + external cost.**

- **Private benefit:** what the producer or consumer gains.
- **External benefit:** the gain to third parties.
- **Social benefit = private benefit + external benefit.**

The market only takes account of private costs and benefits. The best outcome for society is where **marginal social benefit = marginal social cost (MSB = MSC)**.

| | In production | In consumption |
|---|---|---|
| **Negative** | A factory pollutes the air around it | A neighbour's loud music |
| **Positive** | A firm trains staff, who later work elsewhere | A vaccination protects other people too |

## Negative externalities: over-production

Take a factory that pollutes. Its private costs leave out the cost of pollution, so **MSC lies above MPC**.

The market produces where MPB = MPC. Society wants less, where MSB = MSC. So the market **over-produces**, and the price doesn't reflect the full cost.

The gap between MSC and MSB on the extra units is a **welfare loss**: each of those units costs society more than it's worth.

## Positive externalities: under-production

Take vaccination. The person vaccinated gains, and others gain too, as they're less likely to catch the disease. So **MSB lies above MPB**.

The market consumes where MPB = MPC. Society wants more, where MSB = MSC. So the market **under-produces**. Here the welfare loss is the value of the extra units that aren't made, though they're worth more to society than they cost.

## Why property rights matter

**[[property rights|Property rights]]** are legal rights to own and control a resource, and to be paid for its use.

The absence of property rights is a basic cause of externalities. Nobody owns the air or the sea, so nobody can charge a polluter or take them to court. If the river in our example was owned by the fishing business, the factory would have to pay to use it or face legal action. The external cost would become a private one.

So externalities in both production and consumption arise partly because these rights aren't clearly set out or can't be enforced. That's why extending property rights can be a solution, as you'll see in 4.1.8b.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Using a diagram, explain why negative production externalities lead to a misallocation of resources. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** A negative production externality is a cost to third parties, so marginal social cost is above marginal private cost.

**Application:** A steel plant's emissions harm the health of local people, and the firm doesn't pay this cost.

**Analysis:** On the diagram, the firm produces where MPB = MPC, at a higher output than the social optimum, where MSB = MSC. Firms ignore the external cost, so the price is too low and too much is produced. On the extra units, MSC is above MSB, so they cost society more than they're worth. This is a welfare loss, shown by the triangle between MSC and MSB, and it means too many resources are going to this industry.
:::

Why it scores: it says where both outcomes are on the diagram, and it explains the welfare loss.
