Before you read

You have a full-time job or school from 9am to 5pm, Monday to Friday.

The idea in one sentence

Pick one market that fits your hours, money and temperament, practise there with pretend money and strict records, and only judge yourself after many trades, because paper trading is where mistakes are free.

What paper trading is

Paper trading means making pretend trades with pretend money, but following real prices and real rules, and recording everything as if it were real. You find out:

  • whether your ideas would have made money after costs,
  • how often you follow your own rules,
  • how you feel and behave during losing runs.

All without risking a penny. The catch is that paper trading doesn't feel the same: losing pretend money doesn't hurt like losing real money, so people often trade more boldly on paper than they would for real. Treat your paper account as if it were real, with a realistic starting balance and the same position sizes you'd really use (module 23).

You can paper trade with a spreadsheet and live prices from any finance website, or with a provider's demo account. If you use a demo, be wary: many are set up to encourage high leverage and lots of trades, because that's what the provider earns from.

Choosing your market

Different asset classes behave very differently. The main ones retail traders look at:

Market What moves it Hours (UK time) Things to know
UK shares company news, results, the UK economy London Stock Exchange: 8am–4.30pm weekdays familiar names; many smaller UK shares are illiquid
US shares and ETFs company news, US economy and interest rates usually 2.30pm–9pm weekdays the world's biggest, most liquid market; currency adds a layer
Forex (currencies, e.g. GBP/USD) interest rates, economic data, politics almost round the clock, Sunday night to Friday night very liquid; usually traded with high leverage
Crypto sentiment, news, regulation 24 hours, 7 days very volatile; most crypto isn't regulated

Things to think about:

  • Trading hours versus your life. If you're busy 9 to 5, UK shares are mostly open while you can't watch them. US shares open at 2.30pm UK time and run into the evening. Forex runs almost all week. Strategies that hold for days (swing trading) suit busy people better than ones needing you to watch every minute.
  • Volatility and your nerves. Crypto can move 10% in a day; a large UK company rarely does. More movement means more opportunity and more risk.
  • Costs. Compare spreads and fees in each market (module 7).
  • Regulation. Crypto is high risk and mostly unregulated: the FCA warns people to be prepared to lose all the money they put in. Crypto derivatives are banned for retail since 6 January 2021 (crypto exchange-traded notes on UK exchanges allowed for retail from 8 October 2025, with no FSCS cover; buying crypto itself is mostly unregulated).

Most people learn best by choosing one market and sticking with it, so they get to know how it behaves.

Your trading journal

A trading journal is the single most useful tool a trader has. Without one, you can't tell skill from luck, or spot your own patterns. For every paper trade, record:

Field Example
Date and time 3 March, 14:45
Market and instrument US shares: an S&P 500 ETF
Long or short Long
Why you're taking it (your setup) Bounced off support at 500 on high volume
Entry price 502.00
Stop-loss 497.00 (risk 5.00 per unit)
Target 512.00 (2R)
Position size worked out from 1% of the paper account (module 23)
Exit price and date 511.50, 6 March
Result in £ and in R +£190, +1.9R
Did you follow your rules? Yes / no, and what you'd do differently
How you felt Nervous on day 2 when it dipped

The R column (your result divided by the amount you risked) is what lets you compare trades fairly. Module 23 explains it, and module 31 reviews 20 trades using it.

Write the "why" before you enter. Afterwards, everyone has a convincing story.

Your first paper trade

Keep it simple. Your first few paper trades aren't about profit; they're about practising the routine:

  1. Choose your market and one or two instruments in it.
  2. Watch them for a week. Note their typical daily range and spread.
  3. Write a simple rule for entering and exiting (modules 15 and 19 will give you ideas).
  4. Before each trade, fill in the journal's "before" columns: setup, entry, stop, target, size.
  5. Enter, then don't move your stop further away.
  6. After exiting, fill in the rest, honestly.

Aim for consistency, not excitement. A boring, rules-based trade you journal properly teaches more than a lucky punt.

The common mistake

"Paper trading is pointless because it's not real money."

It's true it doesn't feel the same, and that matters. But the alternative, learning with real money, means paying for every beginner's mistake. Paper trading lets you test whether you can follow rules, whether your ideas have any edge, and how you handle losing runs, for free. Most people who skip it pay a lot more to learn the same lessons.

In real life

  • One market, one routine to start. Depth beats breadth.
  • Journal every trade, including the ones you'd rather forget.
  • Trade at real sizes in your paper account: a realistic balance and the 1% rule (module 23).
  • Give it time. Twenty trades is a first look; a hundred starts to tell you something.
  • Red flag: demo accounts with huge bonus balances, or providers pushing you to "go live" quickly. Their business is your trading volume.

Think about it

Why might someone trade much more aggressively on paper than they would with real money?

Losing pretend money doesn't trigger loss aversion (module 26) the way real losses do. That makes paper results look better than real ones would. Countering it means sizing paper trades realistically and taking each loss seriously in the journal.

Why is it useful to note how you felt in your journal?

Emotions drive many trading mistakes: revenge trades after a loss, overconfidence after wins, fear of pulling the trigger. Seeing patterns ("I break my rules after two losses") is the first step to fixing them, which module 31 covers.

Try it

  1. Set up a journal: a spreadsheet with the columns above is enough.
  2. Pick your market. Watch one instrument for a week and note its daily range and spread.
  3. Plan (don't take yet) your first paper trade, filling in the "before" columns.
