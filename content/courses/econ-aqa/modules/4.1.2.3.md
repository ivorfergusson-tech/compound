## What behavioural economics challenges

Traditional theory assumes people are rational and try to maximise their utility. **[[behavioural economics|Behavioural economics]]** uses ideas from psychology to study how people really decide. It shows that people often make predictable mistakes, which means the rational model doesn't always describe real behaviour.

## Bounded rationality and bounded self-control

**[[bounded rationality|Bounded rationality]]** means people try to make good decisions, but their rationality is limited by:

- the **information** they have,
- their brain's ability to **process** it, and
- the **time** available.

Nobody compares every phone contract in the UK. Most people look at a few and pick one that's good enough. This is sometimes called **satisficing**: settling for a satisfactory choice, not the best one.

**[[bounded self-control|Bounded self-control]]** means people have limited willpower. They may know what's best but not do it. They plan to revise, and then scroll on their phone. They join a gym in January, and stop going by March. It helps explain why people smoke, eat too much and save too little for retirement, even when they know the long-term cost.

## Biases in decision making

Because thinking is hard, people use shortcuts. These save time, but they can lead to predictable errors, called biases. You need to know four.

- **[[rules of thumb|Rules of thumb]]:** simple mental shortcuts, such as "always buy the mid-priced wine" or "spend a third of your income on rent". Quick, but not always right for the situation.
- **[[anchoring|Anchoring]]:** being pulled towards the first number you see, even if it means nothing. The "Was £120" label anchors you, so £60 feels cheap. The first price in a negotiation works the same way.
- **[[availability bias|Availability]]:** judging how likely something is by how easily examples come to mind. After news of a plane crash, people overrate the risk of flying. A friend's lottery win can make winning seem more likely than it is.
- **[[social norms|Social norms]]:** behaving the way others around you do, or the way that's seen as acceptable. People may buy what their friends buy or smoke because their friends do, not because it's what they would choose alone.

## Altruism and fairness

Traditional theory assumes people act in their own self-interest. Real people often don't.

- **[[altruism|Altruism]]** means caring about the welfare of others, even at a cost to yourself. People give to charity, donate blood and volunteer, for no personal gain.
- **[[perceptions of fairness|Perceptions of fairness]]** also shape choices. People may refuse a deal they see as unfair, even when refusing leaves them worse off. In experiments, people often reject a very small share of a sum of money, so that both sides get nothing. A purely self-interested person would take anything above zero.

It matters for firms and workers too. Shops that sharply raise prices during a shortage can face anger and lose customers, and staff who feel underpaid compared with colleagues may work less hard.

## How far does this overturn traditional theory?

Behavioural economics doesn't mean the old model is useless. Models simplify, and on average people do respond to prices and incentives. But it does show the model's limits, especially for big, complicated or long-term decisions like pensions, health and debt. It also gives governments new ways to influence behaviour, which is the next lesson.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain how bounded self-control and anchoring may cause consumers to make decisions that differ from the predictions of traditional economic theory. (9 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Traditional theory assumes that consumers are rational and maximise their utility by weighing up costs and benefits. Behavioural economics argues that decisions are often biased.

**Analysis (bounded self-control):** Bounded self-control means people have limited willpower, so they may fail to act in their own long-term interest. For example, a consumer may overspend on a credit card, knowing the debt will cost more in the future. Because the pleasure comes now and the cost comes later, they give too little weight to the future, so they consume more today and save less than a rational, utility-maximising consumer would.

**Analysis (anchoring):** Anchoring means people rely too much on the first figure they see. If a sofa is shown as "was £1,500, now £900", the first price becomes a reference point, so the consumer judges the deal against it and not against the value of the sofa to them. They may buy something they would otherwise have rejected, so the choice depends on how the price is presented, which rational theory says should not matter.

**Evaluation:** The size of these effects varies. For small repeat purchases, people learn from experience, so the rational model may work reasonably well. Biases are likely to matter most for rare, complicated decisions, such as buying a pension or a car.
:::

Why it scores: it starts from what the traditional model predicts, gives each idea its own chain ending in how the outcome differs, and evaluates by asking when the effect is strongest.
