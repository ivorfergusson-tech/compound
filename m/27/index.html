Recap

Module 23 gave you position sizing, the stop-loss and results measured in R. Modules 15 and 19 gave you ideas from charts and indicators. But an idea isn't a strategy, and a few winning trades aren't proof. This module shows how to turn ideas into exact rules, test them honestly on past data, and judge whether they have an edge, without fooling yourself.

Before you read

A strategy wins 40% of its trades. Each win makes 2R and each loss costs 1R.

The idea in one sentence

A strategy is a set of rules exact enough that a computer could follow them; you judge it by expectancy over many trades after costs, and the biggest danger in testing is tuning the rules until they fit the past perfectly and then fail in the future.

From idea to rules

"Buy when it looks strong" isn't a strategy. A strategy is a set of rules so exact that two people following it would take the same trades. It needs:

  1. The market and timeframe: e.g. a large US share ETF, daily candles.
  2. Entry: e.g. buy at the close when price closes above the 20-day high and above its 50-day moving average.
  3. Stop: e.g. just below the lowest low of the last 10 days.
  4. Exit / target: e.g. sell at 2R, or when price closes below the 10-day moving average.
  5. Position size: e.g. risk 1% of the account per trade (module 23).
  6. Filters: e.g. no new trades in the week of company results.

If any part needs a judgement call, it can't be tested properly, and you won't know whether results came from the rules or from your mood that day.

Win rate and expectancy

The win rate is the share of trades that make money. On its own, it tells you almost nothing: a strategy that wins 90% of the time but loses 20R on the other 10% is a disaster.

Expectancy combines win rate and the size of wins and losses into the average result per trade:

expectancy = (win rate × average win) − (loss rate × average loss)

Back to the question at the start: 0.4 × 2R − 0.6 × 1R = +0.2R per trade. Over 100 trades that's about +20R, or roughly +20% of the account if you risk 1% per trade (a bit less after compounding and costs). It loses more often than it wins, and still makes money.

Costs come straight off expectancy. If spreads and fees cost 0.1R per trade, expectancy halves to +0.1R. A strategy with +0.05R expectancy before costs is probably a loser after them.

Losing streaks are normal

Even good strategies have long losing runs. With a 40% win rate, the chance of at least 6 losses in a row somewhere in 100 trades is about 87%, and the chance of 8 in a row is about 49%. That's not the strategy breaking; it's arithmetic.

This is why the 1% rule matters (module 23), and why you shouldn't abandon a tested strategy after a few losses, or trust a new one after a few wins.

Backtesting

A backtest applies your exact rules to past price data to see how they would have done. Done well, it tells you:

  • the number of trades, win rate, average win and loss, and expectancy in R,
  • the worst losing run and the biggest drawdown,
  • whether results depend on one lucky period.

How to do it honestly, even by hand on a chart:

  1. Write the rules first, before looking at the results.
  2. Use plenty of data: at least 30, ideally 100+ trades, across different market conditions (rising, falling, sideways).
  3. Take every signal the rules give, including the ugly ones. Skipping trades you "wouldn't really have taken" is cheating.
  4. Include costs: spreads, commission, slippage.
  5. Hide some data: test on, say, 2015–2021, and keep 2022–2025 untouched. Then run the final rules once on the unseen part. This is out-of-sample testing.

Then forward test on paper, live, for weeks or months. Real time is the toughest test.

Overfitting: the biggest trap

Overfitting is tweaking rules until they fit the past perfectly. With enough tweaks, any random data can be made to look like a goldmine: a 17-day moving average, but only on Tuesdays, unless RSI is above 63. The rules end up describing past noise, not a real pattern, and they fall apart on new data.

Warning signs:

  • Lots of rules and precise numbers (17 days, 63 RSI) that were chosen because they tested best.
  • Results that collapse when you nudge a setting slightly (a 16- or 18-day average does terribly).
  • Great in-sample, poor out-of-sample.
  • Very few trades. Ten trades can look amazing by luck.

Simple rules with a reason behind them (why should this work?) are more likely to hold up than complex rules found by searching.

Other biases in testing

  • Survivorship bias: testing only on companies that exist today leaves out the ones that went bust, flattering results.
  • Look-ahead bias: using information you couldn't have had at the time, like a day's close to decide a trade at that day's open.
  • Cherry-picking: showing only the market or period where it worked.

The common mistake

"My backtest made 300% a year, so I've found a goldmine."

Spectacular backtests are almost always a sign of overfitting, missing costs, look-ahead bias or too few trades. Real edges, when they exist, tend to be modest: a small positive expectancy, repeated many times, with tight risk control. If a result looks too good to be true, check it harder, not less.

In real life

  • Write rules a stranger could follow.
  • Judge by expectancy in R after costs, over at least 30–100 trades.
  • Expect losing runs and plan your risk for them.
  • Test on data you haven't seen, then forward test on paper.
  • Red flag: strategy sellers showing backtests without the full trade list, costs, or out-of-sample results.

Think about it

Why might a strategy that worked for years suddenly stop working?

Markets change: other traders discover the same pattern and trade it away, conditions shift from trending to ranging, or costs and rules change. Or it was overfitted luck all along. That's why traders keep monitoring live results against the backtest.

Why is "why should this work?" such an important question?

A rule with a sensible reason (for example, trends persist because news spreads slowly and people under-react) is more likely to keep working than a pattern found by trying thousands of combinations. Reason guards against overfitting.

Try it

  1. Write one strategy for your market, with all six parts above, in your journal.
  2. Scroll back through a year of daily charts and record every signal it would have given, with the result in R. Calculate win rate and expectancy with the calculator.
  3. Was the worst losing run longer than you'd have stuck with?
