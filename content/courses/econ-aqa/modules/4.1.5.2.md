Traditional theory assumes firms maximise profit by producing where MC = MR, but managers who don't own the firm may pursue other goals, so real firms have a range of objectives.

## Profit maximisation

The models in this section assume firms aim for the highest possible profit. The rule is:

**Profit is maximised where marginal cost = marginal revenue (MC = MR).**

Here's why:

- If **MR > MC**, one more unit adds more to revenue than to cost, so profit rises. Make more.
- If **MC > MR**, the last unit cost more than it earned, so profit falls. Make less.
- At **MC = MR**, there's no change that would add to profit.

## Divorce of ownership from control

In a large public company, **shareholders own** the firm, while **managers control** it. This is the **[[divorce of ownership from control]]**.

Shareholders usually want high profits and dividends. Managers may want a higher salary, a bigger firm, more status or an easier life. Shareholders can't watch everything, because the managers know more about the business, so this is a case of asymmetric information.

The consequences for firms can include:

- **Objectives:** managers may pursue growth or sales, not the highest profit.
- **Conduct:** more spending on offices, staff or takeovers that build an empire.
- **Performance:** higher costs and lower profits than the owners would choose.

Shareholders try to line up the interests of managers through share options, profit-related bonuses and the threat of being sacked or taken over.

## Other objectives

Firms may aim for:

- **Survival:** especially in a new firm or a recession.
- **Growth:** a bigger firm can gain economies of scale and more security.
- **Quality:** building a reputation that supports higher prices.
- **[[sales revenue maximisation|Maximising sales revenue]]:** making as much revenue as possible, which happens where **MR = 0**.
- **Increasing market share:** gaining sales from rivals, even at lower profit for now.

Some of these can be routes to long-run profit. A firm may cut prices today to win customers it can profit from later.

## Satisficing

**[[satisficing|Satisficing]]** means aiming for a result that's good enough, not the best possible. A firm might aim for a level of profit that keeps shareholders content, and then pursue other goals.

It happens because:

- Managers and owners want different things.
- Firms have bounded rationality: they lack the information to find the exact MC = MR output.
- Groups within the firm, such as workers, managers and shareholders, have to compromise.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain how the divorce of ownership from control may affect the objectives of a firm. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** The divorce of ownership from control means that the shareholders who own a firm are not the managers who run it.

**Application:** In a large company, thousands of shareholders may each own a small share, and few of them can check the decisions of the board.

**Analysis:** Shareholders want high profits and dividends, while managers may gain more from growth, because salaries and status tend to rise with the size of the firm. Since managers know more than shareholders, they can pursue these goals. The firm may then aim for sales growth, or satisfice by making just enough profit to keep shareholders content.
:::

Why it scores: it shows why the two groups differ, why managers can get their way, and what objective results.
