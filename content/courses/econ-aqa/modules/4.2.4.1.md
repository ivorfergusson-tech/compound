## What money does

**[[money|Money]]** has four main functions:

- **Medium of exchange:** accepted in payment.
- **Store of value:** can be kept and used later.
- **Unit of account:** a way to measure and compare values.
- **Standard of deferred payment:** a way to settle debts in the future.

To do these jobs, money needs certain characteristics. It should be widely accepted, hard to forge, durable, divisible, portable and limited in supply.

## The money supply

The **[[money supply|money supply]]** is the total amount of money in an economy.

- **[[narrow money|Narrow money]]** is money that can be spent straight away: notes, coins and balances that banks hold at the central bank.
- **[[broad money|Broad money]]** adds bank and building society deposits. It's far larger, since most money today is held in bank accounts.

## Three kinds of market

- **The money market** is for short-term borrowing and lending, usually for less than a year.
- **The capital market** is for long-term finance, through shares and bonds.
- **The foreign exchange market** is where currencies are traded.

## The role of financial markets

Financial markets:

- Move funds from savers to borrowers.
- Help firms and governments raise money for investment.
- Allow risk to be shared.
- Help trade, by providing ways to pay and to exchange currencies.

Firms raise finance in three main ways: **issuing shares**, **issuing corporate bonds** and **borrowing from a bank**.

## Debt and equity

- **[[debt|Debt]]** is money that's borrowed and must be repaid, usually with interest. Bank loans and bonds are debt.
- **[[equity shares|Equity]]** here means ownership. Shareholders own part of the firm, and they have a right to a share of the profit. The firm doesn't have to repay them.

Debt is less risky for the lender, as it's paid before shareholders if the firm fails. Equity holders can gain more if the firm does well.

## Government bonds

A **[[bond|bond]]** is a loan that can be bought and sold. UK government bonds are called **gilts**.

- **Face value:** the amount repaid at the end, say £100.
- **[[coupon]]:** the fixed interest paid each year, say £5.
- **[[maturity]]:** the date when the loan is repaid.

**Yield = (coupon ÷ market price) × 100**

## Why bond prices and interest rates move in opposite ways

The coupon is fixed. Only the market price changes.

If a bond pays £5 a year and costs £100, the yield is 5%. If market interest rates rise to 6%, nobody will pay £100 for a 5% return. The bond's price has to fall until its yield matches: about £83.

So there's an **inverse relationship**:

- **Interest rates rise:** bond prices fall.
- **Interest rates fall:** bond prices rise.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain why there is an inverse relationship between market interest rates and bond prices. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** A bond pays a fixed coupon. Its yield is the coupon as a percentage of its market price.

**Application:** Suppose a gilt with a face value of £100 pays £3 a year, and market interest rates rise from 3% to 4%.

**Analysis:** New savers can now get 4% elsewhere, so a bond paying a 3% return on £100 is less attractive. Holders sell it, which pushes its price down. This goes on until the fixed £3 coupon gives a yield of about 4%, which is at a price of about £75. So a rise in interest rates lowers bond prices, and a fall raises them.
:::

Why it scores: it starts from the fixed coupon, and shows the price adjusting with numbers.
