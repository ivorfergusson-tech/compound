Long answers are won on depth and judgement: a few well-developed points, weighed against each other, beat a long list, and the 25-marker lives or dies on evaluation.

## How the levels work

Long answers are marked by level. The examiner decides which level fits best, then sets the mark within it.

| Level | 25-mark question | 15-mark question |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 21 to 25 | |
| 4 | 16 to 20 | |
| 3 | 11 to 15 | 11 to 15 |
| 2 | 6 to 10 | 6 to 10 |
| 1 | 1 to 5 | 1 to 5 |

At the top of the 25-mark grid, examiners want sound, focused analysis **and** well-supported evaluation. At the bottom, they see knowledge with little analysis and no real judgement. So however good your knowledge, you can't reach the top without evaluation.

## Build chains of reasoning

A [[chain of reasoning]] links a cause to an effect, one step at a time. Use "so", "because" and "which means".

**Weak:** Higher interest rates reduce inflation.

**Strong:** A rise in Bank Rate raises the cost of borrowing, **so** households with variable-rate mortgages have less to spend. Consumption falls, **so** AD shifts left. Firms face weaker demand, **which means** they have less power to raise prices, **so** inflation falls.

Each step should follow from the last. Don't skip the middle.

## The 15-mark question

This is usually an **analyse** or **explain** question, with no need for a final judgement. It rewards depth and accuracy.

- Plan **two or three** points.
- Develop each into a full chain.
- Include a diagram if it helps.

## The 25-mark question

Plan for two or three minutes first. A simple plan for 37 minutes:

1. **Introduction:** define the key terms, and say what the question turns on.
2. **Two or three arguments for:** each a full chain, using the extract or real examples.
3. **Two or three arguments against:** the same.
4. **Evaluation as you go:** weigh each point when you make it.
5. **Conclusion:** a clear, reasoned judgement.

Include a relevant diagram, and use **both** the extract and your own knowledge if the question says so.

## How to evaluate

Saying "on the other hand" isn't evaluation. That's just another argument. [[evaluation|Evaluation]] means weighing. Ask yourself:

- **How big is the effect?** Is it large or small here?
- **Time:** short run or long run? How long is the lag?
- **It depends on what?** Elasticity, spare capacity, the size of the change or the state of the economy.
- **Who gains and who loses?** Is it fair?
- **How reliable is the evidence?** Is this data recent, or a one-off?
- **Compared with what?** Is another policy better? What's the opportunity cost?
- **Unintended consequences:** could it cause government failure?

Good evaluation sounds like this: "This effect is **likely to be small, because** demand for petrol is inelastic, **so** most of the tax is passed on and quantity hardly falls. **It would matter more if** good public transport existed."

## The conclusion

Don't just repeat your points. A strong conclusion:

1. **Answers the question directly:** yes, no or to what extent.
2. **Gives the deciding factor:** the one thing that matters most.
3. **Says what it depends on:** when would your answer change?

:::callout exam
**Sample conclusion (ours, not AQA's):** Overall, a sugar tax is likely to reduce consumption, but by less than its supporters hope, because demand from heavy consumers is fairly inelastic. The deciding factor is whether firms cut the sugar in their products, as this changes what everybody buys and not only what's in the shop. So it's a useful part of a wider policy, but it's unlikely to be enough on its own.
:::

## Common ways to lose marks

- Listing many points, with no development.
- One-sided answers, with no judgement.
- Never mentioning the extract when the question asks for it.
- Running out of time and writing no conclusion.
- Writing generally about the topic, not the question.
