Recap

You've now covered the whole 💷 Money strand: pay and tax (module 5), budgets and savings (module 9), credit and debt (module 13), ISAs (module 17), pensions (module 21) and pay, student loans and living costs (module 25). This capstone puts them into one plan: yours.

It's different from the other modules. Less reading, more doing. By the end you'll have a written plan for the next five to ten years that you can come back to and update.

Before you read

At the start of the course you answered this question: you're 18 and someone gives you £1,000; you want as much money as possible by 30; what would you do with it?

The idea in one sentence

A financial plan is just a set of priorities in the right order: protect yourself first, stop expensive debt, grab free money, then let time and compounding do the heavy lifting.

The money priority ladder

Most guidance in the UK, from MoneyHelper to independent planners, puts money priorities in roughly this order. Each rung protects the ones above it. It's a guide, not a law, and your situation might reorder it, but it's a sound default.

  1. Know your numbers. Your take-home pay and where it goes (modules 5 and 9).
  2. A starter emergency fund. Even £500 to £1,000 in easy-access savings, so a surprise doesn't become debt.
  3. Never miss minimum payments on any debt, and protect your credit file (module 13).
  4. Take free money. Stay in your workplace pension, at least up to whatever your employer matches (module 21).
  5. Clear expensive debt: credit cards, overdrafts, payday loans, starting with the highest APR.
  6. Build the full emergency fund: three to six months of essential costs (module 9).
  7. Save for goals in the next five years in cash: a car, a course, a deposit (savings accounts, Cash ISA, Lifetime ISA for a first home; module 17).
  8. Invest for the long term (five years or more) in a Stocks and Shares ISA and extra pension contributions (modules 14 and 30).

Notice that investing comes last. Not because it's unimportant, but because it works best on money you won't need to touch for years. Investing while carrying 30% card debt is like filling a bath with the plug out.

Step 3: protection and debt

Write down every debt: card, overdraft, BNPL, car finance, loans (a student loan is different; see module 25). Order them by APR, highest first. If you have none, write how you'll keep it that way: a card paid in full by direct debit, BNPL counted in the budget.

Step 6: rules for future you

Plans fail at the moments you're not thinking about them: a pay rise, a windfall, a bad month. Write rules now so the decision is already made. Examples:

  • "When I get a pay rise, half of it goes to savings."
  • "Any windfall (birthday money, bonus, tax refund): half to goals, half to enjoy."
  • "I don't use BNPL for anything under £100."
  • "If my card isn't paid off in full, I stop using it until it is."
  • "I review this plan every April when the new tax year starts."

The common mistake

"I'll make a plan when I earn more."

The habits matter most when the amounts are small, because that's when they're cheap to learn. Someone who saves £50 a month on £16,000 usually keeps doing it on £40,000. Someone who waits often finds spending has grown to match their new pay. Start with whatever you can, even if it feels too small to matter.

Keeping it alive

A plan you never look at isn't a plan. Put a date in your calendar each year, around April when the new tax year brings new allowances. Check:

  • Has your pay changed? Update the take-home and budget.
  • Have tax figures, ISA rules or pension rules changed? (This course shows the latest figures, with their dates.)
  • Are your savings rates still competitive?
  • Have your goals moved?

Your answers on this page are saved, and if you have an account they're kept with it. You can come back and edit them any time.

In real life

  • Write it down. A plan in your head changes with your mood.
  • Automate it. Standing orders on payday for savings; direct debits for bills and the full card balance.
  • One change at a time. If the whole plan feels like a lot, pick the lowest unfinished rung and do just that this month.
  • Get free help if you're stuck: MoneyHelper offers free, impartial guidance by phone and online.
  • Red flag: anyone selling a "complete financial plan" that mainly involves buying their product.

Think about it

Your old answer and your new answer about the £1,000: what changed?

Common changes: people who said "invest it all" now mention an emergency fund or clearing debt first; people who said "keep it in the bank" now think about inflation and a Stocks and Shares ISA for money they won't need for 12 years. Whatever changed, that's what the course taught you.

When might someone sensibly break the priority ladder's order?

A few examples: someone about to buy a first home might build a Lifetime ISA before a full six-month emergency fund, because the bonus is time-limited; someone with very secure income might keep a smaller emergency fund. The ladder is a default. Breaking it should be a deliberate choice with a reason, not an accident.
