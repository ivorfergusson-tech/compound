## What an index number is

An **[[index number]]** shows how a value has changed compared with a **base year**, which is set at 100.

**Index = (value this year ÷ value in the base year) × 100**

If a price was £50 in the base year and is £60 now, the index is 60 ÷ 50 × 100 = **120**. So the price is 20% higher than in the base year.

An index has no units. That's what makes it useful: it lets you compare changes in things measured in different ways, such as prices, wages and output.

## Percentage changes from an index

Be careful when working between two years that aren't the base year.

**% change = (new index − old index) ÷ old index × 100**

If an index goes from 120 to 126, it has risen by 6 **points**, but by 6 ÷ 120 × 100 = **5%**. It's a common exam mistake to give the points as the percentage.

## Weights

A simple average would treat all items alike. But households spend far more on some things than others, so each item is given a **weight** that shows its share of spending.

**Weighted index = sum of (weight × price index of the item) ÷ sum of the weights**

## The CPI and RPI

The CPI and RPI are weighted price indices. You don't need the technical detail, only the main features:

- **The basket of goods and services:** a list of items, chosen to reflect what households buy. It's updated each year as spending patterns change.
- **The average family:** weights come from surveys of how households spend their money.
- **Prices:** collected regularly from many shops and websites.

So an index measures the change in the cost of a typical basket, for a typical household. **Few real households are typical.** A pensioner spends more on heating and less on commuting, so their personal rate of inflation may differ.

Other limits are that new products and better quality are hard to allow for, and people switch away from items that get dearer.

## Other uses of index numbers

Index numbers aren't only for prices. They can show changes in wages, output, share prices or house prices. To see the change in **real wages**, compare a wage index with a price index:

**Real wage index = (money wage index ÷ price index) × 100**

If a wage index is 110 and a price index is 105, the real wage index is about 104.8, so real wages have risen by about 4.8%.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain why weights are used when calculating a price index. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** A price index measures the average change in prices, compared with a base year. A weight shows the share of household spending on an item.

**Application:** Households might spend 10% of their budget on food, and only 1% on books.

**Analysis:** Without weights, a 10% rise in book prices would count as much as a 10% rise in food prices, even though the food rise costs households ten times as much. Weighting each price change by its share of spending means that the index reflects the effect on the cost of living. So it gives a more accurate measure of inflation for a typical household.
:::

Why it scores: it defines both terms and shows with numbers why an unweighted index would mislead.
