## Production and productivity

**[[production|Production]]** converts inputs, the services of factors of production such as capital and labour, into final output.

**[[productivity|Productivity]]** measures how much output is made from a given input. The most common measure is **[[labour productivity]]**: output per worker, or output per hour worked.

**Labour productivity = total output ÷ number of workers (or hours worked)**

Don't confuse production and productivity. A factory can raise **production** by hiring more workers. **Productivity** only rises if each worker makes more.

Why it matters: higher productivity lowers the cost per unit, helps firms compete, and over time allows higher wages and living standards.

Productivity rises with better training, better machines, better technology, better management, and stronger motivation.

## Specialisation and division of labour

**[[specialisation|Specialisation]]** means people, firms, regions or countries concentrating on what they do best, and producing only some goods.

**[[division of labour|Division of labour]]** is specialisation within a firm: production is split into small tasks, and each worker does one of them. Adam Smith made the idea famous in 1776 with his example of a pin factory.

**Benefits:**

- Workers get faster and more skilled through repetition.
- People can do the job that suits their abilities.
- No time is wasted moving between tasks.
- Tasks become simple enough to use machines, which raises productivity further.
- Output rises, and cost per unit falls.

**Drawbacks (useful for evaluation):**

- Repetitive work can be boring, so motivation and quality can fall.
- Workers' skills are narrow, so they're vulnerable if the task disappears.
- If one stage stops, the whole line stops.

## Why specialisation needs exchange

If you only make one thing, you have to swap it for everything else you need. So specialisation needs an efficient way of **exchanging** goods and services.

Without money, you'd need **barter**: swapping goods directly. Barter needs a "double coincidence of wants": a baker who wants shoes has to find a shoemaker who wants bread.

**Money as a [[medium of exchange]]** solves this. You sell your labour for money, and use money to buy anything. It's what makes a specialised economy possible.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain how the division of labour can reduce a firm's average costs of production. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Division of labour means splitting production into separate tasks, with each worker specialising in one.

**Application:** In a car plant, one worker might only fit doors, and another only fit seats.

**Analysis:** Repeating one task makes workers faster and more skilled, and no time is lost switching between jobs. So output per worker rises. With a higher output from the same wage bill, labour cost per unit falls, which lowers the firm's average cost.
:::

Why it scores: it defines the term, gives an example, and follows the chain from skill to productivity to cost per unit.
