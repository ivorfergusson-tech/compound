A band sells 5,000 concert tickets at £30 and they sell out in a minute. Thousands of fans miss out.

:::callout idea
Price adjusts until the quantity buyers want equals the quantity sellers offer: excess demand pushes the price up, excess supply pushes it down, and equilibrium is where there's no pressure to change.
:::

## Equilibrium

**[[equilibrium]]** is a state of rest. In a market, it's where **quantity demanded equals quantity supplied**, at the point where the demand and supply curves cross. The price there is the **equilibrium price**, or market-clearing price.

At equilibrium, there's no shortage and no surplus, so nothing pushes the price up or down.

**[[disequilibrium|Disequilibrium]]** is any other situation: quantity demanded and quantity supplied are not equal, so there's pressure for the price to change.

## Excess demand and excess supply

Move the price away from £60.

:::chart disequilibrium
:::

**[[excess supply|Excess supply]]** happens when the price is **above** equilibrium. Firms want to sell more than buyers want to buy. Unsold stock builds up, so firms cut prices. As the price falls, quantity demanded extends and quantity supplied contracts, until the market clears.

**[[excess demand|Excess demand]]** happens when the price is **below** equilibrium. Buyers want more than firms offer, and shortages and queues appear. Firms see they can charge more, and buyers bid up the price. As the price rises, quantity demanded contracts and quantity supplied extends, until the market clears.

Those £30 concert tickets are an example of excess demand. Resale prices rise because fans will pay more than £30.

## Using the diagram

To analyse any change, work through four steps:

1. Which curve moves, **demand or supply**? (Usually just one.)
2. Which **direction**, and why?
3. At the old price, is there now **excess demand or excess supply**?
4. How does the price adjust, and what's the **new equilibrium price and quantity**?

:::chart market {"d":0,"s":0}
:::

The four basic results:

| Change | Price | Quantity |
|---|---|---|
| Demand shifts right | Rises | Rises |
| Demand shifts left | Falls | Falls |
| Supply shifts right | Falls | Rises |
| Supply shifts left | Rises | Falls |

## Real-world markets and the model's assumptions

The model applies to all sorts of markets: oil, housing, football shirts, foreign currency. How much price moves depends on elasticity, as you saw in the last two lessons.

The basic model makes assumptions you should be aware of:

- Lots of **buyers and sellers**, none big enough to set the price.
- Products are much the **same**.
- Buyers and sellers have **good information**.
- Prices can **move freely** and quickly, and the government doesn't interfere.
- Other things are **held constant** (ceteris paribus).

Real markets often break these assumptions. Rent controls stop prices moving, and big firms can influence prices. Later modules look at what happens then.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** With the help of a diagram, explain why a successful advertising campaign for a product would be likely to raise its equilibrium price. (9 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Equilibrium price is the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. Demand is the quantity consumers are willing and able to buy at each price.

**Application:** On the diagram, the market begins at equilibrium at P1 and Q1, where D1 crosses S.

**Analysis:** A successful campaign makes the product more attractive, so more is demanded at every price and the demand curve shifts right to D2. At the original price P1, there is now excess demand, as quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied. Shortages encourage firms to raise prices, and consumers compete for limited stock. As the price rises, quantity supplied extends, because the higher price offers more profit, and quantity demanded contracts. This continues until a new equilibrium is reached at P2 and Q2, with a higher price and quantity.

**Evaluation:** The size of the rise depends on the price elasticity of supply. If firms have spare capacity, supply is elastic and most of the effect is on quantity. If supply is inelastic, the price rises by more. The effect may also fade if the advertising doesn't change tastes for long, or if rivals respond with campaigns of their own.
:::

Why it scores: it uses the four steps, moves the curve for a stated reason, explains the process of adjustment (not just the result), and evaluates with elasticity.
