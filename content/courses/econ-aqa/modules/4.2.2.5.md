Short-run aggregate supply depends on the price level and on firms' costs: a rise in the price level brings more output, and a change in costs shifts the whole curve.

## The shape of SRAS

**Short-run aggregate supply** is the total output firms will supply at each price level, in a period when some costs are fixed.

The two main determinants are the **price level** and **production costs**.

The curve slopes upwards. If the price level rises, while wages and other costs are slow to change, then selling is more profitable, so firms supply more. A change in the price level is a **movement along** the SRAS curve.

## What shifts SRAS

A change in costs shifts the whole curve.

**Money wage rates.** Wages are the biggest cost for many firms. A general rise in wages, with no rise in productivity, shifts SRAS left.

**Raw material prices.** Oil, gas, metals and food are used across the economy. In the UK, a lot of these are imported, so a weaker pound raises their price too.

**Business taxation.** Higher taxes on firms add to costs. A rise in employer National Insurance is an example. Lower ones shift SRAS right.

**Productivity.** If workers make more per hour, the cost of each unit falls, so SRAS shifts right.

Remember: a leftward shift of SRAS is the same as an upward shift on the diagram, since firms need a higher price to supply the same output.

## SRAS and LRAS

A fall in SRAS doesn't by itself mean that the economy has lost capacity. A rise in oil prices makes production dearer, but the workers and factories are still there. LRAS is about the productive potential of the economy, while SRAS is about the costs of using it.

Some events affect both. A rise in productivity lowers unit costs in the short run, and it adds to capacity in the long run.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain how a rise in the price of imported raw materials would affect the UK's short-run aggregate supply. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** SRAS depends on production costs. Raw materials are a cost for many firms.

**Application:** UK firms rely on imported gas and metals, so a rise in world prices or a fall in the pound raises their costs.

**Analysis:** Each unit now costs more to make, so at any given price level, firms are willing to supply less. SRAS shifts to the left. On an AD/AS diagram, the price level rises and real output falls. This is cost-push inflation, and it would add to the pressure on firms' profits as well.
:::

Why it scores: it links the cost to the shift, and then reads the result off the diagram.
