The government raises the minimum wage by a large amount.

:::callout idea
A national minimum wage is a legal wage floor: it lifts the pay of the lowest paid, and whether it costs jobs depends on how high it's set and on how competitive the labour market is.
:::

## What it is

A **[[national minimum wage]]** is the lowest hourly rate that employers can legally pay. In the UK, the rates from April 2026 are:

| Group | Hourly rate |
|---|---|
| Aged 21 and over (the National Living Wage) | £12.71 |
| Aged 18 to 20 | £10.85 |
| Under 18 | £8.00 |
| Apprentices | £8.00 |

*Source: GOV.UK. The rates change every April. From April 2027, the National Living Wage is due to rise to £13.21, so check the latest figures.*

## Effects on labour markets

**In a competitive labour market,** a minimum wage above equilibrium works like a union wage. Firms hire fewer workers, more people want to work, and there's unemployment. This is the textbook prediction.

**In a monopsony,** a minimum wage can raise both pay and employment, as you saw with unions, up to the competitive wage.

**In practice,** the Low Pay Commission, which advises on the rates, has found no robust evidence that recent rises have had a significant negative effect on total employment. Some employers do report hiring less, some studies find effects in particular sectors, and the result depends on how high the rate is set. Firms may also respond in ways the simple diagram misses:

- Raising prices.
- Accepting lower profits.
- Cutting hours, training or other benefits.
- Finding ways to raise productivity.
- Buying machines to replace workers.

## Advantages

- **Higher pay for the lowest paid,** which reduces poverty among workers.
- **Less exploitation** by employers with monopsony power.
- **More incentive to work,** since work pays more than benefits.
- **Higher productivity:** better-paid workers may be more motivated and stay longer, which saves firms recruitment costs.
- **Less spending on in-work benefits** by the government, and more tax revenue.
- **Narrower pay gaps,** including between men and women, since women are more likely to be low paid.

## Disadvantages

- **Possible job losses,** especially for young and low-skilled workers.
- **Higher costs for firms,** particularly small ones, which can lead to higher prices.
- **Poor targeting:** many of the poorest households have nobody in work, so they don't gain.
- **Regional effects:** a single rate may bite harder where wages are lower.
- **Differentials:** those paid just above the minimum may ask for a rise too, adding to costs.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain why a national minimum wage may not cause unemployment. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** A national minimum wage is a legal floor on the hourly rate of pay.

**Application:** UK employers must pay workers aged 21 and over at least the National Living Wage.

**Analysis:** If an employer has monopsony power, it was holding wages and employment down. A minimum wage means it no longer has to raise pay for all its workers to hire one more, so its marginal cost of labour is flat and it can hire more, up to the competitive wage. Also, higher pay can raise motivation and cut staff turnover. This raises productivity and MRP, so the demand for labour shifts right, which offsets the higher wage.
:::

Why it scores: it gives two distinct reasons and takes each one through to employment.
