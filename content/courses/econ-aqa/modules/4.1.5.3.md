You grow wheat, one farmer among thousands. All the wheat is the same, and buyers can see every price.

:::callout idea
In perfect competition, firms take the market price, and free entry and exit push profits to normal in the long run, which makes the model a yardstick for judging real markets.
:::

## The assumptions

[[perfect competition|Perfect competition]] is a model with four key assumptions. Each one shapes behaviour:

- **Large numbers of producers:** no single firm can change the market price.
- **Identical products:** buyers have no reason to pay more to one firm.
- **Freedom of entry and exit:** firms can join or leave, so profits don't last.
- **Perfect knowledge:** everyone knows all prices and methods, so nobody can overcharge or keep a secret advantage.

## Firms are price takers

The price is set by market demand and supply. Each firm is a **[[price taker]]**: it accepts the market price.

Charge more, and every customer goes elsewhere. Charge less, and the firm loses money it needn't lose, since it can already sell all it wants at the market price.

So each firm's demand curve is **horizontal** at the market price. It's perfectly elastic, and every extra unit brings in the same revenue:

**P = AR = MR**

## The short run

The firm produces where **MC = MR**. Its profit depends on where the price sits against ATC at that output:

- **Price above ATC:** abnormal profit.
- **Price equal to ATC:** normal profit.
- **Price below ATC:** a loss.

The profit or loss per unit is the gap between price and ATC. Multiply it by output and you have the box on the diagram.

## The long run

Short-run abnormal profit can't last.

**If firms make abnormal profit:**

1. New firms enter, since entry is free and everyone knows about the profit.
2. Market supply shifts right.
3. The market price falls.
4. This stops when firms make normal profit.

**If firms make losses,** the same thing runs in reverse: firms leave, supply shifts left and the price rises until the firms that remain make normal profit.

In **long-run equilibrium**, P = MC = AC at the lowest point of ATC. Set the slider above to £50 to see it.

## Efficiency, and a yardstick

In long-run equilibrium, a perfectly competitive firm is:

- **Productively efficient:** it produces at the lowest point of ATC.
- **Allocatively efficient:** P = MC, so the value consumers put on the last unit equals the cost of making it.

Given certain assumptions, such as no externalities, perfect competition results in an efficient allocation of resources. That's why economists use it as a **yardstick**: the further a market is from it, the more resources may be misallocated.

Be critical of that claim:

- The assumptions are rarely met, and externalities are common.
- Identical products mean less choice for consumers.
- Small firms making normal profit have little to spend on research, so there may be less innovation.
- Small firms can't gain economies of scale. A larger firm may have lower costs than the "efficient" small firm.
- Perfect knowledge removes the reward for new ideas, since any idea could be copied at once.

## Worked exam answer

**Question:** Explain why firms in perfect competition make only normal profit in the long run. (4 marks)

:::callout exam
**Knowledge:** Normal profit is the minimum profit needed to keep a firm in the industry. Perfect competition assumes freedom of entry and exit and perfect knowledge.

**Application:** If the market price is above average total cost at the output where MC = MR, the firms in the market make abnormal profit.

**Analysis:** Perfect knowledge means outsiders see this profit, and free entry lets them join. Market supply shifts right, so the market price falls. Each firm takes this lower price, and entry continues until price equals the lowest ATC, where only normal profit is left and there's no reason for more firms to join.
:::

Why it scores: it links the assumptions to a clear chain, right through to the end point.
