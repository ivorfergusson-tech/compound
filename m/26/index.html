Behavioural biases

Recap

Module 10 showed that the right level of risk depends on how you'll actually behave in a crash. Module 14 showed that a cheap index fund beats most professionals. So why don't more people simply buy one and leave it? Because the hardest part of investing isn't maths. It's us. This module covers the mental shortcuts that lead clever people into expensive mistakes, and simple ways to guard against them.

Before you read

Choose one:

  • A: you're given £500 for certain.
  • B: a coin flip: heads you get £1,000, tails you get nothing.

Now a second choice:

  • C: you lose £500 for certain.
  • D: a coin flip: heads you lose £1,000, tails you lose nothing.
The idea in one sentence

Our brains evolved for quick decisions, not for investing, so we feel losses too sharply, trust ourselves too much and follow the crowd; good investors don't remove these biases, they build rules that stop the biases making the decisions.

Why behaviour matters so much

The gap between what funds earn and what their investors actually earn has a name: the behaviour gap. Investors tend to buy after prices have risen and sell after they've fallen, so they miss part of the return. Morningstar's research on US funds found the gap was about 1.2 percentage points a year (US funds, 10 years to end-2024 (funds 8.2% a year, investors 7.0%); the 2026 study found 9.9% vs 8.7%).

Over a working life, that kind of gap can cost tens of thousands of pounds, without any difference in the funds themselves. The cause is predictable human behaviour.

Loss aversion

Back to the question at the start. Most people choose A (the certain £500) and D (the gamble to avoid a certain loss). Both pairs have the same average outcome, yet we play safe with gains and gamble to avoid losses.

That's loss aversion: losses hurt more than equal gains feel good. Psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, whose research on this won a Nobel prize, estimated that a loss feels roughly twice as strong as a gain of the same size.

How it costs investors:

  • Selling winners too early to lock in the good feeling, and holding losers too long hoping to "get back to even".
  • Panic selling in a crash, because the pain of watching losses grow feels unbearable.
  • Keeping everything in cash for fear of any loss, while inflation quietly shrinks it (module 1).

The guard: decide in advance what you'll do in a fall (usually: nothing, or keep buying monthly). Check your investments less often: the more often you look, the more often you see losses.

Overconfidence

Most people think they're above-average drivers, which can't be true for most of us. Investors are the same. overconfidence shows up as:

  • believing you can pick winning shares or time the market,
  • trading too often (each trade has costs, and frequent traders tend to do worse),
  • putting too much in one company you "know",
  • remembering your wins and explaining away your losses.

Module 14 showed that even professionals rarely beat the market after costs. A beginner with an app is very unlikely to do better.

The guard: keep a written record of every investment decision and why you made it (traders call it a journal, module 11). Check honestly later. Default to a diversified index fund for the core of your money.

Herding

Humans copy the crowd. If everyone around you is buying something, it feels safe; if everyone's selling, it feels dangerous. That's herding.

It's behind bubbles: prices rise, people pile in because prices are rising, which pushes prices higher, until it breaks. History is full of them, from tulip bulbs in the 1600s to the dot-com crash around 2000 and, more recently, crazes in particular shares and cryptocurrencies spread on social media.

Herding also works in reverse: crowds sell in a panic, pushing prices below sensible values.

The guard: be wary of anything everyone is talking about, especially if the main reason to buy is that the price has gone up. "Fear of missing out" is a feeling, not a reason.

Recency bias

We give too much weight to what's just happened. After a few strong years, people assume shares will keep rising and take more risk; after a crash, they assume it'll keep falling and sell. That's recency bias.

In reality, recent returns tell you little about the next few years. Big falls have often been followed by strong recoveries, and long booms have ended suddenly.

The guard: look at long-run history, not last year. Set your mix of shares, bonds and cash from your goals and timeframe (module 30), not from recent headlines.

The sunk cost fallacy

A sunk cost is money already spent that you can't get back. The sunk cost fallacy is letting it drive future decisions: "I've put £2,000 into this share, I can't sell now." Or staying in a course or job you hate because of the time already invested.

The amount you've already spent doesn't change what's best from here. The only question that matters is: if I had this money in cash today, would I buy this investment? If not, why hold it?

Other traps worth knowing

  • Anchoring: fixing on one number, like the price you paid or a share's old high, as if it mattered to the future.
  • Confirmation bias: seeking out news that agrees with what you already believe, and ignoring the rest.
  • Mental accounting: treating money differently depending on where it came from, like spending a tax refund freely while paying 30% on a credit card.

Building guardrails

You can't switch biases off. You can design around them:

  1. Automate: a monthly direct debit into a fund means you keep buying through the ups and downs without deciding each time.
  2. Write an investment plan while calm: goals, mix, what you'll do in a crash. Read it before any big decision.
  3. Look less often. Checking daily invites emotional decisions.
  4. Add friction: wait 48 hours before any unplanned trade.
  5. Keep it simple: a few broad funds give biases less to work with than dozens of individual shares.

The common mistake

"I know about these biases now, so they won't affect me."

Knowing about biases helps a little, but research suggests people are much better at spotting them in others than in themselves. That's why the guard against bias is a system, such as automation, a written plan and fewer checks, rather than willpower in the moment.

In real life

  • Automate your investing and review it on a schedule (say, once a year), not when the news is loud.
  • Before selling in a fall, read your plan. Ask what you'd do with the money in cash today.
  • Be extra careful with hype: social media tips, "can't lose" stories, friends' big wins.
  • Red flag: pressure to decide fast ("offer ends today"), or someone using your fear of missing out. That's a sales tactic, and common in scams.

Think about it

Why might checking your investments every day make you a worse investor?

Over a single day, a share fund falls almost as often as it rises, so you'd see lots of losses. With loss aversion, each feels twice as bad as a gain feels good, so frequent checking makes investing feel worse than it is and tempts you to act. Checking once a year shows mostly the long-run upward trend.

How could herding make markets less efficient for a while?

If many investors buy just because others are, prices can rise well above what profits justify, and fall well below in panics. Eventually fundamentals usually reassert themselves, but "eventually" can be years, which is why timing bubbles is so hard.

Try it

  1. Look back at a decision you made with money (or anything) where you stuck with something because of what you'd already put in. Was it a sunk cost?
  2. Write three lines for your future self: "If my investments fall by a third, I will…". Keep it with your plan from module 29.
